The Geometric Wall: When Flat Boundaries Fail

In our analysis of standard $K$-means, we uncovered a geometric truth: the decision boundary separating any two clusters is the perpendicular bisector hyperplane between their centroids. Consequently, every cluster produced by Lloyd's algorithm is a convex Voronoi cell.

While convex cells work remarkably well for spherical blobs, they fail catastrophically on non-linear geometries. Consider two concentric rings: the true clusters are the inner manifold and the enclosing outer manifold. Because the outer ring completely encloses the inner ring, no single straight hyperplane can ever isolate them. When linear $K$-means ($K=2$) runs on this data, its centroids $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ settle on opposite sides of the origin, and the linear Voronoi boundary slices both rings in half like a pie.

How do we break past the tyranny of flat hyperplanes? In Representation Learning, when linear PCA failed on curved manifolds, we mapped our data into a high-dimensional feature space $\phi(x)$ where non-linear curves straightened out into linear subspaces, unlocking Kernel PCA.

Can we do the exact same thing for $K$-means? What does Kernel $K$-Means look like, and what surprising connection does it forge with Spectral Clustering?

Formulating Kernel $K$-Means

Let $\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathcal{H}$ be a non-linear feature mapping that lifts data points into a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) $\mathcal{H}$.

In this lifted feature space, the $K$-means objective seeks cluster assignments $z_1, \dots, z_n \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ that minimize the sum of squared distances to feature-space centroids:

$$\min_{z_1, \dots, z_n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|\phi(x_i) - \mu_{z_i}\|^2 \quad \text{where} \quad \mu_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i) \mathbf{1}(z_i = k)}{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}(z_i = k)}$$

The Immediate Obstacle: The feature space $\mathcal{H}$ can be infinitely dimensional (for instance, under a Gaussian RBF kernel). We cannot explicitly compute or store $\phi(x_i)$, let alone calculate coordinates for the centroid $\mu_k$.

The Central Question

Can we rewrite the entire feature-space distortion objective strictly in terms of pairwise inner products (kernel evaluations) $K_{ij} = \langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle$?

The Matrix Architecture: Building $Z$, $L$, and $M$

To translate the summation into clean linear algebra, let us stack our lifted data vectors as columns of an operator matrix $\Phi$:

$$\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & & | \\ \phi(x_1) & \phi(x_2) & \dots & \phi(x_n) \\ | & | & & | \end{bmatrix}$$

Next, we define the corresponding matrix of assigned cluster means $M$:

$$M = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & & | \\ \mu_{z_1} & \mu_{z_2} & \dots & \mu_{z_n} \\ | & | & & | \end{bmatrix}$$

The total distortion objective is simply the squared Frobenius norm of the residual matrix:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \|\phi(x_i) - \mu_{z_i}\|^2 = \|\Phi - M\|_F^2$$

How can we express $M$ in terms of $\Phi$? Let us inspect how the cluster assignments behave.

1. The Assignment Matrix $Z \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times K}$

Each row $i$ of $Z$ contains a 1-of-$K$ one-hot encoding indicating the cluster assignment of $x_i$:

$$Z_{ik} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if point } x_i \in \text{cluster } k \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

2. The Inverse Cluster-Size Matrix $L \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$

Let $n_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}(z_i = k)$ denote the total number of points in cluster $k$. We define $L$ as the diagonal matrix of inverse cluster counts:

$$L = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{n_1} & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \frac{1}{n_K} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$$
The Fundamental Matrix Identity of Cluster Counts

Observe what happens when we compute the inner product $Z^T Z$:

$$Z^T Z = \begin{bmatrix} n_1 & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & n_K \end{bmatrix} = L^{-1}$$

Therefore, $Z^T Z L = I_K$ and $L^{1/2} Z^T Z L^{1/2} = I_K$.

3. The Mean Reconstruction Matrix $A$

When we multiply $\Phi$ by $Z$, column $k$ of the product $\Phi Z$ computes the unnormalized sum of all feature vectors in cluster $k$: $\sum_{i \in C_k} \phi(x_i)$.

Multiplying by $L$ divides each column by its cluster size $n_k$, yielding the exact cluster centroids $\mu_k$:

$$\Phi Z L = \begin{bmatrix} | & & | \\ \mu_1 & \dots & \mu_K \\ | & & | \end{bmatrix}$$

Finally, multiplying by $Z^T$ broadcasts each centroid $\mu_k$ back to all data points belonging to cluster $k$:

The Centroid Projection Matrix
$$M = \Phi \underbrace{Z L Z^T}_{A} = \Phi A$$

where $A = Z L Z^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is an $n \times n$ symmetric block-averaging matrix with entries $A_{ij} = \frac{1}{n_k}$ if $i, j \in C_k$, and $0$ otherwise.

The Trace Derivation: Unlocking the Kernel Gram Matrix

Now let us substitute $M = \Phi Z L Z^T$ directly into our squared Frobenius distortion objective:

$$\|\Phi - M\|_F^2 = \|\Phi - \Phi Z L Z^T\|_F^2$$

Using the standard trace identity for Frobenius norms $\|B\|_F^2 = \text{tr}(B^T B)$:

$$\|\Phi - M\|_F^2 = \text{tr}\left[ (\Phi - \Phi Z L Z^T)^T (\Phi - \Phi Z L Z^T) \right]$$

Expanding the product gives four terms:

$$= \text{tr}\left[ \Phi^T \Phi - \Phi^T \Phi Z L Z^T - Z L Z^T \Phi^T \Phi + Z L Z^T \Phi^T \Phi Z L Z^T \right]$$

Let $K = \Phi^T \Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ denote the Kernel (Gram) Matrix, where each entry is the scalar dot product $K_{ij} = \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j) = k(x_i, x_j)$.

$$= \text{tr}\left[ K - K Z L Z^T - Z L Z^T K + Z L Z^T K Z L Z^T \right]$$

Now apply the trace linearity $\text{tr}(A + B) = \text{tr}(A) + \text{tr}(B)$ and the cyclic permutation property of trace $\text{tr}(ABC) = \text{tr}(CAB) = \text{tr}(BCA)$:

  1. $\text{tr}(Z L Z^T K) = \text{tr}(K Z L Z^T)$ (by swapping the cyclic order).
  2. For the fourth term: $$\text{tr}(Z L Z^T K Z L Z^T) = \text{tr}(K \cdot \underbrace{Z L Z^T Z L Z^T}_{A^2})$$ Since $Z^T Z = L^{-1}$, the middle factors collapse: $$Z L (Z^T Z) L Z^T = Z L (L^{-1}) L Z^T = Z L Z^T$$ Therefore, $\text{tr}(Z L Z^T K Z L Z^T) = \text{tr}(K Z L Z^T)$!

Look at what happens when we substitute these back:

$$\|\Phi - M\|_F^2 = \text{tr}(K) - \text{tr}(K Z L Z^T) - \text{tr}(K Z L Z^T) + \text{tr}(K Z L Z^T)$$
The Kernel $K$-Means Trace Theorem
$$\sum_{i=1}^n \|\phi(x_i) - \mu_{z_i}\|^2 = \text{tr}(K) - \text{tr}(K Z L Z^T)$$

Because the total kernel trace $\text{tr}(K) = \sum_{i=1}^n k(x_i, x_i)$ is a fixed constant determined solely by the dataset, minimizing distortion is mathematically identical to maximizing the trace term:

$$\min_{Z} \|\Phi - M\|_F^2 \iff \max_{Z \in \{0,1\}^{n \times K}} \text{tr}(K Z L Z^T)$$

The Computational Wall & The Continuous Relaxation

Let us split the diagonal matrix $L$ as $L = L^{1/2} L^{1/2}$ and use the cyclic property of trace once more:

$$\text{tr}(K Z L Z^T) = \text{tr}\left( K (Z L^{1/2}) (L^{1/2} Z^T) \right) = \text{tr}\left( (Z L^{1/2})^T K (Z L^{1/2}) \right)$$

Let us define a transformed assignment matrix $H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$:

$$H = Z L^{1/2}$$

Let us check the column inner products of $H$:

$$H^T H = (Z L^{1/2})^T (Z L^{1/2}) = L^{1/2} (Z^T Z) L^{1/2} = L^{1/2} L^{-1} L^{1/2} = I_K$$

The columns of $H$ are strictly orthonormal!

Our optimization problem is:

$$\max_{Z \in \{0,1\}^{n \times K}} \text{tr}\left( H^T K H \right) \quad \text{where } H = Z L^{1/2}$$

The Computational Roadblock: Because each element $Z_{ik} \in \{0, 1\}$ is a discrete binary choice, optimizing this directly over the combinatorial set of all partitions is NP-hard.

The Continuous Relaxation

Instead of restricting $H$ to have the rigid discrete structure $Z L^{1/2}$, what if we relax the problem and allow $H$ to be any real-valued continuous matrix in $\mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$ satisfying $H^T H = I_K$?

The Relaxed Continuous Problem
$$\max_{H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}} \text{tr}(H^T K H) \quad \text{subject to} \quad H^T H = I_K$$

By the Rayleigh-Ritz / Ky Fan Theorem, the exact global solution to this continuous optimization problem is achieved when the columns of $H^*$ are the top $K$ eigenvectors of the Kernel matrix $K$!

Mapping $H^*$ Back to $Z^*$: The Spectral Clustering Algorithm

We have an exact continuous solution $H^* = [h_1, h_2, \dots, h_K] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$, where $h_k$ are the top eigenvectors of $K$. But our end goal is to assign each data point to a discrete cluster.

How do we map continuous rows of $H^*$ back to discrete cluster assignments $Z$?

The Ideal Case Insight: Why Row Normalization Works

Suppose we were in the ideal world where the continuous optimum $H^*$ matched the true discrete clustering $Z L^{1/2}$. What would the rows of $H^*$ look like?

$$H^* = Z L^{1/2} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_2}} & 0 \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_1}} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_2}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_3}} \end{bmatrix}$$

In this ideal matrix:

  • Every data point belonging to cluster $k$ has non-zeros only in column $k$, with value $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_k}}$.
  • If we normalize each row of $H^*$ to have unit Euclidean norm ($\|H_{i,:}^*\| = 1$), every point in cluster $k$ becomes the pure unit standard basis vector $e_k = [0, \dots, 1, \dots, 0]$!
The Complete Spectral Clustering Algorithm (Ng-Jordan-Weiss)
  1. Given dataset $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ and kernel function $k(\cdot, \cdot)$, compute the $n \times n$ Kernel matrix $K$.
  2. Compute the top $K$ eigenvectors of $K$: $H^* = [h_1, h_2, \dots, h_K] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$.
  3. Normalize the rows of $H^*$ to unit length on the sphere $\mathbb{S}^{K-1}$: $$U_{ik} = \frac{H_{ik}^*}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^K (H_{ij}^*)^2}}$$
  4. Treat each row $U_{i,:} \in \mathbb{R}^K$ as a new $K$-dimensional representation of data point $x_i$, and run standard Lloyd's $K$-means algorithm on $\{U_{1,:}, \dots, U_{n,:}\}$.
  5. Assign point $x_i$ to cluster $k$ if row $U_{i,:}$ was assigned to cluster $k$.

The Grand Synthesis: Connecting the Dots

When students first encounter Spectral Clustering in textbooks, it is often introduced via graph cuts, normalized Laplacians ($L = D - W$), and graph degree matrices.

Our first-principles derivation reveals what Spectral Clustering truly is:

The Unifying Truth

Spectral Clustering is the continuous spectral relaxation of Kernel $K$-Means.

When you choose the Kernel matrix $K$ to be the pseudo-inverse or normalized adjacency matrix of a similarity graph, graph spectral partitioning and kernel $K$-means are mathematically identical.
