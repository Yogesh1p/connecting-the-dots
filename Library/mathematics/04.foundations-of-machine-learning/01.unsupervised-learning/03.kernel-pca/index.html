The Non-Linear Bottleneck

The second limitation of linear PCA is that it only finds flat subspaces (lines, planes, and hyperplanes).

The Circle Experiment

Consider a simple 2D dataset where all data points lie on the circumference of a circle with center $(a, b)$ and radius $R$:

$$(f_1 - a)^2 + (f_2 - b)^2 = R^2$$

Let us trace step-by-step what happens when standard linear PCA is applied to this dataset:

  1. Centering: Subtracting the mean shifts the origin to the center of the circle: $(a, b) \to (0, 0)$. The equation simplifies to $f_1^2 + f_2^2 = R^2$.
  2. Rotational Symmetry: Because the points are distributed uniformly around the perimeter, the spread along the $x$-axis is identical to the spread along the $y$-axis:
    $$\text{Var}(f_1) = \text{Var}(f_2) = \frac{R^2}{2}, \qquad \text{Cov}(f_1, f_2) = 0$$
  3. Eigendecomposition: The covariance matrix is a multiple of the identity:
    $$C = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \sigma^2$$
  4. Dimensionality Retention: Because $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$, each component explains exactly $50\%$ of the total variance. If we apply the standard rule of thumb to preserve $95\%$ of the variance, PCA forces us to retain both dimensions ($k = 2$).

Linear PCA concludes that the data is 2-dimensional and achieves no dimensionality reduction, even though the points lie on a 1-dimensional curve.

Linearizing Curves in Higher Dimensions

How can linear algebra handle curved data? The key idea:

We can linearize non-linear relationships by mapping the data into a higher-dimensional space.

Let us expand the circle equation algebraically:

$$f_1^2 + f_2^2 - 2a f_1 - 2b f_2 + (a^2 + b^2 - R^2) = 0$$

Now define a non-linear feature map $\phi(x)$ that lifts any 2D point $x = [f_1, f_2]^T$ into a 6-dimensional feature space $\mathbb{R}^6$:

$$\phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ f_1^2 \\ f_2^2 \\ f_1 f_2 \\ f_1 \\ f_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6$$

and define a constant parameter vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^6$ based on the circle's parameters:

$$u = \begin{bmatrix} a^2 + b^2 - R^2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ -2a \\ -2b \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6$$

Take the dot product between $\phi(x)$ and $u$:

$$\phi(x)^T u = (a^2 + b^2 - R^2)(1) + (1)f_1^2 + (1)f_2^2 + (0)f_1 f_2 - 2a f_1 - 2b f_2 = 0$$
The Geometric Insight of Non-Linear Lifting

In 2D, the circle is non-linear and curved. But in 6D, every mapped point $\phi(x)$ lies on a perfectly flat hyperplane orthogonal to $u$.

In this 6D space, the variance in the direction of $u$ is exactly zero. Standard linear PCA in the lifted space will immediately detect this flat linear subspace and compress the data cleanly!

Implicit Inner Products & Kernels

Mapping data into a higher-dimensional space makes curved relationships linear. However, explicitly computing and storing high-dimensional feature vectors is often computationally impractical.

If you have input vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and want to consider all polynomial interactions up to degree $p$, the dimension of the mapped feature vector $\phi(x)$ explodes combinatorially:

$$\dim(\phi(x)) = \binom{d + p}{p}$$

For a modest input of $d = 100$ features with degree $p = 5$, $\dim(\phi(x)) = \binom{105}{5} \approx 96.5\text{ million}$. And for richer, smooth mappings, the dimension can be literally infinite ($\infty$). You cannot compute, store, or manipulate vectors with 96 million components.

The Dual Bridge

Here is where our earlier work on the Dual Representation pays off. Look back at what we discovered when we solved PCA in the dual space:

  1. To find the principal components, we solve $K \vec{\alpha}_k = n \lambda_k \vec{\alpha}_k$. The matrix $K$ requires only the pairwise dot products between samples: $K_{ij} = \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j)$.
  2. To project a new query point $x$, we compute:
    $$y_k(x) = \phi(x)^T w_k = \phi(x)^T \left( \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{kj} \phi(x_j) \right) = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{kj} \left( \phi(x)^T \phi(x_j) \right)$$

Look at both equations carefully. The dual representation never needs the individual coordinates of $\phi(x)$! It only ever needs the inner product between pairs of vectors: $\phi(x)^T \phi(x')$.

This leads directly to the central question:

Can we compute the high-dimensional inner product $\phi(x)^T \phi(x')$ directly from our original low-dimensional inputs $x$ and $x'$, without ever calculating or storing the vectors $\phi(x)$?

Algebraic Proof: Evaluating 6D Inner Products in 2D

Let us test this idea on a concrete example. Take two 2D vectors $x = [f_1, f_2]^T$ and $x' = [g_1, g_2]^T$.

Instead of mapping $x$ and $x'$ into 6D, consider the following simple scalar operation computed purely using their 2D dot product:

$$f(x, x') = (x^T x' + 1)^2$$

Let us expand this scalar expression algebraically:

$$\begin{aligned} (x^T x' + 1)^2 &= (f_1 g_1 + f_2 g_2 + 1)^2 \\ &= (f_1 g_1)^2 + (f_2 g_2)^2 + 1^2 + 2(f_1 g_1)(f_2 g_2) + 2(f_1 g_1)(1) + 2(f_2 g_2)(1) \\ &= f_1^2 g_1^2 + f_2^2 g_2^2 + 1 + 2 f_1 f_2 g_1 g_2 + 2 f_1 g_1 + 2 f_2 g_2 \end{aligned}$$

Now consider the 6-dimensional feature vectors that represent all quadratic interactions:

$$\phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} f_1^2 \\ f_2^2 \\ 1 \\ \sqrt{2}f_1 f_2 \\ \sqrt{2}f_1 \\ \sqrt{2}f_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6, \qquad \phi(x') = \begin{bmatrix} g_1^2 \\ g_2^2 \\ 1 \\ \sqrt{2}g_1 g_2 \\ \sqrt{2}g_1 \\ \sqrt{2}g_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6$$

Take their direct Euclidean dot product $\phi(x)^T \phi(x')$:

$$\phi(x)^T \phi(x') = f_1^2 g_1^2 + f_2^2 g_2^2 + 1 + 2 f_1 f_2 g_1 g_2 + 2 f_1 g_1 + 2 f_2 g_2$$

The two expressions are identically equal! Computing $(x^T x' + 1)^2$ in 2D gives the exact inner product in $\mathbb{R}^6$, with zero high-dimensional overhead.

Naming the Mechanism: Kernel Functions & The Kernel Trick

We have just evaluated an inner product in a 6-dimensional feature space using nothing more than a simple scalar operation in 2-dimensional space.

Now that the purpose and mechanism are clear, we give it its formal name:

The Kernel Function & The Kernel Trick

A function $k(x, x')$ that computes the inner product between two points in some higher-dimensional feature space directly from their original coordinates—$k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$—is called a kernel function.

Performing linear algorithms in high-dimensional (or even infinite-dimensional) spaces purely through kernel evaluations without ever visiting those spaces explicitly is known as the Kernel Trick.

Common Kernel Functions

  • Polynomial Kernel: $k(x, x') = (x^T x' + c)^p$. Maps data into the space of all monomial combinations up to degree $p$.
  • Radial Basis Function (RBF) / Gaussian Kernel:
    $$k(x, x') = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - x'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

    The RBF kernel maps data into an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. To see why, recall the Taylor series of the exponential function:

    $$\exp\left(\frac{x^T x'}{\sigma^2}\right) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{1}{m!} \left(\frac{x^T x'}{\sigma^2}\right)^m$$

    The exponential contains polynomial terms of every integer degree from $0$ to $\infty$!

Mercer's Theorem: What Makes a Kernel Valid?

Can you pick any arbitrary mathematical function $k(x, x')$ and call it a kernel? No. Because $k(x, x')$ represents an inner product $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$ in some real Hilbert space, it must obey the geometric laws of inner products:

  1. Symmetry: $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle = \langle \phi(x'), \phi(x) \rangle \implies k(x, x') = k(x', x)$.
  2. Non-Negative Lengths (Positive Semidefiniteness): For any set of points $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ and any choice of real coefficients $c_1, \dots, c_n$, the squared length of the vector $v = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \phi(x_i)$ must be non-negative:
    $$\|v\|^2 = \left\langle \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \phi(x_i), \sum_{j=1}^n c_j \phi(x_j) \right\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n c_i c_j k(x_i, x_j) = c^T K c \geq 0$$

This connects directly back to our lesson on Positive Definite Matrices:

Mercer's Theorem

A continuous symmetric function $k(x, x')$ is a valid kernel corresponding to an inner product in some feature space if and only if the Gram matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ (with $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$) is positive semidefinite (PSD) for every possible finite dataset.

Centering in Feature Space

Standard PCA requires the data to be centered at the origin: $\sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i) = \mathbf{0}$. If the data is not centered, the first component simply points toward the center of mass rather than capturing the direction of maximum variance.

Recall that standard PCA strictly requires the data to be centered at the origin: $\sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i) = \mathbf{0}$. If the data is not centered, the first "principal component" simply points from the origin toward the center of mass rather than capturing the axes of variance.

Even if your raw input data $x_i$ is centered in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the mapped features $\phi(x_i)$ in Hilbert space are almost never centered (e.g., if $\bar{x} = 0$, $x^2$ is strictly positive, so the mean of $\phi(x)$ is non-zero).

The Dilemma: We cannot compute the mean vector $\bar{\phi} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i)$ directly because we cannot access the infinite-dimensional vectors $\phi(x_i)$. How do we center points that we cannot see?

Deriving the Centered Gram Matrix $K_c$

Let the true, unobserved centered feature vector for sample $i$ be:

$$\tilde{\phi}(x_i) = \phi(x_i) - \bar{\phi} = \phi(x_i) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r)$$

We want to compute the entry $(K_c)_{ij} = \tilde{\phi}(x_i)^T \tilde{\phi}(x_j)$ of the centered Gram matrix. Let us expand the inner product directly:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = \left( \phi(x_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r) \right)^T \left( \phi(x_j) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_s) \right)$$

Multiplying out the four terms:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = \underbrace{\phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j)}_{K_{ij}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_s)}_{\text{Row } i \text{ average}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r)^T \phi(x_j)}_{\text{Col } j \text{ average}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_r)^T \phi(x_s)}_{\text{Grand matrix average}}$$

Notice that every single term is an average of entries from our original, uncentered kernel matrix $K$:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = K_{ij} - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{s=1}^n K_{is} - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n K_{rj} + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n K_{rs}$$

The Centering Projector Matrix

We can write this entire operation in a single, beautiful matrix equation. Define the $n \times n$ centering projection matrix $H$:

$$H = I_n - \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}_n \mathbf{1}_n^T$$

where $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix and $\mathbf{1}_n$ is an $n \times 1$ column vector of all ones. Notice the geometric properties of $H$:

  • $H$ is symmetric: $H^T = H$.
  • $H$ is an orthogonal projector (idempotent): $H^2 = H$. It projects any vector onto the subspace orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}_n$, subtracting its mean.

Multiplying $K$ on both sides by $H$ gives:

The Centered Gram Matrix
$$K_c = H K H$$

Multiplying on the left subtracts column means; multiplying on the right subtracts row means; and their composition automatically adds back the grand mean. We have centered the infinite-dimensional feature space using only $n \times n$ matrix multiplications!

Kernel PCA & Synthesis

Putting all our pieces together, here is the complete, rigorous algorithm for non-linear dimensionality reduction:

The 5 Steps of Kernel PCA

  1. Compute the Kernel Matrix: Choose a valid Mercer kernel (such as Gaussian RBF) and compute the $n \times n$ Gram matrix:
    $$K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$$
  2. Center the Kernel Matrix: Apply the centering projection:
    $$K_c = H K H = K - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T K - K\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T + \frac{1}{n^2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T K \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$$
  3. Solve the Eigenvalue Problem: Compute the eigenvectors $\vec{\beta}_1, \dots, \vec{\beta}_k$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_1', \dots, \lambda_k'$ of $K_c$:
    $$K_c \vec{\beta}_k = \lambda_k' \vec{\beta}_k \qquad \text{with} \quad \|\vec{\beta}_k\| = 1$$
  4. Normalize Dual Coefficients: Scale each eigenvector to satisfy $\vec{\alpha}_k^T K_c \vec{\alpha}_k = 1$:
    $$\vec{\alpha}_k = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{\lambda_k'}}$$
  5. Project New Query Points: To extract the $k$-th non-linear coordinate $y_k(x^*)$ of any new data point $x^*$:
    $$y_k(x^*) = \tilde{\phi}(x^*)^T w_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} \tilde{k}_i(x^*)$$
    where $\tilde{k}_i(x^*)$ is the centered kernel vector between the test point and the training data:
    $$\tilde{k}_i(x^*) = k(x^*, x_i) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n k(x^*, x_j) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n K_{ri} + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n K_{rs}$$

Connecting the Dots: Summary of Representations

Let us step back and appreciate the unified progression we have constructed across this article:

Method Central Matrix Dimensions When to Use Key Insight
Primal Linear PCA $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ $d \times d$ $n \gg d$ (many samples, few features) Maximizing projection variance is identical to minimizing reconstruction error.
Dual Linear PCA $K = X^T X$ $n \times n$ $d \gg n$ (images, genomics, sensor arrays) Eigenvectors must live in the span of the data ($w = X\alpha$), collapsing $O(d^3) \to O(n^3)$.
Kernel PCA $K_c = H K H$ $n \times n$ Non-linear, curved manifolds (rings, Swiss rolls) Lifting to high dimensions linearizes manifolds; kernel trick evaluates dot products in $O(d)$.

Every breakthrough in this lesson emerged from answering a single geometric or computational bottleneck. By connecting the Spectral Theorem, orthogonal projections, Gram matrices, and positive definiteness, linear algebra allows us to unravel non-linear structures in infinite dimensions using nothing more than simple $n \times n$ matrices.
