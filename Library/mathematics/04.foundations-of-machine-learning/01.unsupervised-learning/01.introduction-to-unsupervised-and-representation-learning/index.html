The Unsupervised Setting: What Does It Mean to Learn?

When we train a supervised model, every observation comes paired with an explicit label $y$ telling the algorithm exactly what to predict. The target output defines what is important and what can be ignored.

In unsupervised learning, all supervision is stripped away. We receive no labels, no target answers, and no external guidance. We are handed only a raw collection of observations:

$$\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}, \quad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$$

Each observation $x_i$ is a vector of $d$ numerical measurements. For example, in a medical study, an observation might be a vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ listing a person's height, weight, and age:

$$x_i = \begin{bmatrix} 180 \text{ cm} \\[4pt] 75 \text{ kg} \\[4pt] 37 \text{ yrs} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3$$

The goal of unsupervised learning is stated simply: given a set of unlabeled data points, "understand" something "useful" about them.

At first glance, this sounds tantalizingly vague. What does it actually mean to "understand" an unlabeled cloud of numbers? What makes a mathematical description "useful"?

Chaitin's Principle: "Comprehension is Compression"

The mathematician and algorithmic information theorist Gregory Chaitin formulated a profound answer to this question:

"Comprehension is compression."

— Gregory Chaitin

To truly understand a dataset means discovering underlying rules, constraints, and shared structure that allow you to describe the data using far fewer numbers than the raw catalog. If you merely memorize all $n \times d$ numbers in a table, you have understood nothing—you have only stored a look-up table.

Learning is therefore fundamentally about information redundancy reduction: stripping away repeated or unnecessary coordinates so that only the essential degrees of freedom remain. The core challenge of representation learning can be stated as a concrete computational problem:

Learning is the discovery of a compact, structured representation. The core problem of representation learning can therefore be framed as a concrete computational challenge:

$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Input:} &\quad \text{Dataset } \mathcal{D} = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}, \quad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d \\[6pt] \textbf{Output:} &\quad \text{A compressed representation of the dataset} \end{aligned}$$

Pop Quiz: Lossless Compression on a Line

Before dealing with high dimensions, let us ground this compression principle in a tangible 2D example. Suppose we observe $n = 4$ data points in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} -5 \\[3pt] 2.5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] x_3 &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\[3pt] -2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\[3pt] -2.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Question: How many numbers are needed to store this dataset?

Naively, each point requires $d = 2$ coordinates, so storing all 4 points directly requires:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Naive Storage} &= n \times d \\[6pt] &= 4 \times 2 \\[6pt] &= 8 \text{ numbers} \end{aligned}$$

Now look at the coordinates closely:

$$\frac{2.5}{-5} = \frac{1}{-2} = \frac{-2}{4} = \frac{-2.5}{5} = -0.5$$

Every single observation lies on the exact same straight line passing through the origin: $x_2 = -0.5 x_1$ (Figure 1, Panel A).

Because all 4 points share this geometric constraint, we do not need to store 2 arbitrary coordinates for each observation. Instead, we can choose a single representative direction vector along the line:

$$w = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^2$$

(Notice that $w$ can be any non-zero point along the line, such as a normalized unit vector). Once this single representative direction $w$ is fixed, every data point $x_i$ is completely determined by a single scalar coefficient $c_i$:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= 2.5 \cdot w = 2.5 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -5 \\[3pt] 2.5 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] x_2 &= 1.0 \cdot w = 1.0 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] x_3 &= -2.0 \cdot w = -2.0 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\[3pt] -2 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] x_4 &= -2.5 \cdot w = -2.5 \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\[3pt] -2.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Now tally the numbers needed to store this representation:

  • $1$ representative direction vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^2 \implies 2 \text{ numbers}$
  • $4$ scalar coefficients $\{c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4\} \implies 4 \text{ numbers}$
  • Total storage: $2 + 4 = 6 \text{ numbers}$ (compared to $8$ naively)

Using this compressed representation, we can exactly reconstruct the entire dataset without losing a single decimal point: $x_i = c_i w$. Because the data truly lived on a 1D line inside 2D space, discovering this geometric structure enabled exact, lossless compression.

The Breakdown of Lossless Compression

Now suppose our dataset acquires just one more observation—a point that does not fall on the line:

$$x_5 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\[3pt] 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

What happens if we demand exact reconstruction for arbitrary points in 2D space?

To represent any arbitrary 2D vector without error, a single line is no longer sufficient. We need 2 linearly independent basis vectors (e.g., standard basis vectors $e_1 = [1, 0]^T$ and $e_2 = [0, 1]^T$), plus 2 coordinates for every data point:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Storage for Exact Recovery} &= (\text{basis representatives}) + (\text{coefficients}) \\[6pt] &= (2 \times d) + (n \times d) \\[6pt] &= 4 + 2n \text{ numbers} \end{aligned}$$

Look at that tally: $4 + 2n > 2n$. In attempting exact reconstruction, we are storing more numbers than the raw dataset itself!

This exposes an inevitable reality: in real-world data, noise, measurement discrepancies, and complex factors guarantee that points never fall perfectly on an exact lower-dimensional line or hyperplane. If we demand lossless precision, dimensionality reduction is impossible.

If lossless compression fails on real data, what if we accept a small, controlled amount of error in exchange for massive compression? Let us try lossy compression!

Where Should the Proxy Be Chosen?

In lossy compression, we represent each observation $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ not by its exact coordinates, but by an approximate proxy $\hat{x}$ constrained to lie on a shared candidate line defined by direction vector $w = [w_1, w_2]^T$ (Figure 1, Panel B).

Any point on this line is simply a scalar multiple of $w$:

$$\hat{x} = C w = \begin{bmatrix} C w_1 \\[3pt] C w_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

From a compression point of view, every point on the line is equally cheap—storing any scalar $C$ costs exactly 1 number. But from a reconstruction point of view, they are not equal at all! We want the proxy $\hat{x}$ that sits as close to the true observation $x$ as possible.

The gap between the true observation $x = [x_1, x_2]^T$ and its proxy $\hat{x}$ is the error vector:

$$e = x - \hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 - C w_1 \\[3pt] x_2 - C w_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

To make the proxy as accurate as possible, we choose $C$ to minimize the squared length of this error vector:

$$\min_C \|x - C w\|^2 = \min_C \left[ (x_1 - C w_1)^2 + (x_2 - C w_2)^2 \right]$$

Let us differentiate this objective with respect to $C$ and set it to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dC} \|x - C w\|^2 &= \frac{d}{dC} \left[ (C w_1 - x_1)^2 + (C w_2 - x_2)^2 \right] \\[8pt] &= 2(C w_1 - x_1)w_1 + 2(C w_2 - x_2)w_2 \\[8pt] &= 2C(w_1^2 + w_2^2) - 2(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2) \end{aligned}$$

Setting the derivative equal to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} 2C(w_1^2 + w_2^2) - 2(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2) &= 0 \\[8pt] C(w_1^2 + w_2^2) &= w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 \\[8pt] C &= \frac{w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2}{w_1^2 + w_2^2} \\[8pt] &= \frac{w^T x}{\|w\|^2} \end{aligned}$$

Testing the Arithmetic: Scale Invariance

Let us test this formula on our off-line observation $x = [2, 5]^T$:

Suppose we pick the candidate direction $w = [1, 1]^T$. Evaluating the formula:

$$\begin{aligned} C &= \frac{w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2}{w_1^2 + w_2^2} = \frac{1(2) + 1(5)}{1^2 + 1^2} = \frac{7}{2} = 3.5 \\[8pt] \text{Proxy } \hat{x} &= C w = 3.5 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\[3pt] 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.5 \\[3pt] 3.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Now, what if someone else chose a representative vector pointing in the same direction, but 10 times longer: $w' = [10, 10]^T$?

$$\begin{aligned} C' &= \frac{10(2) + 10(5)}{10^2 + 10^2} = \frac{70}{200} = 0.35 \\[8pt] \text{Proxy } \hat{x}' &= C' w' = 0.35 \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\[3pt] 10 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.5 \\[3pt] 3.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Notice this fundamental property: the scalar coefficient $C$ changes inversely with the length of $w$, but the physical position of the proxy $\hat{x}$ does not change at all.

To eliminate this scaling ambiguity, we can always standardize our representative $w$ to be a unit vector:

$$\|w\|^2 = w_1^2 + w_2^2 = w^T w = 1$$

For any unit representative $w$, the denominator vanishes completely:

$$\begin{aligned} C &= w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 = w^T x \\[6pt] \text{Proxy } \hat{x} &= (w^T x)w \end{aligned}$$

This proves that the optimal proxy under squared reconstruction error is precisely the orthogonal projection of $x$ onto direction $w$!

Compression Economics: Scaling to High Dimensions

What happens when our data does not live in 2D, but in hundreds or thousands of dimensions ($d \gg 2$)?

If the data points approximately cluster near a $K$-dimensional subspace (where $K \ll d$), we do not need to store all $d$ coordinates for each sample. Instead, we can approximate each observation by a linear combination of $K$ shared basis vectors $\{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_K\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$x_i \approx \sum_{k=1}^K (w_k^T x_i)w_k$$

Let us evaluate the storage economics:

  • Uncompressed Storage: Storing $n$ observations with $d$ features requires $n \times d$ floating-point numbers.
  • Compressed Storage: Storing the $K$ shared basis vectors $\{w_1, \dots, w_K\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ requires $K \times d$ numbers. Storing the $K$ scalar coordinates $[w_1^T x_i, \dots, w_K^T x_i]^T$ for all $n$ points requires $n \times K$ numbers.

Consider a typical dataset with $n = 10{,}000$ samples and $d = 1{,}000$ feature measurements, compressed down to $K = 10$ components:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Without Compression} &= n \times d \\[6pt] &= 10{,}000 \times 1{,}000 \\[6pt] &= 10{,}000{,}000 \text{ numbers } (10^7, \text{or 1 crore}) \\[10pt] \text{With Compression} &= (n \times K) + (K \times d) \\[6pt] &= (10{,}000 \times 10) + (10 \times 1{,}000) \\[6pt] &= 100{,}000 + 10{,}000 \\[6pt] &= 110{,}000 \text{ numbers} \\[10pt] \text{Compression Ratio} &= \frac{10{,}000{,}000}{110{,}000} \approx 90.9\times \end{aligned}$$

This is an astronomical reduction: $10$ million numbers collapse down to $110{,}000$ numbers, delivering over a $90\times$ compression ratio while capturing the essential geometry of the data.

The Pivotal Mystery: Who Gives Us the Line?

We have established that if a candidate line $w$ is given, the optimal proxy for any data point is its orthogonal projection $(w^T x)w$.

This brings us directly to the foundational question that drives dimensionality reduction:

Given a line and a point, we now know how to calculate its optimal proxy. But who gives us the line? How do we find the single best direction $w$ through an entire cloud of data points?

Answering this question requires formulating an objective over the entire dataset, shifting the origin to the data's center of mass, and solving a constrained optimization problem that inevitably reveals the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. This discovery is the heart of Principal Component Analysis (PCA).
