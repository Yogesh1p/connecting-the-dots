True mastery of mathematical machine learning does not come from memorizing algorithmic steps—it comes from feeling the gears turn when algebra, geometry, and optimization collide.

In this practice laboratory, we pressure-test our understanding of representation learning across the classical theoretical challenges posed in Christopher Bishop's seminal text, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (PRML, Chapter 12).

Each problem is presented as a complete, guided walkthrough: first framing the core question and its physical intuition, and then unfolding the exact, step-by-step derivation without skipped algebra.

Category 1: Maximum Variance & Orthogonality

In standard PCA, we seek directions in feature space that preserve the greatest spread of data. How does constrained calculus inevitably produce the eigenvalue equation, and why are subsequent principal axes guaranteed to decouple?

Problem 1: The Stationarity Condition & Maximum Projected Variance

PRML Exercise 12.1
Problem Statement

Consider a dataset of $N$ observations $\{x_1, \dots, x_N\} \subset \mathbb{R}^D$ with sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N x_n$ and sample covariance matrix:

$$S = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N (x_n - \bar{x})(x_n - \bar{x})^T$$

We wish to project each centered point onto a single unit direction $u_1 \in \mathbb{R}^D$ with $u_1^T u_1 = 1$. Prove that the direction maximizing the projected variance is an eigenvector of $S$ corresponding to the largest eigenvalue.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Objective Formulation & Constrained Lagrangian

The scalar coordinate of observation $x_n$ along direction $u_1$ is $u_1^T x_n$. The sample variance of these projected coordinates is:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \left( u_1^T x_n - u_1^T \bar{x} \right)^2 &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \left( u_1^T (x_n - \bar{x}) \right)^2 \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N u_1^T (x_n - \bar{x})(x_n - \bar{x})^T u_1 \\[6pt] &= u_1^T \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N (x_n - \bar{x})(x_n - \bar{x})^T \right) u_1 \\[6pt] &= u_1^T S u_1 \end{aligned}$$

Without constraint, $u_1^T S u_1$ can be made arbitrarily large simply by scaling $\|u_1\| \to \infty$. To find a pure direction, we impose the normalization constraint $u_1^T u_1 = 1$ using a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_1$:

$$\mathcal{L}(u_1, \lambda_1) = u_1^T S u_1 - \lambda_1 (u_1^T u_1 - 1)$$

2. Stationarity Condition

Taking the vector gradient with respect to $u_1$ and setting it to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_{u_1} \mathcal{L} &= \nabla_{u_1} \left( u_1^T S u_1 \right) - \lambda_1 \nabla_{u_1} \left( u_1^T u_1 \right) \\[6pt] &= 2 S u_1 - 2 \lambda_1 u_1 \\[6pt] &= \mathbf{0} \end{aligned}$$

Dividing by $2$ reveals the fundamental eigenvalue equation of linear algebra:

$$S u_1 = \lambda_1 u_1$$

Hence, every stationary point of the variance objective is an eigenvector of the covariance matrix $S$, and the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_1$ is its associated eigenvalue.

3. Global Maximization

To determine which eigenvector maximizes the variance, multiply the eigenvalue equation on the left by $u_1^T$:

$$\begin{aligned} u_1^T S u_1 &= u_1^T (\lambda_1 u_1) \\[6pt] &= \lambda_1 (u_1^T u_1) \\[6pt] &= \lambda_1 \end{aligned}$$

The projected variance along direction $u_1$ is identically equal to the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. Therefore, to maximize variance, we must choose the eigenvector corresponding to the maximum eigenvalue:

$$\lambda_1 = \lambda_{\max}(S)$$
Key Takeaway: Projected variance is the Rayleigh quotient $u^T S u / u^T u$. Constrained optimization does not merely find a heuristic direction—it mathematically isolates the principal eigenvector of the covariance matrix.

Problem 2: Successive Orthogonal Subspaces via Lagrange Multipliers

PRML Exercise 12.2
Problem Statement

Once the first principal component $u_1$ is chosen, we seek a second unit direction $u_2$ ($u_2^T u_2 = 1$) that maximizes projected variance $u_2^T S u_2$ while being strictly orthogonal to the first ($u_2^T u_1 = 0$). Prove that $u_2$ is an eigenvector of $S$ corresponding to the second-largest eigenvalue.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Two-Constraint Lagrangian

We construct the Lagrangian incorporating both the unit-norm constraint ($u_2^T u_2 = 1$) and the mutual orthogonality constraint ($u_2^T u_1 = 0$):

$$\mathcal{L}(u_2, \lambda_2, \eta) = u_2^T S u_2 - \lambda_2 (u_2^T u_2 - 1) - 2\eta (u_2^T u_1)$$

where $\lambda_2$ and $\eta$ are scalar Lagrange multipliers (the factor of $2$ is introduced for algebraic convenience).

2. Stationarity Condition

Differentiating with respect to $u_2$ and equating to the zero vector:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_{u_2} \mathcal{L} &= 2 S u_2 - 2 \lambda_2 u_2 - 2 \eta u_1 \\[6pt] &= \mathbf{0} \end{aligned}$$

Dividing by $2$:

$$S u_2 = \lambda_2 u_2 + \eta u_1$$

3. Proving the Cross-Multiplier Vanishes ($\eta = 0$)

To determine the value of $\eta$, multiply this equation on the left by $u_1^T$:

$$\begin{aligned} u_1^T S u_2 &= \lambda_2 (u_1^T u_2) + \eta (u_1^T u_1) \\[6pt] &= \lambda_2 (0) + \eta (1) \\[6pt] &= \eta \end{aligned}$$

Now consider the left-hand side $u_1^T S u_2$. Because the sample covariance matrix $S$ is symmetric ($S = S^T$), and because $u_1$ is an eigenvector of $S$ with $S u_1 = \lambda_1 u_1$:

$$\begin{aligned} u_1^T S u_2 &= (S^T u_1)^T u_2 \\[6pt] &= (S u_1)^T u_2 \\[6pt] &= (\lambda_1 u_1)^T u_2 \\[6pt] &= \lambda_1 (u_1^T u_2) \\[6pt] &= \lambda_1 (0) \\[6pt] &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Comparing both expressions reveals that the cross-constraint multiplier is identically zero:

$$\eta = 0$$

Substituting $\eta = 0$ back into our stationarity condition reduces it directly to:

$$S u_2 = \lambda_2 u_2$$

Thus, $u_2$ is also an eigenvector of $S$. Because $u_2^T S u_2 = \lambda_2$, and the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ was already captured by $u_1$, maximizing variance subject to orthogonality selects the eigenvector associated with the second-largest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$.

Key Takeaway: The symmetry of the covariance matrix ($S = S^T$) is what decouples the Lagrangian constraints ($\eta = 0$). Orthogonality in data space automatically preserves orthogonality in covariance space ($u_1^T S u_2 = 0$).

Category 2: Minimum Reconstruction Error & Duality

An alternative view of PCA asks not about maximizing variance, but about minimizing the information lost during compression. Bishop formalizes this by projecting onto a general affine subspace.

Problem 3: Optimal Scalar Coordinates & Centering Bias

PRML Exercise 12.3
Problem Statement

Let $\{u_1, \dots, u_D\}$ be an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^D$ satisfying $u_i^T u_j = \delta_{ij}$. We approximate each data point $x_n \in \mathbb{R}^D$ using an $M$-dimensional linear subspace ($M < D$) with an affine bias:

$$\tilde{x}_n = \sum_{i=1}^M z_{ni} u_i + \sum_{i=M+1}^D b_i u_i$$

where $z_{ni}$ are sample-specific coordinates and $b_i$ are constant bias parameters shared across all data points. Show that minimizing the distortion measure:

$$J = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \|x_n - \tilde{x}_n\|^2$$

leads to the optimal choices $z_{nj} = x_n^T u_j$ for $j \in \{1, \dots, M\}$ and $b_j = \bar{x}^T u_j$ for $j \in \{M+1, \dots, D\}$.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Basis Expansion of the Original Observation

Because $\{u_1, \dots, u_D\}$ forms a complete orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^D$, any vector $x_n$ can be expanded exactly without approximation:

$$x_n = \sum_{i=1}^D (x_n^T u_i) u_i = \sum_{i=1}^M (x_n^T u_i) u_i + \sum_{i=M+1}^D (x_n^T u_i) u_i$$

2. Expressing the Error Vector

Subtracting the approximation $\tilde{x}_n$ from $x_n$:

$$\begin{aligned} x_n - \tilde{x}_n &= \left( \sum_{i=1}^M (x_n^T u_i) u_i + \sum_{i=M+1}^D (x_n^T u_i) u_i \right) - \left( \sum_{i=1}^M z_{ni} u_i + \sum_{i=M+1}^D b_i u_i \right) \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^M (x_n^T u_i - z_{ni}) u_i + \sum_{i=M+1}^D (x_n^T u_i - b_i) u_i \end{aligned}$$

Because the vectors $u_i$ are mutually orthonormal ($u_i^T u_j = \delta_{ij}$), all cross terms vanish when computing the squared Euclidean norm:

$$\|x_n - \tilde{x}_n\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^M (x_n^T u_i - z_{ni})^2 + \sum_{i=M+1}^D (x_n^T u_i - b_i)^2$$

Summing across all $N$ data points gives the total distortion:

$$J = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{i=1}^M (x_n^T u_i - z_{ni})^2 + \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{i=M+1}^D (x_n^T u_i - b_i)^2$$

3. Solving for the Optimal Coordinates $z_{nj}$

Differentiating $J$ with respect to a specific coordinate $z_{nj}$ (for $j \le M$):

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_{nj}} = -\frac{2}{N} (x_n^T u_j - z_{nj}) = 0$$

Solving immediately yields:

$$z_{nj} = x_n^T u_j \qquad (j = 1, \dots, M)$$

The optimal coordinate along any preserved basis vector is simply the orthogonal projection of the data point onto that vector.

4. Solving for the Optimal Bias $b_j$

Differentiating $J$ with respect to a specific bias parameter $b_j$ (for $j > M$):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial J}{\partial b_j} &= -\frac{2}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N (x_n^T u_j - b_j) \\[6pt] &= -\frac{2}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N x_n^T u_j + \frac{2}{N} (N b_j) \\[6pt] &= -2 \left( \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N x_n \right)^T u_j + 2 b_j \\[6pt] &= -2 (\bar{x}^T u_j) + 2 b_j \\[6pt] &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Solving for $b_j$:

$$b_j = \bar{x}^T u_j \qquad (j = M+1, \dots, D)$$

The optimal bias coordinate along discarded dimensions is the projection of the sample mean vector $\bar{x}$.

Key Takeaway: We did not assume orthogonal projection or mean centering. Both emerged mathematically as the unique global minimizers of the squared reconstruction error.

Problem 4: Expressing Reconstruction Error as Residual Covariance

PRML Exercise 12.4
Problem Statement

Substitute the optimal values $z_{nj} = x_n^T u_j$ and $b_j = \bar{x}^T u_j$ back into the distortion objective $J$. Prove that the total reconstruction error reduces to:

$$J = \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T S u_i$$

where $S$ is the sample covariance matrix of the dataset.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Substitution into the Error Difference

Substitute $z_{ni} = x_n^T u_i$ and $b_i = \bar{x}^T u_i$ into our expansion of the difference vector $x_n - \tilde{x}_n$:

$$\begin{aligned} x_n - \tilde{x}_n &= \sum_{i=1}^M \underbrace{(x_n^T u_i - x_n^T u_i)}_{= 0} u_i + \sum_{i=M+1}^D (x_n^T u_i - \bar{x}^T u_i) u_i \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=M+1}^D ((x_n - \bar{x})^T u_i) u_i \end{aligned}$$

The error lies entirely within the discarded subspace spanned by $\{u_{M+1}, \dots, u_D\}$, measuring how far the centered observation $x_n - \bar{x}$ extends outside the $M$-dimensional hyperplane.

2. Algebraic Expansion into Covariance Forms

Taking the squared norm of $x_n - \tilde{x}_n$ (using orthonormality $u_i^T u_j = \delta_{ij}$):

$$\begin{aligned} \|x_n - \tilde{x}_n\|^2 &= \sum_{i=M+1}^D \left( (x_n - \bar{x})^T u_i \right)^2 \\[6pt] &= \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T (x_n - \bar{x})(x_n - \bar{x})^T u_i \end{aligned}$$

Summing over all $N$ data points and dividing by $N$:

$$\begin{aligned} J &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T (x_n - \bar{x})(x_n - \bar{x})^T u_i \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N (x_n - \bar{x})(x_n - \bar{x})^T \right) u_i \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T S u_i \end{aligned}$$
Key Takeaway: Total reconstruction error is the sum of the sample variances along the discarded basis directions.

Problem 5: Subspace Selection & Eigenvalue Minimization

PRML Exercise 12.5
Problem Statement

Minimize the total reconstruction error $J = \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T S u_i$ subject to the orthonormal constraint $u_i^T u_j = \delta_{ij}$. Prove that the optimal basis vectors $\{u_1, \dots, u_M\}$ span the subspace corresponding to the $M$ largest eigenvalues of $S$.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Constrained Minimization of the Discarded Variance

To minimize $J = \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T S u_i$ subject to $u_i^T u_i = 1$ and mutual orthogonality, we formulate the Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{i=M+1}^D u_i^T S u_i - \sum_{i=M+1}^D \lambda_i (u_i^T u_i - 1) - \sum_{i=M+1}^D \sum_{j \ne i} \eta_{ij} u_i^T u_j$$

As proven in Problem 2, the symmetry of $S$ ensures that all cross-multipliers $\eta_{ij}$ vanish at the optimum. Taking gradients with respect to each discarded vector $u_i$ ($i > M$):

$$\nabla_{u_i} \mathcal{L} = 2 S u_i - 2 \lambda_i u_i = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S u_i = \lambda_i u_i$$

Therefore, each discarded direction $u_i$ must be an eigenvector of $S$ with variance $u_i^T S u_i = \lambda_i$. The minimum reconstruction error is:

$$J = \sum_{i=M+1}^D \lambda_i$$

2. Total Variance Conservation & The Duality Equivalence

Because $\{u_1, \dots, u_D\}$ forms a complete orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^D$, the sum of all $D$ eigenvalues equals the trace of the covariance matrix:

$$\operatorname{Tr}(S) = \sum_{i=1}^D \lambda_i = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^M \lambda_i}_{\text{Preserved Variance}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=M+1}^D \lambda_i}_{\text{Reconstruction Error } J}$$

Because $\operatorname{Tr}(S)$ is a fixed constant determined entirely by the dataset:

$$\min \sum_{i=M+1}^D \lambda_i \quad\iff\quad \max \sum_{i=1}^M \lambda_i$$

To minimize the discarded sum $\sum_{i=M+1}^D \lambda_i$, we must select the $D - M$ smallest eigenvalues of $S$. Consequently, the $M$ vectors $\{u_1, \dots, u_M\}$ spanning our preserved representation must correspond to the $M$ largest eigenvalues.

Key Takeaway: The maximum variance formulation (Category 1) and minimum error formulation (Category 2) are two perspectives of the same Pythagorean truth: total variance is constant, so minimizing error and maximizing preserved variance are mathematically identical.

Category 3: High-Dimensional Dual PCA ($D \gg N$)

When the dimensionality of feature space dwarfs the number of collected samples (such as $D = 100{,}000$ pixels across $N = 500$ images), standard PCA becomes computationally intractable. Bishop Exercise 12.6 derives the exact dual inversion that makes high-dimensional PCA possible.

Problem 6: The Sample Gram Inversion & Dual Normalization

PRML Exercise 12.6
Problem Statement

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ be the centered data matrix whose $n$-th row is $(x_n - \bar{x})^T$. The $D \times D$ sample covariance matrix is $S = \frac{1}{N} X^T X$. When $D \gg N$, finding the eigenvectors of $S$ directly is prohibitive ($\mathcal{O}(D^3)$).

Consider instead the $N \times N$ sample Gram matrix:

$$K = \frac{1}{N} X X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$$

Prove that if $v_i \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is an eigenvector of $K$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i > 0$, then:

$$u_i = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N \lambda_i}} X^T v_i$$

is a normalized unit eigenvector of $S$ with the exact same eigenvalue $\lambda_i$.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Propagating the Eigendecomposition across Matrix Multiplications

Let $v_i$ be an eigenvector of the $N \times N$ matrix $\frac{1}{N} X X^T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$:

$$\left( \frac{1}{N} X X^T \right) v_i = \lambda_i v_i$$

Multiply both sides of this equation on the left by the transpose data matrix $X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times N}$:

$$X^T \left( \frac{1}{N} X X^T v_i \right) = X^T (\lambda_i v_i)$$

Using matrix associativity, regroup the product on the left-hand side:

$$\begin{aligned} \left( \frac{1}{N} X^T X \right) (X^T v_i) &= \lambda_i (X^T v_i) \\[8pt] S (X^T v_i) &= \lambda_i (X^T v_i) \end{aligned}$$

This proves that the $D$-dimensional vector $X^T v_i$ is an eigenvector of $S$ with the exact same eigenvalue $\lambda_i$!

2. Computing the Normalization Constant

While $X^T v_i$ points in the correct direction, eigenvectors in feature space must have unit length ($\|u_i\| = 1$). Let $u_i = \alpha_i X^T v_i$. We compute the squared norm of $X^T v_i$:

$$\begin{aligned} \|X^T v_i\|^2 &= (X^T v_i)^T (X^T v_i) \\[6pt] &= v_i^T (X X^T) v_i \\[6pt] &= v_i^T \left( N \cdot \frac{1}{N} X X^T v_i \right) \\[6pt] &= v_i^T (N \lambda_i v_i) \\[6pt] &= N \lambda_i (v_i^T v_i) \end{aligned}$$

Since $v_i$ is a normalized eigenvector of $K$ ($v_i^T v_i = 1$):

$$\|X^T v_i\|^2 = N \lambda_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \|X^T v_i\| = \sqrt{N \lambda_i}$$

Dividing by this magnitude yields the unit-length principal component:

$$u_i = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N \lambda_i}} X^T v_i$$
Key Takeaway: $N$ data points in $\mathbb{R}^{D}$ span at most an $(N-1)$-dimensional affine hyperplane. The remaining $D - N$ orthogonal dimensions contain zero variance. By solving the $N \times N$ Gram problem, we reduce computational complexity from $\mathcal{O}(D^3)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$.

Category 4: Non-Linear Manifolds & Kernel PCA

When data lies on non-linear manifolds, linear hyperplanes fail. Kernel PCA maps inputs $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$ into a potentially infinite-dimensional Hilbert space $\mathcal{F}$ via $\Phi(x)$. But how do we center data points in a space whose coordinates we cannot evaluate?

Problem 7: Exact Centering of the Gram Matrix in Feature Space

PRML Exercise 12.16
Problem Statement

Let $\Phi(x)$ map inputs to feature space $\mathcal{F}$ with kernel $k(x, x') = \Phi(x)^T \Phi(x')$, and let $K \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ have elements $K_{nm} = k(x_n, x_m)$. Centered feature vectors are defined by:

$$\tilde{\Phi}(x_n) = \Phi(x_n) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{m=1}^N \Phi(x_m)$$

Prove that the centered Gram matrix $\tilde{K}_{nm} = \tilde{\Phi}(x_n)^T \tilde{\Phi}(x_m)$ can be computed strictly from the uncentered kernel matrix $K$ via:

$$\tilde{K} = K - \mathbf{1}_N K - K \mathbf{1}_N + \mathbf{1}_N K \mathbf{1}_N$$

where $\mathbf{1}_N \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is the matrix with all entries equal to $1/N$.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Entry-by-Entry Inner Product Expansion

We expand the inner product $\tilde{K}_{nm} = \tilde{\Phi}(x_n)^T \tilde{\Phi}(x_m)$ directly:

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{K}_{nm} &= \left( \Phi(x_n) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{l=1}^N \Phi(x_l) \right)^T \left( \Phi(x_m) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \Phi(x_j) \right) \\[8pt] &= \Phi(x_n)^T \Phi(x_m) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \Phi(x_n)^T \Phi(x_j) - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{l=1}^N \Phi(x_l)^T \Phi(x_m) + \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{l=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N \Phi(x_l)^T \Phi(x_j) \end{aligned}$$

Replacing every inner product $\Phi(a)^T \Phi(b)$ with its kernel evaluation $K_{ab} = k(x_a, x_b)$:

$$\tilde{K}_{nm} = K_{nm} - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N K_{nj} - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{l=1}^N K_{lm} + \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{l=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N K_{lj}$$

2. Matrix Form Representation

Examine the three correction sums in terms of the uniform matrix $\mathbf{1}_N$ where $(\mathbf{1}_N)_{ij} = \frac{1}{N}$:

  • The $n$-th row average: $$\frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N K_{nj} = (K \mathbf{1}_N)_{nm}$$
  • The $m$-th column average: $$\frac{1}{N} \sum_{l=1}^N K_{lm} = (\mathbf{1}_N K)_{nm}$$
  • The grand average of all matrix entries: $$\frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{l=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N K_{lj} = (\mathbf{1}_N K \mathbf{1}_N)_{nm}$$

Substituting these identifications yields the classic compact matrix equation:

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{K} &= K - \mathbf{1}_N K - K \mathbf{1}_N + \mathbf{1}_N K \mathbf{1}_N \\[6pt] &= (I_N - \mathbf{1}_N) K (I_N - \mathbf{1}_N) \end{aligned}$$
Key Takeaway: Centering in feature space requires zero explicit coordinate calculations. The projection matrix $H = I_N - \mathbf{1}_N$ centers the Gram matrix symmetrically from both sides.

Problem 8: Non-Linear Out-of-Sample Projections

PRML Exercise 12.17
Problem Statement

Let $v_k$ be the $k$-th eigenvector of the centered covariance operator in feature space. Prove that the projection of an arbitrary new test point $x$ onto $v_k$:

$$y_k(x) = v_k^T \tilde{\Phi}(x)$$

can be computed entirely using kernel evaluations between the test point and the training observations.

Solution Walkthrough

1. Representer Expansion of the Feature Eigenvector

By the Representer Theorem, any eigenvector $v_k$ in feature space must lie in the span of the centered training observations $\{\tilde{\Phi}(x_1), \dots, \tilde{\Phi}(x_N)\}$:

$$v_k = \sum_{n=1}^N a_{kn} \tilde{\Phi}(x_n)$$

where $a_k = [a_{k1}, \dots, a_{kN}]^T$ is the eigenvector of the centered Gram matrix $\tilde{K} a_k = \lambda_k a_k$, normalized such that $\lambda_k a_k^T a_k = 1$.

2. Evaluating the Test Projection

The non-linear projection of a test vector $x$ onto component $v_k$ is:

$$\begin{aligned} y_k(x) &= v_k^T \tilde{\Phi}(x) \\[6pt] &= \left( \sum_{n=1}^N a_{kn} \tilde{\Phi}(x_n) \right)^T \tilde{\Phi}(x) \\[6pt] &= \sum_{n=1}^N a_{kn} \left( \tilde{\Phi}(x_n)^T \tilde{\Phi}(x) \right) \end{aligned}$$

Now expand the centered inner product $\tilde{k}(x_n, x) = \tilde{\Phi}(x_n)^T \tilde{\Phi}(x)$ using the training mean:

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{k}(x_n, x) &= \left( \Phi(x_n) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^N \Phi(x_l) \right)^T \left( \Phi(x) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^N \Phi(x_m) \right) \\[8pt] &= k(x_n, x) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^N k(x_n, x_m) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^N k(x_l, x) + \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{l=1}^N \sum_{m=1}^N k(x_l, x_m) \end{aligned}$$

Substituting this back into our projection equation:

$$y_k(x) = \sum_{n=1}^N a_{kn} \tilde{k}(x_n, x)$$

Every single term in this equation involves only evaluations of the scalar kernel function $k(\cdot, \cdot)$ between the test point $x$ and the training set.

Key Takeaway: Even though the feature space $\mathcal{F}$ may be infinite-dimensional (as with the Gaussian RBF kernel), projecting a new test point onto a non-linear principal component requires nothing more than $N$ scalar kernel evaluations.

Connecting the Dots

Across these eight problems from Christopher Bishop's PRML, we have seen that every formulation of representation learning—whether framed through spread, loss, sample duality, or non-linear manifolds—converges onto the exact same spectral foundation:

The Unified Spectral Synthesis of Representation Learning
Perspective Mathematical Core Why It Works
Maximum Variance (PRML 12.1–12.2) $\max u^T S u \text{ s.t. } u^T u = 1$ Stationarity requires $S u = \lambda u$; variance equals the eigenvalue $\lambda$.
Minimum Distortion (PRML 12.3–12.5) $\min \sum \|x_n - \tilde{x}_n\|^2$ Pythagorean invariance ($\text{Tr}(S) = \text{const}$) forces min error to match max variance.
High-Dimensional Dual (PRML 12.6) $\frac{1}{N} X X^T v = \lambda v \implies u \propto X^T v$ $N$ samples span at most $N$ dimensions; solving $N \times N$ replaces $D \times D$.
Non-Linear Kernel PCA (PRML 12.16–12.17) $\tilde{K} = (I - \mathbf{1}) K (I - \mathbf{1})$ Implicit feature coordinates cancel out, allowing non-linear projections via inner products.

By mastering these exercises, we transition from observing machine learning as a collection of black-box algorithms to understanding it as an interconnected branch of applied linear algebra and geometry.
