Finding Order in High Dimensions

The Dimensionality Dilemma

In our introduction to representation learning, we discovered that once a candidate axis $w$ is chosen, the optimal proxy for any data point is its orthogonal projection $(w^T x)w$.

Yet that discovery left us facing a central mystery: who gives us the line? And why would high-dimensional data points lie anywhere near a lower-dimensional line or plane in the first place?

The answer connects directly back to why compression is possible at all: compression is simply the removal of redundant information. If every feature we measured varied completely independently of every other, our data points would fill the entire multi-dimensional space, and reducing dimensions would destroy irreplaceable information.

In real-world data, however, measurements are rarely independent—they carry feature dependency.

Suppose we collect data on homes in a real estate market, recording three features for each property: living area, number of bedrooms, and sale price.

A home with large square footage almost always has more bedrooms; as living area and bedroom count expand, the market price naturally climbs alongside them. Treating them as three separate measurements creates an illusion of having three independent pieces of information—when in truth, feature dependency means duplicate information.

Whenever features depend on each other—whether that dependency is linear, quadratic, or a complex curve—the data points do not scatter randomly to fill the entire 3D volume. Instead, they collapse onto a lower-dimensional manifold: a flat sheet or an elongated cloud.

Our intention in Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is to discover a new coordinate system aligned directly with the data's natural spread, where transformed features become independent and redundant dimensions can be safely dropped.

To see how this redundancy reveals itself geometrically, imagine plotting this real estate dataset in 3D coordinate space: living area along the $x$-axis, bedrooms along the $y$-axis, and price along the $z$-axis (Figure 1). What does the data cloud look like?

Visualizing Redundancy

Because living area, bedrooms, and price increase together, the points do not fill the 3D space—they cluster into an elongated, nearly flat cloud:

  • Area POV vs. Bedrooms POV: Both angles look virtually identical. Area and bedrooms move in lockstep, repeating the same story about house size.
  • Slant Plane (3D → 2D): Because two features duplicate each other, flattening onto a 2D slant plane discards almost no information.
  • Principal Axis (2D → 1D): Within that plane, points still stretch along a single dominant diagonal of maximum spread.

From both points of view, our original axes fail to give us what we want—they only repeat redundant information. This leaves us with an intuitive question:

Can we find a new coordinate axis that aligns directly with the natural spread of the data, rather than our arbitrary measurement tools?

Geometric First Principles

To find a natural coordinate axis, we must explore what "aligning with the data" actually requires. Let us unfold this concept step by step by asking three fundamental questions.

1. Where Should the New Axis Pass Through?

An axis is a straight line through space. But where should this line be anchored?

If we draw an axis through an arbitrary corner of space far away from the data, each home's coordinate along that line will mostly measure the massive distance from the origin to the neighborhood, rather than how homes differ from one another.

To measure pure internal variation, our coordinate system must be anchored at the physical balance point of the data—its center of mass. For a dataset of $n$ observations $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, we calculate the sample mean vector:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$

and shift every observation so that the center of mass rests at the origin:

$$x_i \leftarrow x_i - \bar{x}$$

Now, the dataset satisfies $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \mathbf{0}$. With the origin placed at the heart of the cloud, any line passing through the origin is a candidate axis of variation, and the squared distance $\|x_i\|^2$ measures pure variance from the dataset's mean.

Watch Figure 2 below as the data cloud centers itself. The observations continuously glide along the negative sample mean vector $-\bar{x}$ until their center of mass coincides exactly with the origin $(0, 0, 0)$.

2. How Do We Measure Points Along a Candidate Axis?

Any candidate axis passing through the origin can be defined by a unit direction vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (with length $\|w\| = 1$).

How does a 3D house get placed onto this 1D line? We shine a light perpendicular to the axis and observe where the point casts its shadow:

  • The scalar coordinate along the axis (the length of the projection shadow):
    $$z_i = w^T x_i = \sum_{j=1}^d w_j x_{ij}$$
  • The reconstructed position in space (the shadow itself lying on the line spanned by $w$):
    $$\hat{x}_i = (w^T x_i)w$$
  • The reconstruction error (the perpendicular gap between the original point and the axis):
    $$e_i = x_i - (w^T x_i)w$$

Notice that the original point splits cleanly into two perpendicular pieces:

$$x_i = \underbrace{(w^T x_i)w}_{\text{preserved projection}} \;+\; \underbrace{e_i}_{\text{discarded error}}$$

3. What Does "Best Alignment" Actually Mean?

If we can rotate this candidate axis $w$ in any direction around the origin, how do we decide which direction aligns "best" with the natural shape of the data?

Two completely different, highly intuitive answers immediately come to mind:

  1. The Information View (Maximize Spread): We want the projected shadows along the axis to spread out as wide as possible. If all homes collapse into a tight cluster at the same point, we lose the ability to distinguish small homes from mansions. Therefore, the optimal axis should maximize the variance of the projected coordinates:
    $$\max_{\|w\|=1} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (w^T x_i)^2$$
  2. The Compression View (Minimize Loss): We want the axis to hug the data cloud as closely as possible, keeping the discarded perpendicular drop lines as short as possible. Therefore, the optimal axis should minimize the mean squared reconstruction error:
    $$\min_{\|w\|=1} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - (w^T x_i)w\|^2$$

At first glance, this raises an apparent conflict:

Are these two goals in tension? Could a direction that spreads out the shadows also force some points further away from the axis?

4. The Pythagorean Conservation Law

To resolve this question, examine the right triangle visualized in Figure 3. Because the projection vector $(w^T x_i)w$ lies directly along the axis and the error vector $e_i$ is perpendicular to it (since $w^T e_i = w^T(x_i - (w^T x_i)w) = w^T x_i - (w^T x_i)(w^T w) = 0$), they form the perpendicular legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the observation vector $x_i$.

By the Pythagorean theorem, their squared lengths satisfy:

$$\begin{aligned} \|x_i\|^2 &= \|(w^T x_i)w\|^2 + \|e_i\|^2 \\[6pt] &= (w^T x_i)^2 \|w\|^2 + \|e_i\|^2 \\[6pt] &= (w^T x_i)^2 + \|e_i\|^2 \end{aligned}$$

Summing this equation across all $n$ observations and dividing by $n$:

$$\begin{aligned} \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2}_{\text{Total Variance (Constant)}} &= \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (w^T x_i)^2}_{\text{Preserved Projection Variance}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|e_i\|^2}_{\text{Mean Squared Error}} \end{aligned}$$

This equation resolves our dilemma completely: the left-hand side is the total sum of squared distances from the origin to all data points—a fixed constant determined solely by the dataset itself, completely independent of our choice of $w$.

Because their sum is locked in place, the two goals are not in tension at all. Every unit of variance preserved along the axis directly subtracts from the error discarded perpendicular to it. We have arrived at the foundational duality of dimensionality reduction:

The Equivalence Theorem of PCA
$$\max_{\|w\|=1} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (w^T x_i)^2 \;\iff\; \min_{\|w\|=1} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - (w^T x_i)w\|^2$$

Maximizing the preserved projection variance along a unit direction $w$ is mathematically identical to minimizing the mean squared reconstruction error discarded perpendicular to $w$.

This brings us to the core computational challenge: now that we know what makes a direction optimal, how do we mathematically calculate the exact vector $w$ that achieves it?

How do we mathematically calculate the optimal unit vector $w$ that maximizes this preserved variance?

From Variance to Eigendecomposition

Let us write the preserved variance in compact matrix form. For any centered data vector $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the scalar projection is $x_i^T w = w^T x_i$. Its square is:

$$\begin{aligned} (x_i^T w)^2 &= (w^T x_i)(x_i^T w) \\[6pt] &= w^T (x_i x_i^T) w \end{aligned}$$

Summing across all $n$ points and dividing by $n$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^T w)^2 &= w^T \left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T \right) w \\[6pt] &= w^T C w \end{aligned}$$

Look at the matrix operator that has just emerged naturally between our projection vectors:

$$\begin{aligned} C &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n} X X^T \end{aligned}$$

This is the sample covariance matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, where $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is the data matrix whose columns are the centered observations.

Why the Covariance Matrix Matters

Notice what just happened. Our raw data matrix $X$ was rectangular ($d \times n$). You cannot take eigenvectors of a rectangular matrix! It is not square, it has no symmetry, and its rows and columns live in two completely different spaces.

Yet the moment we asked for the variance along a projection direction $w$, the algebra handed us $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$. And this matrix is wonderful. It is square ($d \times d$), mapping the feature space directly back onto itself. It is symmetric:

$$\begin{aligned} C^T &= \left(\frac{1}{n} X X^T\right)^T \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n} (X^T)^T X^T \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n} X X^T \\[6pt] &= C \end{aligned}$$

And it is positive semidefinite. For any direction $w$, the projected variance $w^T C w = \frac{1}{n} \|X^T w\|^2 \ge 0$ is never negative.

In linear algebra, symmetric matrices are king. The moment a real matrix is symmetric, the Spectral Theorem steps in and does almost all the work for us.

First, the eigenvectors are automatically perpendicular. A symmetric matrix always has a complete orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $\{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_d\}$. We never have to fight or artificially constrain our coordinate axes to be orthogonal—symmetry gives us a perpendicular coordinate grid for free.

Second, the eigenvalues are real, non-negative ($\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$), and they have an immediate physical meaning: the eigenvalue is the variance. Look at what happens when you project the centered data onto any unit eigenvector $w_i$:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(w_i^T x) &= w_i^T C w_i \\[6pt] &= w_i^T (\lambda_i w_i) \\[6pt] &= \lambda_i (w_i^T w_i) \\[6pt] &= \lambda_i \end{aligned}$$

The spread of the data cloud along direction $w_i$ is literally $\lambda_i$.

So before we write down a single line of calculus, the entire picture of Principal Component Analysis is already right in front of us:

Where is the spread largest? Along the eigenvector $w_1$ with the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. That is our first principal component.

Where is the next-largest spread, perpendicular to the first? The Spectral Theorem already guarantees that the other eigenvectors are perpendicular to $w_1$. So the next-best perpendicular direction is the second eigenvector $w_2$, with eigenvalue $\lambda_2$. That is the second principal component. And the remaining components simply follow the descending order of their eigenvalues.

This is the beauty of linear algebra: the geometric axes of maximum spread and the algebraic eigenvectors of the symmetric covariance matrix are the exact same thing. Let us now verify this directly with calculus.

Maximizing the First Principal Component

To find the first principal component $w_1$, we maximize the preserved variance subject to $w$ being a unit vector:

$$\max_{w} \; w^T C w \quad \text{subject to} \quad w^T w = 1$$

We formulate the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = w^T C w - \lambda(w^T w - 1)$. Taking the gradient with respect to $w$ and setting it to zero:

$$\nabla_w \mathcal{L} = 2 C w - 2 \lambda w = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C w = \lambda w$$

Multiplying on the left by $w^T$:

$$\begin{aligned} w^T C w &= w^T (\lambda w) \\[6pt] &= \lambda (w^T w) \\[6pt] &= \lambda \end{aligned}$$

The variance captured along direction $w$ is precisely the eigenvalue $\lambda$. To maximize variance, we pick the eigenvector $w_1$ corresponding to the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = \lambda_{\max}(C)$.

Finding Subsequent Orthogonal Components

To find the second component $w_2$, we maximize variance subject to unit norm ($w_2^T w_2 = 1$) and orthogonality to the first component ($w_2^T w_1 = 0$):

$$\mathcal{L}(w_2, \lambda, \mu) = w_2^T C w_2 - \lambda(w_2^T w_2 - 1) - 2\mu (w_2^T w_1)$$

Setting the gradient with respect to $w_2$ to zero:

$$\nabla_{w_2} \mathcal{L} = 2 C w_2 - 2 \lambda w_2 - 2 \mu w_1 = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C w_2 = \lambda w_2 + \mu w_1$$

Multiplying on the left by $w_1^T$:

$$\begin{aligned} w_1^T C w_2 &= \lambda (w_1^T w_2) + \mu (w_1^T w_1) \\[6pt] &= 0 + \mu(1) \\[6pt] &= \mu \end{aligned}$$

Because $C$ is symmetric ($C = C^T$) and $C w_1 = \lambda_1 w_1$, the left-hand side is:

$$\begin{aligned} w_1^T C w_2 &= (C w_1)^T w_2 \\[6pt] &= (\lambda_1 w_1)^T w_2 \\[6pt] &= \lambda_1 (w_1^T w_2) \\[6pt] &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, $\mu = 0$, which reduces the equation back to $C w_2 = \lambda w_2$. The second principal component $w_2$ is simply the eigenvector corresponding to the second-largest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$.

Why Eigenvectors Globally Maximize Variance

We found $w_1$ and $w_2$ using calculus with Lagrange multipliers. But why are we guaranteed that no other direction in the entire $d$-dimensional space can yield higher variance? Let us prove it from geometric first principles:

  1. The Spectral Theorem Eigenbasis: Because $C$ is a real symmetric matrix ($C^T = C$), the Spectral Theorem guarantees that $C$ has $d$ mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors $\{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_d\}$ with non-negative real eigenvalues sorted in descending order:
    $$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$$
    Because these $d$ vectors are mutually perpendicular and unit length ($w_j^T w_k = \delta_{jk}$), they form a complete orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^d$—a rotated coordinate grid aligned with the data cloud.
  2. Expressing Any Arbitrary Direction $w$: Since $\{w_1, \dots, w_d\}$ is a complete basis, any candidate unit vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ can be expressed as a linear combination of these eigenvectors:
    $$w = c_1 w_1 + c_2 w_2 + \dots + c_d w_d = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j w_j$$
    where each $c_j = w^T w_j$ is the coordinate of $w$ along eigenvector axis $w_j$. Because $w$ has unit length ($\|w\|^2 = w^T w = 1$) and the basis is orthonormal:
    $$\|w\|^2 = \left(\sum_{i=1}^d c_i w_i\right)^T \left(\sum_{j=1}^d c_j w_j\right) = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j^2 = 1$$
    You can think of each $c_j^2 \ge 0$ as a budget allocation (weights summing to $1.0$) describing how much direction $w$ points along each eigenvector axis.
  3. Computing the Variance $w^T C w$: When the covariance matrix $C$ acts on $w$, by definition of eigenvectors ($C w_j = \lambda_j w_j$):
    $$C w = C\left(\sum_{j=1}^d c_j w_j\right) = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j (C w_j) = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j \lambda_j w_j$$
    Multiplying on the left by $w^T$:
    $$w^T C w = \left(\sum_{i=1}^d c_i w_i\right)^T \left(\sum_{j=1}^d c_j \lambda_j w_j\right) = \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j c_j^2$$

    Look at this equation carefully: The variance along any direction $w$ is simply a weighted average of the eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, weighted by $c_j^2$!

  4. Upper Bounding by the Maximum Eigenvalue $\lambda_1$: Since all eigenvalues are bounded by the largest one ($\lambda_j \le \lambda_1$ for all $j$):
    $$w^T C w = \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j c_j^2 \;\le\; \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_1 c_j^2 \;=\; \lambda_1 \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^d c_j^2}_{= 1} \;=\; \lambda_1$$
The Global Optimality of Eigenvectors
$$w^T C w \le \lambda_1$$

To maximize the weighted sum $\sum \lambda_j c_j^2$, you must allocate $100\%$ of your budget to the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$:

$$c_1^2 = 1 \implies c_1 = 1, \quad c_2 = c_3 = \dots = c_d = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w = w_1$$

No unit direction in $\mathbb{R}^d$ can ever yield variance greater than $\lambda_1$, and the unique direction that achieves this maximum is precisely the first eigenvector $w_1$.

Variance Conservation & Information Retention

Total Variance Conservation

Now that we have established that the orthogonal eigenvectors $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_d$ are the principal directions of variance, we can understand how they partition the total information in our dataset.

Since $w_1, \dots, w_d$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$, any centered data vector $x_i$ can be written exactly as a linear combination of these eigenvectors:

$$x_i = \sum_{k=1}^d (x_i^T w_k) w_k$$

Because these basis vectors are orthonormal ($w_j^T w_k = \delta_{jk}$), the Pythagorean theorem holds across all $d$ dimensions:

$$\|x_i\|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^d (x_i^T w_k)^2$$

To find the average spread of our entire dataset, we sum this squared length across all $n$ data points and divide by $n$. By exchanging the order of summation:

$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^d (x_i^T w_k)^2 = \sum_{k=1}^d \left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^T w_k)^2 \right)$$

Notice that the term inside the parenthesis on the right is exactly the sample variance of our projected data points along direction $w_k$, which is equal to $w_k^T C w_k = \lambda_k$. This gives us the Total Variance Conservation Law:

$$\text{Total Variance} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^d \lambda_k$$

This conservation law reveals that the total variance in the original coordinate system is completely preserved when we rotate our axes to the principal component coordinates—the sum of the variances along each eigenvector (eigenvalue) is exactly equal to the total variance of the raw features.

Iterative Residue Decomposition & Deflation

This allows us to view dimensionality reduction as a greedy, iterative deflation of variance. When we project our data onto the first principal component $w_1$, the residual vector for observation $x_i$ is:

$$x_i^{(1)} = x_i - (w_1^T x_i)w_1$$

Crucially, this residue is not useless error—it contains structured information along the remaining directions! To extract the next most informative direction, we construct the residue covariance matrix:

$$C^{(1)} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^{(1)} (x_i^{(1)})^T$$

and solve for the unit direction $w_2$ that maximizes $w^T C^{(1)} w$. Because $w_1^T x_i^{(1)} = w_1^T x_i - (w_1^T x_i)(w_1^T w_1) = 0$, every single residue vector has zero projection onto $w_1$. Consequently, the optimal direction $w_2$ is automatically orthogonal to $w_1$:

$$w_1^T w_2 = 0$$

Furthermore, because $w_1^T w_2 = 0$, projecting the residue onto $w_2$ simplifies cleanly:

$$\begin{aligned} w_2^T x_i^{(1)} &= w_2^T \left( x_i - (w_1^T x_i)w_1 \right) \\[6pt] &= w_2^T x_i - (w_1^T x_i)(w_2^T w_1) \\[6pt] &= w_2^T x_i \end{aligned}$$

and the residue after round 2 becomes simply:

$$x_i^{(2)} = x_i - (w_1^T x_i)w_1 - (w_2^T x_i)w_2$$

Continuing this deflation for $d$ rounds, the residues must collapse to exactly zero:

$$\begin{aligned} x_i - \sum_{k=1}^d (w_k^T x_i)w_k &= \mathbf{0} \\[8pt] x_i &= \sum_{k=1}^d (w_k^T x_i)w_k \end{aligned}$$

because the $d$ orthonormal eigenvectors $\{w_1, \dots, w_d\}$ form a complete basis for $\mathbb{R}^d$. By the Pythagorean theorem, the total variance decomposes into the sum of the individual component variances:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2 &= \sum_{k=1}^d \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (w_k^T x_i)^2 \\[8pt] &= \sum_{k=1}^d w_k^T C w_k \\[8pt] &= \sum_{k=1}^d \lambda_k \end{aligned}$$

Signal vs. Noise: The Scree Spectrum

In each round $k$, the variance captured along direction $w_k$ is precisely the eigenvalue $\lambda_k = w_k^T C w_k$. When we plot these eigenvalues in descending order:

$$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$$

a characteristic pattern appears across real datasets: the curve drops steeply over the first few components (representing the true signal of the underlying data manifold) and then flattens into an extended, low-variance floor (representing random measurement noise).

Concrete Compression Economics

What happens when data lives in an approximately low-dimensional subspace? If the trailing eigenvalues $\lambda_{K+1}, \dots, \lambda_d$ are negligibly small, we can truncate the expansion at $K \ll d$ components:

$$x_i \approx \sum_{k=1}^K (w_k^T x_i)w_k$$

Let us evaluate the actual storage savings with concrete numbers:

  • Uncompressed Storage: Storing $n$ observations in $\mathbb{R}^d$ requires $n \times d$ floating-point numbers.
  • Compressed Storage: Storing the $K$ basis vectors $\{w_1, \dots, w_K\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ requires $K \times d$ numbers. Storing the $K$-dimensional coordinates $[w_1^T x_i, \dots, w_K^T x_i]^T$ for all $n$ points requires $n \times K$ numbers.

Consider a realistic machine learning dataset with $n = 10{,}000$ samples and $d = 1{,}000$ feature measurements, compressed down to $K = 10$ principal components:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Without Compression} &= n \times d \\[6pt] &= 10{,}000 \times 1{,}000 \\[6pt] &= 10{,}000{,}000 \text{ numbers } (10^7) \\[10pt] \text{With Compression} &= (n \times K) + (K \times d) \\[6pt] &= (10{,}000 \times 10) + (10 \times 1{,}000) \\[6pt] &= 100{,}000 + 10{,}000 \\[6pt] &= 110{,}000 \text{ numbers} \\[10pt] \text{Compression Ratio} &= \frac{10{,}000{,}000}{110{,}000} \approx 90.9\times \end{aligned}$$

We have reduced our storage footprint by over $90\times$ while retaining virtually all of the essential geometric information!

What Does It Mean to "Explain X% of Variance"?

When we compress our dataset onto the first $K$ principal components ($K < d$), the fraction of the total dataset variance captured is the Explained Variance Ratio:

$$\text{Explained Variance Ratio} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j}$$

When an analysis states that the top components explain $95\%$ of the variance, it means:

$$\frac{\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j} \ge 0.95$$

Geometrically, $95\%$ of the total squared distance of the centered observations from their mean is preserved along the $K$-dimensional hyperplane spanned by $\{w_1, \dots, w_K\}$, while only $5\%$ lies in the discarded perpendicular residuals.

Why $z_i = W_K^T x_i$? Deriving the Optimal Latent Representation

Suppose we have chosen our $K$-dimensional orthonormal basis $W_K = [w_1, w_2, \dots, w_K] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times K}$, where $W_K^T W_K = I_K$. Any reconstruction of a centered point $x_i$ lying on this $K$-dimensional hyperplane is a linear combination of these basis vectors:

$$\hat{x}_i = W_K z_i = \sum_{k=1}^K z_{ik} w_k$$

for some vector of coordinates $z_i = [z_{i1}, \dots, z_{iK}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^K$. But how do we choose the coordinate vector $z_i$?

We demand that $\hat{x}_i$ be the best possible reconstruction of $x_i$ by minimizing the squared reconstruction error (the Euclidean distance from $x_i$ to the subspace):

$$\min_{z_i} \mathcal{L}(z_i) = \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2 = \|x_i - W_K z_i\|^2$$

Let us expand this quadratic objective using vector inner products:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(z_i) &= (x_i - W_K z_i)^T (x_i - W_K z_i) \\[6pt] &= x_i^T x_i - x_i^T W_K z_i - z_i^T W_K^T x_i + z_i^T W_K^T W_K z_i \\[6pt] &= x_i^T x_i - 2 z_i^T W_K^T x_i + z_i^T (W_K^T W_K) z_i \end{aligned}$$

Because the principal component columns of $W_K$ are orthonormal, $W_K^T W_K = I_K$. The quadratic term simplifies immediately:

$$\mathcal{L}(z_i) = x_i^T x_i - 2 z_i^T W_K^T x_i + z_i^T z_i$$

To find the minimizer $z_i^*$, we take the gradient with respect to $z_i$ and set it to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_{z_i} \mathcal{L}(z_i) &= -2 W_K^T x_i + 2 z_i = \mathbf{0} \\[8pt] 2 z_i &= 2 W_K^T x_i \\[8pt] z_i^* &= W_K^T x_i \end{aligned}$$

The optimal encoding coordinate $z_i$ is nothing other than the orthogonal projection of $x_i$ onto each principal axis: $z_{ik} = w_k^T x_i$.

Plugging this optimal code back into our reconstruction gives the orthogonal projection operator:

$$\hat{x}_i = W_K z_i^* = W_K (W_K^T x_i) = (W_K W_K^T) x_i$$

The matrix $P_K = W_K W_K^T \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is symmetric ($P_K^T = P_K$) and idempotent ($P_K^2 = W_K (W_K^T W_K) W_K^T = W_K I_K W_K^T = P_K$). It orthogonally projects any ambient vector $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ onto the $K$-dimensional subspace, guaranteeing that the residual vector $e_i = x_i - \hat{x}_i$ is strictly perpendicular to the hyperplane:

$$\begin{aligned} W_K^T (x_i - \hat{x}_i) &= W_K^T (x_i - W_K W_K^T x_i) \\[6pt] &= W_K^T x_i - (W_K^T W_K) W_K^T x_i \\[6pt] &= W_K^T x_i - I_K W_K^T x_i = \mathbf{0} \end{aligned}$$
The Operational Blueprint: Principal Component Analysis
Stage Mathematical Formulation Geometric Purpose & Physical Meaning
1. Data Ingestion $$\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$$ $$X = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^T \\ \vdots \\ x_n^T \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$$ Unlabeled collection of $n$ observations living in an ambient $d$-dimensional space with correlated, redundant features.
2. Centering
(Zero-Mean Anchoring) 		$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$ $$x_i \leftarrow x_i - \bar{x}$$ Translates the physical center of mass to the origin ($\sum x_i = \mathbf{0}$). Guarantees that subsequent axes passing through the origin measure pure internal variance rather than offset distance.
3. Spectral Analysis
(Covariance Eigendecomposition) 		$$S = \frac{1}{n} X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$$ $$S w_j = \lambda_j w_j, \quad \|w_j\| = 1$$ $$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$$ By the Spectral Theorem, symmetric positive semidefinite $S$ yields an orthonormal basis of mutually perpendicular axes $\{w_j\}$, where each eigenvalue $\lambda_j$ literally equals the variance preserved along axis $w_j$.
4. Dimensionality Selection
(Energy Conservation Budget) 		$$\text{Choose smallest } K \text{ such that:}$$ $$\frac{\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j} \ge 0.95$$ Discards the $(d - K)$ weakest orthogonal directions whose aggregate variance is negligible ($< 5\%$), preserving $95\%$ of the total data energy in a compact $K$-dimensional subspace.
5. Encoding
(Subspace Projection) 		$$z_i = W_K^T x_i = \begin{bmatrix} w_1^T x_i \\ \vdots \\ w_K^T x_i \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^K$$ $$Z = X W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}$$ Computes the optimal scalar coordinates $z_i$ that minimize the reconstruction loss $\|x_i - W_K z_i\|^2$. Because $W_K^T W_K = I_K$, setting $\nabla_{z_i} \|x_i - W_K z_i\|^2 = \mathbf{0}$ yields the orthogonal projection $z_i = W_K^T x_i$. The transformed features $z_i$ are decorrelated ($\operatorname{Cov}(Z) = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_K)$).
6. Decoding
(Optimal Reconstruction) 		$$\hat{x}_i = W_K z_i + \bar{x} = \sum_{k=1}^K z_{ik} w_k + \bar{x}$$ $$\text{Residual Loss: } \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2 = \sum_{j=K+1}^d (w_j^T x_i)^2$$ Reconstructs the original observation back in ambient $\mathbb{R}^d$ via $\hat{x}_i = W_K W_K^T x_i + \bar{x}$. Since $z_i = W_K^T x_i$ is the least-squares solution and the residual $(x_i - \hat{x}_i) \perp \operatorname{span}(W_K)$, by the Pythagorean Theorem $\hat{x}_i$ is the mathematically closest possible point on the $K$-dimensional hyperplane to the original point $x_i$.

A Concrete Numerical Walkthrough: Putting the Blueprint into Practice

Formulas become second nature when we see their gears turn on real numbers. Let us trace all six steps of our blueprint on a tiny toy dataset: $n = 4$ homes evaluated across $d = 2$ features: living space (in hundreds of square feet) and assessed value (in tens of thousands of dollars).

Our four raw observations in $\mathbb{R}^2$ are:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Step 1: Compute the Center of Mass (Sample Mean)

Before looking for directions of variance, we find the balance point of the data:

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{x} &= \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^4 x_i \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \begin{bmatrix} 16 \\ 24 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

On average, a home in this neighborhood has $400\text{ sq ft}$ and costs $\$60{,}000$.

Step 2: Center the Dataset

We translate every home so that the origin sits squarely at the centroid $\bar{x}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{x}_1 &= x_1 - \bar{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 - 4 \\ 7 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[6pt] \tilde{x}_2 &= x_2 - \bar{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 - 4 \\ 8 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \\[6pt] \tilde{x}_3 &= x_3 - \bar{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 4 \\ 4 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \\[6pt] \tilde{x}_4 &= x_4 - \bar{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 4 \\ 5 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Notice that the centered points sum exactly to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^4 \tilde{x}_i &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 1 - 1 - 2 \\ 1 + 2 - 2 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Step 3: Construct the Sample Covariance Matrix & Eigendecomposition

With the data centered, the outer products $\tilde{x}_i \tilde{x}_i^T$ capture the pairwise spread:

$$\begin{aligned} S &= \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^4 \tilde{x}_i \tilde{x}_i^T \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 8 \\ 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 & 2.0 \\ 2.0 & 2.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

The diagonal entries show that each feature has variance $s_1^2 = s_2^2 = 2.5$. The off-diagonal covariance is $+2.0$, confirming a strong positive correlation: bigger homes cost more. The total variance contained in our system is:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(S) = 2.5 + 2.5 = 5.0 \end{aligned}$$

Next, we solve the characteristic equation $\det(S - \lambda I) = 0$:

$$\begin{aligned} \det \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 - \lambda & 2.0 \\ 2.0 & 2.5 - \lambda \end{bmatrix} &= (2.5 - \lambda)^2 - (2.0)^2 \\[6pt] &= \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6.25 - 4 \\[6pt] &= \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 2.25 \\[6pt] &= (\lambda - 4.5)(\lambda - 0.5) = 0 \end{aligned}$$

The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 4.5$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.5$. Notice that their sum preserves the total variance: $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 4.5 + 0.5 = 5.0 = \operatorname{tr}(S)$.

Finding the normalized eigenvectors $(S - \lambda I)w = 0$:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{For } \lambda_1 = 4.5: \quad &\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} w_{11} \\ w_{12} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies w_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] \text{For } \lambda_2 = 0.5: \quad &\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} w_{21} \\ w_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies w_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

The vector $w_1$ points along the $45^\circ$ line where size and price grow together. The vector $w_2$ is strictly orthogonal ($w_1^T w_2 = 0$) and captures perpendicular noise.

A Practical Note on Real-World Scale: Solving $\det(S - \lambda I) = 0$ by expanding the characteristic polynomial is simple for a $2 \times 2$ problem with pen and paper. However, for matrices with hundreds or thousands of features, polynomial root-finding becomes mathematically and numerically impossible (by the Abel–Ruffini theorem and Wilkinson's extreme instability). In production, scientific software never evaluates characteristic polynomials; instead, it relies on iterative numerical linear algebra methods (such as the QR algorithm, Lanczos Krylov iteration, and direct bidiagonal SVD), which we explore in depth in our companion lesson PCA ●─● Spectral Theorem & SVD.

Step 4: Select Subspace Dimension ($K$)

We evaluate the Explained Variance Ratio (EVR) for $K = 1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{EVR}(1) &= \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2} \\[6pt] &= \frac{4.5}{4.5 + 0.5} \\[6pt] &= \frac{4.5}{5.0} = 90.0\% \end{aligned}$$

A single principal axis preserves $90\%$ of all information in the dataset! We choose $K = 1$, reducing our feature space from 2 dimensions down to 1. Our projection matrix is simply:

$$\begin{aligned} W_1 = w_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Step 5: Encode (Compress $2\text{D} \to 1\text{D}$)

We project each centered home $\tilde{x}_i$ onto $w_1$ by taking the dot product $z_i = w_1^T \tilde{x}_i$:

$$\begin{aligned} z_1 &= w_1^T \tilde{x}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \approx +2.121 \\[6pt] z_2 &= w_1^T \tilde{x}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \approx +2.121 \\[6pt] z_3 &= w_1^T \tilde{x}_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \approx -2.121 \\[6pt] z_4 &= w_1^T \tilde{x}_4 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \approx -2.121 \end{aligned}$$

Each 2-dimensional property is now completely summarized by a single scalar coordinate $z_i$. Let us verify the sample variance of this compressed latent representation:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(z) &= \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^4 z_i^2 \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( \left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 \right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( 4.5 + 4.5 + 4.5 + 4.5 \right) \\[6pt] &= 4.5 = \lambda_1 \end{aligned}$$

The variance of our 1D coordinates matches the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ to the last decimal place!

Step 6: Decode (Reconstruction & Error Quantification)

Can we take our compressed scalar $z_i$ and reconstruct the original 2D home? Using the decoding formula $\hat{x}_i = W_1 z_i + \bar{x} = z_i w_1 + \bar{x}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{x}_1 &= z_1 w_1 + \bar{x} \\[6pt] &= \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right) + \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] &= \frac{3}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 1.5 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5.5 \\ 7.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Similarly, decoding all four points:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{x}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 5.5 \\ 7.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 5.5 \\ 7.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 4.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \hat{x}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 4.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Let us calculate the exact reconstruction error vector $e_i = x_i - \hat{x}_i$ for each home:

$$\begin{aligned} e_1 &= x_1 - \hat{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 5.5 \\ 7.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} +0.5 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] e_2 &= x_2 - \hat{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 5.5 \\ 7.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ +0.5 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] e_3 &= x_3 - \hat{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 4.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} +0.5 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] e_4 &= x_4 - \hat{x}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 4.5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ +0.5 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Notice that every error vector is directed strictly along the discarded axis $w_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} [-1, 1]^T$:

$$\begin{aligned} e_1 &= -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} w_2, \qquad e_2 = +\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} w_2, \qquad e_3 = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} w_2, \qquad e_4 = +\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} w_2 \end{aligned}$$

Finally, we evaluate the mean squared reconstruction error across the dataset:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{MSE} &= \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^4 \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2 \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( (0.5^2 + (-0.5)^2) + ((-0.5)^2 + 0.5^2) + (0.5^2 + (-0.5)^2) + ((-0.5)^2 + 0.5^2) \right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{1}{4} \left( 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 \right) \\[6pt] &= 0.5 = \lambda_2 \end{aligned}$$

The reconstruction error matches the discarded eigenvalue $\lambda_2 = 0.5$ with mathematical perfection!

$$\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\text{Preserved})}_{\lambda_1 = 4.5} + \underbrace{\operatorname{MSE}(\text{Discarded})}_{\lambda_2 = 0.5} = \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\text{Total})}_{\operatorname{tr}(S) = 5.0}$$ The Pythagorean theorem guarantees that no variance is created or destroyed—what we do not encode in $z_i$ becomes our exact reconstruction loss.

Where This Leads: From Vectors to Matrix Operators

We have constructed Principal Component Analysis entirely from first principles. By asking where an axis should be anchored, in which direction it should point, and how subsequent directions must be constrained, we discovered:

  • Centering observations at the center of mass ensures that distance from the origin measures pure internal variance rather than coordinate offset.
  • Maximizing projected variance via Lagrange multipliers leads inevitably to the dominant eigenvector $w_1$ of the sample covariance matrix $C$.
  • Deflating the dataset produces mutually orthogonal eigenvectors $w_2, \dots, w_d$, each capturing a decreasing slice of variance equal to its eigenvalue $\lambda_k$.
  • The Pythagorean theorem guarantees exact energy conservation: $\operatorname{Var}(\text{Preserved}) + \operatorname{MSE}(\text{Discarded}) = \operatorname{Var}(\text{Total})$.
  • The optimal low-dimensional latent code that minimizes Euclidean reconstruction error is simply the orthogonal projection shadow $z_i = W_K^T x_i$.

Our geometric and statistical journey is complete—we can now compute, encode, and decode PCA models by hand with pen and paper.

Yet this opens a critical practical question: How do scientific software packages execute this transformation simultaneously across millions of dimensions and samples without looping through one eigenvector at a time? And why does production software almost never form the covariance matrix $X^T X$ directly?
