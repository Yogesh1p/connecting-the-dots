In the previous lesson, we looked at Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). The idea was straightforward: collect some data, write down the probability of observing that data given a candidate parameter $\theta$, and pick the parameter value that makes that probability as large as possible.

If you have huge amounts of data, MLE works very well. But when data is scarce, it runs into severe trouble.

Suppose you pick up a coin, flip it 3 times, and get 3 heads. What does MLE say?

$$\hat{p}_{ML} = \frac{3}{3} = 1.0$$

MLE claims that the probability of getting tails is literally zero. According to MLE, this coin will never land on tails for the rest of eternity.

Nobody actually believes that. If a friend flips a coin 3 times and gets 3 heads, you wouldn't bet your life savings that the 4th toss is guaranteed to be heads. Why? Because you didn't enter the room with a blank slate. You already have experience with physical coins. You have a hunch that coins are usually close to fair.

Earlier, we saw Laplace fix this with his rule of succession:

$$\hat{p}_{\text{new}} = \frac{n_h + 1}{n + 2}$$

By pretending we had already seen 1 virtual head and 1 virtual tail, Laplace gave us $\frac{3 + 1}{3 + 2} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$ instead of $1.0$. And we proved that this biased estimator actually gets a lower Mean Squared Error than the "optimal" unbiased MLE when data is scarce.

"Where did the $+1$ and $+2$ come from? How do we formalize a hunch mathematically and systematically combine it with data?"

This brings us to Bayesian Estimation.

In Maximum Likelihood, the parameter $\theta$ is treated as a fixed, unknown constant in the world. Probability is only used to describe the data.

In Bayesian estimation, we flip that assumption on its head: we treat the parameter $\theta$ as a random variable itself.

Before we look at the data, we codify our hunch as a probability distribution over $\theta$. This is our prior distribution $P(\theta)$.

Then the data $D = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ arrives. We evaluate the likelihood $P(D \mid \theta)$.

Finally, we use Bayes' law to update our initial hunch into an updated belief: the posterior distribution $P(\theta \mid D)$.

Bayes' Law for Parameters

Let $\theta$ be an unknown parameter with prior distribution $P(\theta)$, and let $D$ be observed data. The posterior distribution of belief is:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\theta \mid D) &= \frac{P(D \mid \theta) P(\theta)}{P(D)} \\[8pt] &= \frac{P(D \mid \theta) P(\theta)}{\int_\Theta P(D \mid \theta') P(\theta')\,d\theta'} \end{aligned}$$

Here are the four pieces:

  • Prior $P(\theta)$: What you believed about $\theta$ before collecting any data.
  • Likelihood $P(D \mid \theta)$: How well each candidate value of $\theta$ explains the observed data.
  • Evidence $P(D)$: The normalizing constant (the denominator) that ensures the posterior integrates to $1$.
  • Posterior $P(\theta \mid D)$: Your updated belief about $\theta$ after absorbing the empirical evidence.

What If We Have No Hunch? The Uniform Prior

A fair question to ask first: "What if we have no prior hunch at all? What if we're flipping a coin from an alien civilization and literally have zero clue what $p$ could be?"

When we have no reason to favor any value over another, we use a flat, uniform prior. Every value of $p \in [0, 1]$ gets equal probability density:

$$P(p) = 1, \quad \forall p \in [0, 1]$$

Let's see what Bayes' law does when we plug in this uniform prior for $n$ coin flips with $n_h$ heads and $n_t$ tails:

$$\begin{aligned} P(p \mid D) &= \frac{P(D \mid p) \cdot 1}{\int_0^1 P(D \mid p') \cdot 1 \,dp'} \\[8pt] &= \frac{p^{n_h} (1 - p)^{n_t}}{\int_0^1 u^{n_h} (1 - u)^{n_t} \,du} \end{aligned}$$

Notice what happened: because $P(p) = 1$ is just a constant, the numerator is literally just the likelihood:

$$P(p \mid D) \propto P(D \mid p)$$

Under a uniform prior, the posterior is simply the normalized likelihood function.

Because the curve has the exact same shape as the likelihood, where is its peak (the mode)? At the exact same location:

$$\hat{p}_{\text{mode}} = \arg\max_p P(p \mid D) = \arg\max_p P(D \mid p) = \frac{n_h}{n} = \hat{p}_{ML}$$

This connects our first two dots: Maximum Likelihood is secretly a special case of Bayesian inference. It is what you get if you take the peak of the posterior under a flat, non-informative prior.

Taking the Mean of the Posterior

Picking the peak (the mode) is only one way to summarize a distribution. What if, instead of finding the single most likely point, we compute the center of mass (the mean) of our posterior belief?

Let's integrate $\int_0^1 p \cdot P(p \mid D) \,dp$. We can evaluate these integrals using the Beta function identity $\text{B}(a, b) = \int_0^1 u^{a-1}(1-u)^{b-1}\,du = \frac{(a-1)!(b-1)!}{(a+b-1)!}$ for integers:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[p \mid D] &= \frac{\int_0^1 p \cdot p^{n_h} (1 - p)^{n_t} \,dp}{\int_0^1 u^{n_h} (1 - u)^{n_t} \,du} \\[8pt] &= \frac{\int_0^1 p^{(n_h + 1)} (1 - p)^{n_t} \,dp}{\text{B}(n_h + 1, \; n_t + 1)} \\[8pt] &= \frac{\text{B}(n_h + 2, \; n_t + 1)}{\text{B}(n_h + 1, \; n_t + 1)} \\[8pt] &= \frac{\frac{(n_h + 1)! \, n_t!}{(n + 2)!}}{\frac{n_h! \, n_t!}{(n + 1)!}} \\[8pt] &= \frac{n_h + 1}{n + 2} \end{aligned}$$

Look at the result: $\frac{n_h + 1}{n + 2}$.

This is Laplace's rule of succession! Laplace's $+1$ wasn't an arbitrary hack. It is the exact expected value of your belief when you start with a uniform prior.

When Fisher picked the mode, he focused only on the peak and got $3/3 = 1.0$. When Laplace computed the expectation, he averaged across the entire curve, taking into account the possibility of tails, which gave $\frac{3 + 1}{3 + 2} = 0.8$.

"A uniform prior means total ignorance. How do we encode real hunches — like knowing a coin is probably fair?"

Flexible Hunches: The Beta Family & Conjugacy

Think carefully about what the parameter $p$ actually represents.

In our coin toss experiment, $p$ is the true underlying probability of landing Heads. Because it is a probability, it cannot be negative, and it cannot exceed $1$. But within that interval, $p$ can take any continuous real value between $0$ and $1$ ($p \in [0, 1]$). It could be $0.500$, $0.723$, $0.001$, or $0.999$.

In the previous section, we assumed complete ignorance: we drew a flat horizontal line across $[0, 1]$ and declared that every single value of $p$ was equally plausible ($P(p) = 1$).

But what if you do have a hunch?

If someone hands you a standard quarter from their pocket, you don't believe that $p = 0.05$ or $p = 0.95$ is just as likely as $p = 0.5$. You suspect—with high confidence—that the coin is manufactured well and lands near $0.5$. On the other hand, if a carnival operator hands you a coin, you might have a hunch it is heavily weighted, but you don't know which side is favored. If you are tossing a thumbtack or an asymmetric bottle cap, you might suspect it lands point-up far more often than point-down.

Because $p$ lives on a continuous continuum, we cannot represent our hunch as a single number or a discrete list. We need a continuous probability density function $P(p)$ defined over the entire interval $[0, 1]$.

What properties must such a distribution have?

  • Valid Probability Density: The total area under the curve across the unit interval must equal $1$: $$\int_0^1 P(p) \, dp = 1$$ and the density can never be negative ($P(p) \ge 0$).
  • Universal Geometric Flexibility: Depending on our prior knowledge, the curve must be able to bend, stretch, peak, or flatten into whatever shape our belief demands:
    • A sharp, narrow spike centered at $0.5$ (high certainty of a fair coin).
    • A gentle, wide mound around $0.5$ (a weak hunch of fairness).
    • An asymmetric hill leaning towards $1$ or $0$ (a hunch that heads or tails dominates).
    • A U-shaped bowl that peaks at both boundaries and dips in the middle (a belief that the coin is rigged toward extremes, but we aren't sure which side).
    • A flat horizontal line across $[0, 1]$ (our baseline of total ignorance).

"What mathematical function can flex into all of these continuous shapes over the interval $[0, 1]$ without spilling outside?"

Constructing the Function: The Beta Family

Look at the likelihood function we have been working with: $p^{n_h} (1 - p)^{n_t}$.

Notice how that algebraic product behaves on $[0, 1]$:

  • The factor $p$ naturally pulls the value to zero as $p \to 0$.
  • The factor $(1 - p)$ naturally pulls the value to zero as $p \to 1$.
  • Between $0$ and $1$, the product rises smoothly to form a curved hill!

What if we generalize this algebraic structure? Instead of data counts $n_h$ and $n_t$, let's introduce two adjustable continuous shape parameters, which we will call $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta > 0$:

$$P(p) \propto p^{\alpha - 1} (1 - p)^{\beta - 1}, \quad \text{for } p \in [0, 1]$$

(The $-1$ in the exponents is a standard convention so that when $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 1$, the powers vanish ($p^0 (1 - p)^0 = 1$), returning our familiar flat uniform prior.)

By simply turning these two knobs, $\alpha$ and $\beta$, we can sculpt every single hunch we imagined:

  • $\alpha = 1, \beta = 1$: A flat line ($p^0 (1-p)^0 = 1$). Total ignorance.
  • $\alpha = 2, \beta = 2$: A gentle parabolic mound peaking at $p = 0.5$. A mild hunch of fairness.
  • $\alpha = 10, \beta = 10$: A tall, concentrated bell curve at $0.5$. High confidence that the coin is fair.
  • $\alpha = 2, \beta = 5$: Asymmetric curve leaning toward tails, peaking at $p = 0.2$.
  • $\alpha = 0.5, \beta = 0.5$: Because both exponents are negative ($\alpha - 1 = -0.5$), the density shoots upward as $p \to 0$ and $p \to 1$, creating a U-shaped bowl! This models a belief that the coin is strongly rigged, but we don't know which side.

To turn this proportional shape into a strict probability distribution whose area integrates to $1$, we divide by the normalizing integral:

$$\text{B}(\alpha, \beta) = \int_0^1 u^{\alpha - 1}(1 - u)^{\beta - 1}\,du$$

This gives us the Beta distribution.

The Beta Distribution

A continuous random variable $p \in [0, 1]$ follows a Beta distribution with shape parameters $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta > 0$, written $p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$, if its probability density function is:

$$\text{Beta}(p; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{1}{\text{B}(\alpha, \beta)} p^{\alpha - 1} (1 - p)^{\beta - 1}, \quad p \in [0, 1]$$

where the Beta function $\text{B}(\alpha, \beta) = \int_0^1 u^{\alpha - 1}(1 - u)^{\beta - 1}\,du$ is the normalizing constant.

The Mathematical Miracle: Conjugacy & Pseudo-Counts

Beyond its geometric ability to shape any hunch over $[0, 1]$, the Beta family possesses a second, extraordinary mathematical superpower.

In Bayesian estimation, to compute the posterior distribution $P(p \mid D)$ from an arbitrary prior, Bayes' theorem requires dividing by the marginal likelihood:

$$P(p \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid p) \cdot P(p)}{\int_0^1 P(D \mid p') \cdot P(p') \, dp'}$$

If you pick an arbitrary mathematical function for your prior (like a piecewise polynomial or a cosine wave), that integral in the denominator becomes an intractable bottleneck that cannot be solved in closed form.

Now look at what happens when our prior comes from the Beta family.

We multiply our Bernoulli likelihood $P(D \mid p) \propto p^{n_h} (1 - p)^{n_t}$ directly by our Beta prior $P(p) \propto p^{\alpha - 1} (1 - p)^{\beta - 1}$:

$$\begin{aligned} P(p \mid D) &\propto P(D \mid p) \cdot P(p) \\[6pt] &\propto \Big[ p^{n_h} (1 - p)^{n_t} \Big] \cdot \Big[ p^{\alpha - 1} (1 - p)^{\beta - 1} \Big] \\[8pt] &= p^{(\alpha - 1) + n_h} (1 - p)^{(\beta - 1) + n_t} \\[8pt] &= p^{(\alpha + n_h) - 1} (1 - p)^{(\beta + n_t) - 1} \end{aligned}$$

Look at that final expression. The posterior has the exact same algebraic form as the prior!

We don't need to evaluate a nasty integral. We already know the normalizing constant for this functional form—it is simply another Beta distribution:

$$p \mid D \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + n_h, \;\beta + n_t)$$
Conjugate Prior

A prior distribution is called conjugate to a likelihood function if the resulting posterior distribution belongs to the exact same distribution family as the prior.

When a prior is conjugate, updating your belief after observing data never requires calculus. It simply reduces to updating the parameters of that distribution:

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha_{\text{post}} &= \alpha + n_h \\[6pt] \beta_{\text{post}} &= \beta + n_t \end{aligned}$$

This gives the parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ a clear physical meaning: they are pseudo-counts (virtual coin flips).

  • $\alpha - 1$ is the number of virtual heads you had observed in your mind before the experiment began.
  • $\beta - 1$ is the number of virtual tails you had observed in your mind before the experiment began.
  • $\alpha + \beta - 2$ represents your prior sample size—the strength or certainty of your hunch.

Every time you observe a real coin flip, you simply add it to your ledger: heads increment $\alpha$, tails increment $\beta$.

What Do We Do With the Posterior? MAP vs. Expected Belief

Now that we have the full posterior distribution $P(p \mid D) = \text{Beta}(\alpha + n_h, \beta + n_t)$, what do we do with it?

Often in machine learning, we need a single number to make a decision. There are two standard choices:

1. Maximum A Posteriori (MAP)

Pick the mode (peak) of the posterior distribution:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\theta}_{MAP} &= \arg\max_\theta P(\theta \mid D) \\[6pt] &= \arg\max_\theta \Big[ \log P(D \mid \theta) + \log P(\theta) \Big] \\[8pt] &= \arg\max_\theta \Big[ \underbrace{\ell(\theta)}_{\text{Likelihood}} + \underbrace{\log P(\theta)}_{\text{Prior Penalty}} \Big] \end{aligned}$$

Look at that last line: MAP estimation is Maximum Likelihood with a regularization term! The prior penalizes extreme, absurd parameter values, acting as a built-in regularizer.

For our Beta posterior with parameters $\alpha' = \alpha + n_h$ and $\beta' = \beta + n_t$, taking the derivative of the log density and setting it to zero gives the mode:

$$\hat{p}_{MAP} = \frac{\alpha' - 1}{\alpha' + \beta' - 2} = \mathbf{\frac{n_h + (\alpha - 1)}{n + (\alpha + \beta - 2)}}$$

2. Expected Posterior Belief (Bayes Estimator)

Instead of picking the peak, pick the mean (center of mass) of the posterior distribution:

$$\hat{p}_{\text{Bayes}} = \mathbb{E}[p \mid D] = \frac{\alpha'}{\alpha' + \beta'} = \mathbf{\frac{n_h + \alpha}{n + \alpha + \beta}}$$

If you evaluate your estimator using Mean Squared Error $(\hat{p} - p)^2$, decision theory proves that the posterior mean is the unique estimator that minimizes your expected loss.

Laplace from Both Perspectives

Notice how both estimators connect back to Laplace's rule of succession:

  • Under a uniform prior $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$, the posterior mean is $\frac{n_h + 1}{n + 2}$.
  • Under a weak fairness prior $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ (which encodes 1 prior head and 1 prior tail), the MAP mode is $\frac{n_h + (2-1)}{n + (2+2-2)} = \frac{n_h + 1}{n + 2}$.

Both paths arrive at the exact same formula.

What Happens When You Collect More Data?

What if your initial hunch was wrong? Suppose you thought a coin was biased toward heads ($\alpha = 10, \beta = 2$), but the coin is actually fair ($p = 0.5$).

At first, your estimate will be pulled toward your hunch. But as you collect real observations ($n \to \infty$):

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \hat{p}_{MAP} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n_h + \alpha - 1}{n + \alpha + \beta - 2} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n_h}{n} = \hat{p}_{ML}$$

The data always wins in the end. As sample size grows, the influence of the prior decays away, and the Bayesian estimate converges to the empirical MLE.

Continuous Parameters: Gaussian Conjugacy & "Ghost Samples"

We saw how this works for coin flips. Now let's apply the exact same logic to continuous numbers.

Suppose we collect $n$ measurements $x_1, \dots, x_n$ drawn from a Gaussian distribution with known measurement noise $\sigma^2$, but unknown mean $\mu$:

$$x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$$

The likelihood of our dataset given $\mu$ is:

$$P(X \mid \mu) = \left( 2\pi\sigma^2 \right)^{-n/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \right)$$

What prior over $\mu$ keeps this conjugate? Since the Gaussian likelihood is an exponential of a quadratic in $\mu$, choosing a Gaussian prior over the mean preserves that quadratic form:

$$\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2) \implies P(\mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_0^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(\mu - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2} \right)$$

Here, $\mu_0$ is your hunch about the mean, and $\sigma_0^2$ is your uncertainty in that hunch.

Completing the Square

Let's multiply the likelihood by the prior and group the terms inside the exponent:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\mu \mid X) &\propto P(X \mid \mu) \cdot P(\mu) \\[8pt] &\propto \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \frac{(\mu - \mu_0)^2}{\sigma_0^2} \right] \right) \end{aligned}$$

Let's expand the terms in the brackets and group by powers of $\mu$:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Exponent} &= \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^2 - 2x_i\mu + \mu^2) + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} (\mu^2 - 2\mu\mu_0 + \mu_0^2) \\[8pt] &= \mu^2 \left( \frac{n}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} \right) - 2\mu \left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2} \right) + \text{constant} \end{aligned}$$

We want this to match the exponent of a new Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu_n, \sigma_n^2)$, which has the form $\frac{\mu^2}{\sigma_n^2} - \frac{2\mu \mu_n}{\sigma_n^2}$.

1. Match the $\mu^2$ coefficient:

$$\mathbf{\frac{1}{\sigma_n^2} = \frac{n}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}}$$

2. Match the linear $\mu$ coefficient:

$$\frac{\mu_n}{\sigma_n^2} = \frac{n \hat{\mu}_{ML}}{\sigma^2} + \frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}$$

Multiplying by $\sigma_n^2$ gives the updated posterior mean:

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_n &= \frac{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}\hat{\mu}_{ML} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}\mu_0}{\frac{n}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}} \\[10pt] &= \mathbf{\frac{n \sigma_0^2 \hat{\mu}_{ML} + \sigma^2 \mu_0}{\sigma^2 + n \sigma_0^2}} \end{aligned}$$
Gaussian-Gaussian Updating

If the data is Gaussian with known variance $\sigma^2$, and the prior is $\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$, then the posterior is also Gaussian:

$$\mu \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_n, \sigma_n^2)$$

The posterior precision (precision is inverse variance, $\tau = 1/\sigma^2$) is the direct sum:

$$\tau_n = \tau_0 + n \tau_{\text{data}}$$

and the posterior mean $\mu_n$ is a precision-weighted average between your prior hunch $\mu_0$ and the sample mean $\hat{\mu}_{ML}$.

Effective "Ghost Samples"

To understand this formula physically, look at the ratio of measurement variance to prior variance:

$$n_0 = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_0^2}$$

Now substitute $n_0$ into the posterior formulas:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\mu}_{MAP} = \mu_n &= \mathbf{\frac{n \hat{\mu}_{ML} + n_0 \mu_0}{n + n_0}} \\[10pt] \sigma_n^2 &= \mathbf{\frac{\sigma^2}{n + n_0}} \end{aligned}$$

Look at $n_0$. It literally represents the effective number of "ghost samples" you held in your head before seeing any real data!

  • Your prior acts like $n_0$ virtual observations with average value $\mu_0$.
  • If your prior was very uncertain ($\sigma_0^2 \to \infty$), your ghost count $n_0 \to 0$. The estimate relies purely on real data: $\mu_n \to \hat{\mu}_{ML}$.
  • If your prior was absolute certainty ($\sigma_0^2 \to 0$), $n_0 \to \infty$. No amount of data will ever budge your estimate from $\mu_0$.

Connecting the Dots: The Estimation Spectrum

We now have a complete picture of single-distribution parameter estimation across both frequentist and Bayesian paradigms:

Method What is $\theta$? What does it compute? Small-Sample Behavior
Maximum Likelihood (MLE) Fixed, unknown number $\arg\max_\theta \log P(D \mid \theta)$ Overfits easily; prone to zero-frequency errors ($3/3 \implies 1.0$).
Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) Random variable with prior $\arg\max_\theta [\log P(D \mid \theta) + \log P(\theta)]$ Regularized by the prior hunch; beats MVUE when the hunch is sensible.
Full Bayesian Posterior Random variable with full distribution $P(\theta \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid \theta)P(\theta)}{\int P(D \mid \theta')P(\theta')d\theta'}$ Retains all uncertainty; avoids false certainty completely.

From Single Distributions to Ambiguous Mixtures

Notice the unspoken assumption behind every model we've built so far—whether MLE, MAP, or conjugate updating: we assumed every data point came from a single, known distribution whose source was directly observed. In coin flips, every toss was generated by the same coin. In Gaussian estimation, every measurement came from the same bell curve.

Earlier, when we studied clustering, we saw the need to group data into discrete prototypes. But $K$-Means forced a rigid, deterministic choice: every point belonged 100% to one cluster and 0% to all others.

When a data point could plausibly belong to multiple clusters—like an animal image with dog-like ears but cat-like paws—Bayesian estimation provides a principled way to represent this uncertainty: by assigning probabilities to candidate cluster assignments rather than making a purely deterministic choice.

The Hidden Barrier: Latent Variables

Every model we've built up to this point—whether MLE, MAP, or conjugate updating—shared one unspoken assumption: we assumed every data point came from a single, known distribution whose source was directly observed.

What happens in real machine learning problems when data emerges from a mixture of multiple sub-populations? For example:

  • Customer purchase histories from three different buyer personas (students, bargain hunters, luxury buyers).
  • Sensor signals from an engine operating across distinct modes (idle, cruise, heavy load).
  • Images of handwritten digits from 10 different number categories without any labels.

If you try to fit a single Gaussian to multimodal data, it smears across all the clusters and misses the true structure completely. But if you try to fit a mixture of Gaussians, you run into a fundamental chicken-and-egg dilemma: you don't know which data point came from which cluster. The cluster assignments are hidden, unobserved, and latent.

Even worse: when you write down the log-likelihood of a mixture, you get a logarithm outside a sum:

$$\ell(\Theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \left( \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k) \right)$$

You can no longer set the derivative to zero and solve cleanly in closed form.

"How do we estimate parameters and assign cluster probabilities when the cluster labels themselves are latent?"
