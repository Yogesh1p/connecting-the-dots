The Wall of Multimodality & Latent Variable Models

Suppose an observation sits right on the boundary between two clusters—like an image of an animal that has dog-like ears but cat-like paws.

When we studied $K$-Means, the algorithm made a rigid, deterministic choice: every data point was forced to belong 100% to one centroid and 0% to all others. It arbitrarily snapped boundary points to one side, completely discarding the real-world ambiguity of the data.

As we saw in the previous lesson, Bayesian estimation provides a principled way to represent this uncertainty: instead of making an arbitrary hard choice, we assign probabilities to the candidate clusters (e.g. $55\%$ Dog, $45\%$ Cat).

To do this, we need to move beyond single unimodal distributions and model the data as a mixture of multiple sub-populations:

If we force a single Gaussian onto multimodal data, the MLE algorithm behaves mechanically: it averages all observations together into a single global mean $\hat{\mu}$, and blows up the variance $\hat{\sigma}^2$ to stretch across the entire dataset. The result is catastrophic: the model predicts peak probability density in the vast empty valleys where virtually no data exists!

Introducing Latent Variables

To faithfully represent multimodal data, we imagine that nature operates via a two-stage hierarchical generative process:

  1. Latent Cluster Choice: For each data point $i$, nature rolls a $K$-sided die to choose a hidden cluster identity $z_i \in \{1, 2, \dots, K\}$ according to categorical mixing probabilities: $$P(z_i = k) = \pi_k, \quad \text{where } \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k = 1, \quad \pi_k \ge 0$$
  2. Conditional Emission: Once cluster $k$ is selected, nature draws the observed feature vector $x_i$ from that specific component distribution: $$x_i \mid (z_i = k) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$$

The variable $z_i$ is latent (hidden): we observe only the resulting coordinate vector $x_i$.

The Multivariate Gaussian Mixture Model in $\mathbb{R}^d$

In real-world applications, our data points are not single numbers; they are $d$-dimensional coordinate vectors $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

For each component $k$, the emission density is a multivariate normal distribution governed by a mean vector $\mu_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a symmetric positive-definite covariance matrix $\Sigma_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$:

$$\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\Sigma_k|^{1/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}(x - \mu_k)^T \Sigma_k^{-1} (x - \mu_k) \right)$$

By marginalizing (summing) over all possible discrete states of the latent indicator $z \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, the marginal density of an observation is:

$$\begin{aligned} p(x) &= \sum_{k=1}^K p(z = k) \, p(x \mid z = k) \\[6pt] &= \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k) \end{aligned}$$

Why Classical MLE Fails on Latent Variable Models

Now let us attempt to estimate the full parameter vector $\Theta = \{\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K, \mu_1, \dots, \mu_K, \Sigma_1, \dots, \Sigma_K\}$ using Fisher's Maximum Likelihood procedure.

We write the marginal log-likelihood for an observed sample $\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \ell(\Theta) &= \log \prod_{i=1}^N p(x_i; \Theta) \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^N \log \left( \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k) \right) \end{aligned}$$

Look closely at this equation. A mathematical disaster has occurred: the summation $\sum_{k=1}^K$ is trapped inside the logarithm $\log(\cdot)$!

In standard Gaussian MLE, the logarithm acted directly on the exponential function, vaporizing it: $\log \exp(-u^2) = -u^2$. But the logarithm of a sum is NOT the sum of logarithms: $\log(a + b) \ne \log a + \log b$!

If we attempt to differentiate $\ell(\Theta)$ directly with respect to $\mu_k$ using the chain rule:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu_k} &= \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sum_{\ell=1}^K \pi_\ell \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_\ell, \Sigma_\ell)} \cdot \pi_k \frac{\partial}{\partial \mu_k}\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k) \\[10pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^N \underbrace{\left[ \frac{\pi_k \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^K \pi_\ell \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_\ell, \Sigma_\ell)} \right]}_{\gamma_{ik}} \Sigma_k^{-1}(x_i - \mu_k) \\[10pt] &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Notice that the term in brackets—which we denote by $\gamma_{ik}$—is the posterior responsibility (the conditional probability that component $k$ generated point $x_i$).

Look at the circular dependency:

  • To solve for the optimal cluster mean $\mu_k$, we need the responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$.
  • To compute the responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$, we need the cluster mean $\mu_k$!

The parameters are completely entangled in a complex non-linear system with no closed-form analytical solution.

The Non-Convexity of Mixture Likelihoods

Worse still, single exponential families have strictly concave log-likelihood functions with a unique global maximum. But as soon as we construct a mixture of such distributions, concavity is completely destroyed:

The Geometric Core: Jensen's Inequality & The ELBO

How do we bypass the intractable sum inside the logarithm? The breakthrough comes from a foundational result in convex geometry: Jensen's Inequality.

Theorem: Jensen's Inequality

Let $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a strictly concave function (such as $g(u) = \log(u)$), and let $U$ be a random variable. Then:

$$\mathbb{E}[g(U)] \le g(\mathbb{E}[U])$$

For discrete probabilities $q_1, \dots, q_K$ with $\sum q_k = 1$ and $q_k \ge 0$:

$$\sum_{k=1}^K q_k \log(u_k) \le \log\left( \sum_{k=1}^K q_k u_k \right)$$

The Variational Decomposition: Deriving the ELBO

Now let us leverage Jensen's inequality to construct a tractable lower bound for the marginal log-likelihood $\log p(x; \Theta)$.

Let $q(z)$ be any arbitrary candidate probability distribution over the hidden states $z$. We multiply and divide inside the sum by $q(z)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \log p(x; \Theta) &= \log \sum_z p(x, z; \Theta) \\[8pt] &= \log \sum_z q(z) \left[ \frac{p(x, z; \Theta)}{q(z)} \right] \end{aligned}$$

Notice that the expression inside the logarithm is now an expectation with respect to $q(z)$:

$$\log p(x; \Theta) = \log \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\left[ \frac{p(x, z; \Theta)}{q(z)} \right]$$

Applying Jensen's inequality pushes the logarithm inside the expectation, flipping the equality into a universal lower bound:

$$\log p(x; \Theta) \ge \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\left[ \log \frac{p(x, z; \Theta)}{q(z)} \right] \equiv \mathbf{\operatorname{ELBO}(q, \Theta)}$$

This lower bound is the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO).

The Exact Decomposition & KL Divergence

To discover exactly how loose or tight this lower bound is, let us compute the difference $\log p(x; \Theta) - \operatorname{ELBO}(q, \Theta)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \log p(x; \Theta) - \operatorname{ELBO}(q, \Theta) &= \sum_z q(z) \log p(x; \Theta) - \sum_z q(z) \log \frac{p(x, z; \Theta)}{q(z)} \\[8pt] &= \sum_z q(z) \left[ \log p(x; \Theta) - \log \frac{p(z \mid x; \Theta) p(x; \Theta)}{q(z)} \right] \\[8pt] &= \sum_z q(z) \log \frac{q(z)}{p(z \mid x; \Theta)} = \mathbf{D_{\text{KL}}\Big( q(z) \;\parallel\; p(z \mid x; \Theta) \Big)} \end{aligned}$$
Theorem: The Fundamental Variational Identity
$$\mathbf{\log p(x; \Theta) = \operatorname{ELBO}(q, \Theta) + D_{\text{KL}}\Big( q(z) \;\parallel\; p(z \mid x; \Theta) \Big)}$$

Because the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is non-negative ($D_{\text{KL}} \ge 0$) and equals zero if and only if $q(z) = p(z \mid x; \Theta)$:

  1. $\operatorname{ELBO}(q, \Theta) \le \log p(x; \Theta)$ for any distribution $q$.
  2. The lower bound becomes an exact equality if and only if $q(z)$ is chosen to be the exact true posterior distribution $p(z \mid x; \Theta)$!

The Expectation-Maximization Algorithm

The variational decomposition reveals a stunning strategy: instead of optimizing the intractable log-likelihood $\ell(\Theta)$ directly, we perform coordinate ascent on the ELBO!

This two-step alternating coordinate ascent loop is the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm:

1. The E-Step (Expectation)

Fix the model parameters at their current values $\Theta^{(t)}$. Maximize the ELBO with respect to $q$:

$$q^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_q \operatorname{ELBO}(q, \Theta^{(t)})$$

Because $\log p(x; \Theta^{(t)})$ does not depend on $q$, maximizing the ELBO with respect to $q$ is identical to minimizing $D_{\text{KL}}(q(z) \parallel p(z \mid x; \Theta^{(t)}))$. The minimum of KL divergence is 0, which occurs when $q$ matches the true posterior:

$$\mathbf{q^{(t+1)}(z) = p(z \mid x; \Theta^{(t)})}$$

For a Gaussian Mixture Model, evaluating this posterior is simple Bayes' rule:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{\gamma_{ik}} &= p(z_i = k \mid x_i; \Theta^{(t)}) \\[8pt] &= \mathbf{\frac{\pi_k^{(t)} \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k^{(t)}, \Sigma_k^{(t)})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^K \pi_\ell^{(t)} \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_\ell^{(t)}, \Sigma_\ell^{(t)})}} \end{aligned}$$

Geometrical effect of the E-step: The lower bound curve is elevated until it touches the true log-likelihood function at the current parameter value $\Theta^{(t)}$ with zero gap!

2. The M-Step (Maximization)

Now hold the posterior distribution $q^{(t+1)}$ (the responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$) completely fixed. Maximize the ELBO with respect to the model parameters $\Theta$:

$$\begin{aligned} \Theta^{(t+1)} &= \arg\max_\Theta \operatorname{ELBO}(q^{(t+1)}, \Theta) \\[8pt] &= \arg\max_\Theta \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q^{(t+1)}}\big[ \log p(X, z; \Theta) \big] \end{aligned}$$

This expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood under the current responsibilities is known as the $Q$-function:

$$Q(\Theta \mid \Theta^{(t)}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^K \gamma_{ik} \Big[ \log \pi_k + \log \mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \Sigma_k) \Big]$$

Notice the breakthrough: the logarithm now acts directly on the individual Gaussian components without any inner summation! The parameters decouple cleanly into standard weighted statistics:

The M-Step Closed-Form Update Equations

Define the effective number of data points assigned to cluster $k$:

$$N_k = \sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik}$$

The closed-form parameter updates are:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{\pi_k^{(t+1)}} &= \frac{N_k}{N} \\[8pt] \mathbf{\mu_k^{(t+1)}} &= \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik} x_i \\[8pt] \mathbf{\Sigma_k^{(t+1)}} &= \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{i=1}^N \gamma_{ik} (x_i - \mu_k^{(t+1)})(x_i - \mu_k^{(t+1)})^T \end{aligned}$$

A Complete By-Hand Solved Example (Pencil & Paper)

Abstract formulas reveal the architecture of an algorithm, but true numerical intuition is cemented when you calculate an entire iteration step-by-step with a pencil and paper.

Let us execute one complete, rigorous iteration (Step 0 likelihood evaluation, E-step responsibility normalization, and M-step parameter updates) on a simple 1-D dataset.

Problem Setup & Initial State

Consider a dataset containing just $N = 3$ scalar data points:

$$X = \{x_1 = 1, \quad x_2 = 2, \quad x_3 = 5\}$$

We fit a $K = 2$ component Gaussian Mixture Model. Before seeing any data, we initialize our model parameters $\Theta^{(0)} = \{\pi_1, \pi_2, \mu_1, \mu_2, \sigma_1, \sigma_2\}$ to:

  • Equal Mixing Proportions: $\pi_1 = 0.5, \quad \pi_2 = 0.5$
  • Initial Cluster Means: $\mu_1 = 1.0, \quad \mu_2 = 5.0$
  • Initial Standard Deviations: $\sigma_1 = 1.0, \quad \sigma_2 = 1.0$ (unit variance $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = 1.0$)

The standard normal probability density function for a scalar component with mean $\mu_k$ and standard deviation $\sigma_k = 1$ is:

$$f_k(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}(x - \mu_k)^2 \right)$$

The prefactor constant is $c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.398942$.

Step 0: Evaluating Component Likelihoods $f_k(x_i)$

We evaluate the probability density of each data point under both Gaussian components:

  1. Data Point $x_1 = 1$:
    $$\begin{aligned} f_1(1) &= c \cdot \exp\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}(1 - 1)^2 \right) = c \cdot e^0 = c \approx \mathbf{0.398942} \\[6pt] f_2(1) &= c \cdot \exp\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}(1 - 5)^2 \right) = c \cdot e^{-8} \approx 0.398942 \times 0.000335 \approx \mathbf{0.000134} \end{aligned}$$
  2. Data Point $x_2 = 2$:
    $$\begin{aligned} f_1(2) &= c \cdot \exp\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}(2 - 1)^2 \right) = c \cdot e^{-0.5} \approx 0.398942 \times 0.606531 \approx \mathbf{0.241971} \\[6pt] f_2(2) &= c \cdot \exp\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}(2 - 5)^2 \right) = c \cdot e^{-4.5} \approx 0.398942 \times 0.011109 \approx \mathbf{0.004432} \end{aligned}$$
  3. Data Point $x_3 = 5$:
    $$\begin{aligned} f_1(5) &= c \cdot \exp\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}(5 - 1)^2 \right) = c \cdot e^{-8} \approx \mathbf{0.000134} \\[6pt] f_2(5) &= c \cdot \exp\left( -\tfrac{1}{2}(5 - 5)^2 \right) = c \cdot e^0 = c \approx \mathbf{0.398942} \end{aligned}$$

Step 1: The E-Step (Computing Posterior Responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$)

Using Bayes' rule, we calculate the responsibility $\gamma_{ik} = p(z_i = k \mid x_i; \Theta)$ that component $k$ generated point $x_i$:

$$\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k f_k(x_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^2 \pi_j f_j(x_i)}$$

Because $\pi_1 = \pi_2 = 0.5$, the prior weights cancel out, leaving the ratio of densities:

  • For $x_1 = 1$: Sum $S_1 = 0.5(0.398942) + 0.5(0.000134) = 0.199538$. $$\begin{aligned} \gamma_{11} &= \frac{0.199471}{0.199538} \approx \mathbf{0.999665} \\[6pt] \gamma_{12} &= \frac{0.000067}{0.199538} \approx \mathbf{0.000335} \end{aligned}$$
  • For $x_2 = 2$: Sum $S_2 = 0.5(0.241971) + 0.5(0.004432) = 0.123201$. $$\begin{aligned} \gamma_{21} &= \frac{0.120985}{0.123201} \approx \mathbf{0.982014} \\[6pt] \gamma_{22} &= \frac{0.002216}{0.123201} \approx \mathbf{0.017986} \end{aligned}$$
  • For $x_3 = 5$: Sum $S_3 = 0.5(0.000134) + 0.5(0.398942) = 0.199538$. $$\begin{aligned} \gamma_{31} &= \frac{0.000067}{0.199538} \approx \mathbf{0.000335} \\[6pt] \gamma_{32} &= \frac{0.199471}{0.199538} \approx \mathbf{0.999665} \end{aligned}$$

Step 2: The M-Step (Closed-Form Parameter Updates)

With responsibilities fixed, we recalculate our parameters using the analytical MLE formulas derived earlier.

1. Effective Cluster Sample Sizes ($N_k$)

$$\begin{aligned} N_1 &= \sum_{i=1}^3 \gamma_{i1} = 0.999665 + 0.982014 + 0.000335 = \mathbf{1.982014} \\[6pt] N_2 &= \sum_{i=1}^3 \gamma_{i2} = 0.000335 + 0.017986 + 0.999665 = \mathbf{1.017986} \end{aligned}$$

Notice that $N_1 + N_2 = 1.982014 + 1.017986 = 3.0 = N$, confirming that total statistical mass is strictly conserved.

2. Updated Mixing Proportions ($\pi_k^{\text{new}}$)

$$\begin{aligned} \pi_1^{\text{new}} &= \frac{N_1}{N} = \frac{1.982014}{3} \approx \mathbf{0.660671} \quad (66.1\%) \\[6pt] \pi_2^{\text{new}} &= \frac{N_2}{N} = \frac{1.017986}{3} \approx \mathbf{0.339329} \quad (33.9\%) \end{aligned}$$

3. Updated Cluster Means ($\mu_k^{\text{new}}$)

For Cluster 1:

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_1^{\text{new}} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^3 \gamma_{i1} x_i}{N_1} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.999665(1) + 0.982014(2) + 0.000335(5)}{1.982014} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.999665 + 1.964028 + 0.001675}{1.982014} = \frac{2.965368}{1.982014} \approx \mathbf{1.496139} \end{aligned}$$

For Cluster 2:

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_2^{\text{new}} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^3 \gamma_{i2} x_i}{N_2} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.000335(1) + 0.017986(2) + 0.999665(5)}{1.017986} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.000335 + 0.035972 + 4.998325}{1.017986} = \frac{5.034632}{1.017986} \approx \mathbf{4.945677} \end{aligned}$$

4. Updated Variances and Standard Deviations ($\sigma_k^{2\,\text{new}}$)

For Cluster 1 ($\mu_1^{\text{new}} \approx 1.496139$):

$$\begin{aligned} i=1: \quad &0.999665 \times (1 - 1.496139)^2 = 0.999665 \times 0.246154 \approx 0.246072 \\[4pt] i=2: \quad &0.982014 \times (2 - 1.496139)^2 = 0.982014 \times 0.253876 \approx 0.249310 \\[4pt] i=3: \quad &0.000335 \times (5 - 1.496139)^2 = 0.000335 \times 12.277041 \approx 0.004113 \\[6pt] \sigma_1^{2\,\text{new}} &= \frac{0.246072 + 0.249310 + 0.004113}{1.982014} = \frac{0.499495}{1.982014} \approx \mathbf{0.252014} \\[6pt] \sigma_1^{\text{new}} &= \sqrt{0.252014} \approx \mathbf{0.502010} \end{aligned}$$

For Cluster 2 ($\mu_2^{\text{new}} \approx 4.945677$):

$$\begin{aligned} i=1: \quad &0.000335 \times (1 - 4.945677)^2 = 0.000335 \times 15.568366 \approx 0.005215 \\[4pt] i=2: \quad &0.017986 \times (2 - 4.945677)^2 = 0.017986 \times 8.677013 \approx 0.156065 \\[4pt] i=3: \quad &0.999665 \times (5 - 4.945677)^2 = 0.999665 \times 0.002951 \approx 0.002950 \\[6pt] \sigma_2^{2\,\text{new}} &= \frac{0.005215 + 0.156065 + 0.002950}{1.017986} = \frac{0.164230}{1.017986} \approx \mathbf{0.161328} \\[6pt] \sigma_2^{\text{new}} &= \sqrt{0.161328} \approx \mathbf{0.401657} \end{aligned}$$

What Just Happened Geometrically?

Examine the leap taken in a single iteration:

  • Mean Adaptation: Mean $\mu_1$ moved from $1.0$ to $\approx 1.496$, shifting halfway between $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 2$ (the points it owns). Mean $\mu_2$ shifted minutely from $5.0$ to $\approx 4.946$, reflecting the tiny gravitational pull of $x_2$.
  • Mixing Weight Recalibration: $\pi_1$ expanded from $0.50$ to $0.661$ ($2$ out of $3$ data points belong to it), while $\pi_2$ dropped to $0.339$ ($1$ out of $3$ points).
  • Variance Contraction: Both standard deviations collapsed from the broad initial guess of $\sigma = 1.0$ down to $\sigma_1 \approx 0.50$ and $\sigma_2 \approx 0.40$, tightly hugging the dense clusters.

Without specifying a learning rate, without numerical instability, and without gradient overshoot, the model made an enormous leap toward the optimal clustering in a single closed-form step.

EM vs. Gradient Descent & The Bridge to Variational Autoencoders

A natural question arises: Why did we construct this entire mathematical apparatus of latent variables, Jensen's inequality, and alternating auxiliary bounds when we already possess universal optimization workhorses like Gradient Descent?

Can we not simply differentiate the marginal log-likelihood directly with backpropagation? Where does direct gradient ascent fail, where does EM triumph, and when does EM itself finally break down?

The Direct Gradient of Marginal Log-Likelihood

Recall our marginal log-likelihood objective:

$$\begin{aligned} \ell(\Theta) &= \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(x_i; \Theta) \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^N \log \left( \sum_z p(x_i, z; \Theta) \right) \end{aligned}$$

Applying the chain rule to differentiate with respect to any parameter $\Theta$:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\Theta \ell(\Theta) &= \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sum_{z'} p(x_i, z'; \Theta)} \sum_z \nabla_\Theta p(x_i, z; \Theta) \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_z \underbrace{\frac{p(x_i, z; \Theta)}{p(x_i; \Theta)}}_{p(z \mid x_i; \Theta)} \frac{\nabla_\Theta p(x_i, z; \Theta)}{p(x_i, z; \Theta)} \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_z p(z \mid x_i; \Theta) \, \nabla_\Theta \log p(x_i, z; \Theta) \end{aligned}$$

This gradient reveals a profound insight: the gradient of the marginal log-likelihood is the expected score of the complete-data log-likelihood under the posterior distribution $p(z \mid x_i; \Theta)$!

Furthermore, if we evaluate the gradient of our surrogate $Q$-function at the current parameter estimate $\Theta = \Theta^{(t)}$:

$$\left. \nabla_\Theta Q(\Theta \mid \Theta^{(t)}) \right|_{\Theta = \Theta^{(t)}} = \left. \nabla_\Theta \ell(\Theta) \right|_{\Theta = \Theta^{(t)}}$$

The surrogate $Q$-function has the exact same tangent slope as the true marginal log-likelihood at the current operating point!

Why EM is Vastly Superior for Classical Mixture Models

If their gradients point in the same direction, why not just run gradient ascent $\Theta^{(t+1)} = \Theta^{(t)} + \eta \nabla_\Theta \ell(\Theta)$?

  1. Complex Manifold Constraints: GMM parameters are subject to rigid geometric constraints: mixing weights must lie on the probability simplex ($\sum_k \pi_k = 1, \pi_k \ge 0$), and covariance matrices must remain symmetric positive-definite ($\Sigma_k \succ 0$). Gradient descent requires constrained projection steps or reparameterizations (such as softmax transformations for $\pi_k$ and Cholesky factors $\Sigma = L L^T$), which frequently destabilize optimization near boundary singularities. In contrast, EM's analytical M-step automatically satisfies every single constraint by construction.
  2. No Learning Rate Tuning ($\eta$): Gradient descent requires meticulous tuning of step sizes, decay schedules, and momentum. A learning rate that is too large causes variances to collapse to zero ($\sigma_k^2 \to 0$), driving the Gaussian density toward a Dirac delta singularity where likelihood explodes to $+\infty$. A learning rate that is too small crawls hopelessly across flat saddle regions. EM requires zero learning rates: every M-step makes an optimal, closed-form jump to the unconstrained peak of the surrogate.
  3. Natural Curvature Preconditioning: Standard gradient descent follows the steepest direction in arbitrary Euclidean coordinates. The M-step of EM implicitly preconditions the gradient by the inverse Fisher Information Matrix, performing what is mathematically equivalent to natural gradient ascent on Riemannian manifold geometry.
  4. Strict Monotonicity Guarantee: By the ELBO inequality, every iteration of EM is mathematically guaranteed never to decrease the likelihood: $\ell(\Theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \ell(\Theta^{(t)})$. Gradient descent can overshoot and degrade the objective.
Dimension Expectation-Maximization (EM) Gradient Descent (GD / Adam)

Step Computation Closed-form analytical equations for each parameter block; zero hyperparameter tuning. Requires learning rate $\eta$, momentum schedules, and gradient clipping.
Convergence Guarantee Strictly monotonic: $\ell(\Theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \ell(\Theta^{(t)})$ at every single step. Non-monotonic; susceptible to oscillations, overshooting, and divergence.
Per-Iteration Speed Heavier computation per step, but converges to high accuracy in very few iterations. Very fast per-step matrix multiplies, but requires hundreds or thousands of steps.

Scope of Applicability Strictly limited to models where posterior $p(z \mid x)$ and M-step are analytically tractable. Universal: operates on any differentiable loss function and arbitrary architectures.

When EM Breaks Down: The Intractable Posterior

If EM is so elegant, why does modern deep learning rely almost exclusively on gradient descent?

Because classical EM depends on an Achilles' heel: the exact posterior $p(z \mid x; \Theta)$ must be computable in closed form during the E-step.

In a Gaussian Mixture Model, the latent space is discrete with only $K$ possible states ($z \in \{1, \dots, K\}$). Computing the marginal evidence requires summing over only $K$ terms:

$$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$$

Now suppose our latent representation is continuous and multi-dimensional: $z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$ (e.g. a 128-dimensional latent vector representing the semantics of an image), and the likelihood $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ is generated by passing $z$ through a deep non-linear neural network (a "Decoder").

To compute the posterior $p(z \mid x)$, Bayes' rule forces us to evaluate the integral across the continuous latent space:

$$p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z) p(z)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_z}} p_\theta(x \mid z') p(z') \, dz'}$$

Because $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ is parameterized by non-linear neural activations, this integral is analytically intractable! There is no closed-form solution. We cannot calculate $p(z \mid x)$, meaning the exact E-step cannot be executed. Classical Expectation-Maximization collapses completely.

The Modern Bridge: Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

How did modern machine learning solve this dilemma? By uniting EM's Variational ELBO with deep neural networks through Variational Autoencoders (Kingma & Welling, 2013).

Instead of calculating the intractable exact posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ in an analytical E-step, VAE introduces a second neural network—the Inference Network or Encoder $q_\phi(z \mid x)$, parameterized by weights $\phi$:

$$q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}\Big(z; \, \mu_\phi(x), \, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x))\Big)$$

Recall our exact variational decomposition from Section 4:

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \operatorname{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x) + D_{\text{KL}}\Big( q_\phi(z \mid x) \;\parallel\; p_\theta(z \mid x) \Big)$$

Expanding the ELBO reveals the celebrated two-part loss function of the VAE:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{ELBO}(\theta, \phi; x) &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\big[ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \big]}_{\text{Reconstruction Quality (Decoder)}} \\[10pt] &\quad - \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}\Big( q_\phi(z \mid x) \;\parallel\; p(z) \Big)}_{\text{Latent Regularization to Prior (Encoder)}} \end{aligned}$$

Notice the direct conceptual lineage:

  • The VAE Encoder ($q_\phi$) is an Amortized E-Step: Instead of recalculating responsibilities from scratch for every single data point at every iteration, the encoder network learns a functional mapping that outputs the approximate posterior parameters $\mu_\phi(x)$ and $\sigma_\phi(x)$ in a single forward pass.
  • The VAE Decoder ($p_\theta$) is a Continuous M-Step: The decoder reconstructs data samples from latent vectors, driving up the expected log-likelihood.
  • The Reparameterization Trick Bypasses the Integral: To propagate gradients through stochastic sampling, VAE reparameterizes $z$ as a deterministic function with external Gaussian noise:
    $$z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$
    This enables standard backpropagation to compute $\nabla_\phi \operatorname{ELBO}$ and $\nabla_\theta \operatorname{ELBO}$ simultaneously using stochastic gradient ascent.
The Evolutionary Arc

Expectation-Maximization is the analytical ancestor of the Variational Autoencoder.

Where classical EM executes exact coordinate ascent on the ELBO using closed-form algebra for linear exponential families, the VAE executes stochastic gradient ascent on the ELBO using neural networks and the reparameterization trick for continuous, highly non-linear manifolds.

Connecting the Dots: The Grand Synthesis

We have traversed the complete arc of unsupervised learning: from deterministic spatial projections to latent probabilistic mixtures and modern deep variational architectures.

Now, look back across our entire journey. How does the probabilistic EM algorithm connect back to the geometric algorithms we studied earlier?

How GMM and EM Naturally Generalize $K$-Means

Consider what happens to the Expectation-Maximization algorithm under two specific physical constraints:

  1. All components share equal, isotropic variance: $\sigma_k^2 = \sigma^2$ for all $k$.
  2. We send the variance to zero: $\sigma^2 \to 0$.

Look at the posterior responsibilities computed during the E-step:

$$\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k \exp\left( -\frac{\|x_i - \mu_k\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)}{\sum_{\ell=1}^K \pi_\ell \exp\left( -\frac{\|x_i - \mu_\ell\|^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)}$$

As $\sigma^2 \to 0$, the exponential term with the smallest Euclidean distance $\|x_i - \mu_k\|^2$ dominates exponentially over all others. The responsibilities become binary step functions:

$$\lim_{\sigma^2 \to 0} \gamma_{ik} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } k = \arg\min_\ell \|x_i - \mu_\ell\|^2 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

This is identically the hard Voronoi nearest-centroid assignment of Lloyd's $K$-means algorithm!

And what does the M-step do with binary responsibilities $\gamma_{ik} \in \{0, 1\}$? It computes the unweighted sample average of all points assigned to cluster $k$: $\mu_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} x_i$, which is identically the centroid update of Lloyd's algorithm!

The Fundamental Unification

$K$-Means is the zero-variance limit of the Gaussian Mixture Model trained via Expectation-Maximization.

Where $K$-means cuts space into rigid, hard Voronoi hyperplanes, GMM endows every point with smooth, probabilistic posterior responsibilities, capturing cluster uncertainty, varying cluster scales, and elliptical orientations.

By recognizing that geometry is simply probability in the zero-variance limit, and that modern deep generative networks are simply amortized, gradient-based realizations of the classical Expectation-Maximization bound, we unite linear algebra, statistics, optimization, and deep learning into a single, majestic tapestry.
