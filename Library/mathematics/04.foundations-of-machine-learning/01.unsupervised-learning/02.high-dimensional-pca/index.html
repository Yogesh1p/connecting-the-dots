The Two Geometric Views of a Dataset

When we first learn Principal Component Analysis, we are taught to think of data in a very specific way: each observation is a point floating in a space whose axes are the measured features.

In this standard view, we compute the sample covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and search for directions $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ that maximize preserved variance. If two features (like the height and weight of an individual, or two neighboring pixels in an image) covary strongly, PCA rotates our coordinate axes to replace those redundant measurements with a single decorrelated principal axis.

However, any dataset consisting of $n$ observations across $d$ features arranged into a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ poses a deeper, fundamental question:

The Central Question of Representation

What determines what gets plotted as an axis, and what gets plotted as a point?

A matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ can be read in two completely symmetrical ways:

  • Features as Axes ($\mathbb{R}^d$): The $d$ features form the coordinate system, and the $n$ observations are points floating inside it.
  • Data Points as Axes ($\mathbb{R}^n$): The $n$ observations form the coordinate system, and the $d$ features are points (profile vectors) floating inside it.

To build an intuitive foundation for both spaces before touching high-dimensional abstractions, let us walk through a concrete example.

A Concrete Dataset: Real Estate Housing

Suppose we record $d = 2$ features across $n = 3$ residential properties:

Observation ($x_i$) Living Area ($F_1$, in $100\text{ sq ft}$) Market Price ($F_2$, in $\$100\text{k}$)
Property 1 ($x_1$) — Compact Urban Studio $8$ ($800\text{ sq ft}$) $1$ ($\$100\text{k}$)
Property 2 ($x_2$) — Suburban Townhouse $16$ ($1{,}600\text{ sq ft}$) $5$ ($\$500\text{k}$)
Property 3 ($x_3$) — Luxury Estate $18$ ($1{,}800\text{ sq ft}$) $6$ ($\$600\text{k}$)

Arranging these raw observations into our data matrix $X_{\text{raw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}$:

$$X_{\text{raw}} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 16 & 18 \\ 1 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

The dataset has a mean feature vector $\bar{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$. Subtracting the mean shifts the center of mass to the origin, giving our centered data matrix $X$:

$$\begin{aligned} X = X_{\text{raw}} - \bar{x}\mathbf{1}^T &= \begin{bmatrix} 8 - 14 & 16 - 14 & 18 - 14 \\ 1 - 4 & 5 - 4 & 6 - 4 \end{bmatrix} \\[6pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} -6 & 2 & 4 \\ -3 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3} \end{aligned}$$

Every property exhibits a genuine, non-zero market deviation from average. Now let us explore what happens when we plot this exact matrix $X$ through our two geometric perspectives.

Perspective 1: The Primal View (2D Feature Space $\mathbb{R}^2$)

In the primal view, our coordinate axes are the Features:

  • Horizontal Axis ($F_1$): Living Area deviation from average (in $100\text{ sq ft}$).
  • Vertical Axis ($F_2$): Market Price deviation from average (in $\$100\text{k}$).

The columns of $X$ represent our $n = 3$ property observations as points in this 2D plane:

$$x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Studio}), \qquad x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Townhouse}), \qquad x_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Estate})$$

What Does PCA Do in Feature Space?

Looking at Figure 1, the three property points fall on a single diagonal line. Larger houses cost more money. Because Living Area ($F_1$) and Market Price ($F_2$) are strongly correlated, maintaining two separate coordinate axes carries redundant information.

To find the optimal direction of maximum variance, we multiply the rows of $X$ by themselves, forming the $d \times d$ feature covariance matrix:

$$\begin{aligned} X X^T &= \begin{bmatrix} r_1 \cdot r_1 & r_1 \cdot r_2 \\ r_2 \cdot r_1 & r_2 \cdot r_2 \end{bmatrix} \\[6pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} 56 & 28 \\ 28 & 14 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2} \end{aligned}$$

The eigenvalues of $X X^T$ are $\lambda_1 = 70$ and $\lambda_2 = 0$. The leading unit eigenvector is:

$$w = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.894 \\ 0.447 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^2$$
The Primal Interpretation: A Recipe Over Features

The eigenvector $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a recipe over Features:

$$\text{Principal Axis } w = 0.894 \times (\text{Living Area}) + 0.447 \times (\text{Market Price})$$

Diagonalizing $X X^T$ uncorrelates the features. It compresses our two original measurements into a single "Size & Value Index" without discarding any genuine variance.

Perspective 2: The Dual View (3D Sample Space $\mathbb{R}^n$)

Now let us flip the entire formulation on its head. What happens if we treat the Observations as the coordinate axes?

  • Axis 1 ($S_1$): Property 1 (Compact Urban Studio)
  • Axis 2 ($S_2$): Property 2 (Suburban Townhouse)
  • Axis 3 ($S_3$): Property 3 (Luxury Estate)

Because we have $n = 3$ observations, this is a 3-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$).

In this 3D space, the points are the Feature Profiles (the rows of $X$):

$$r_1 = \begin{bmatrix} -6 \\ \phantom{-}2 \\ \phantom{-}4 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Living Area Profile}), \qquad r_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ \phantom{-}1 \\ \phantom{-}2 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Market Price Profile})$$

Each feature is now plotted as a single point in this 3-dimensional space, where its coordinate position along each axis reflects its deviation value for that specific property.

What Does PCA Do in Sample Space?

Look closely at Figure 2. In this 3-dimensional sample space, both feature points ($r_1$ and $r_2$) lie on the exact same straight line passing through the origin:

  • Living Area Profile ($r_1$): $\begin{bmatrix} -6 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}^T$
  • Market Price Profile ($r_2$): $\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}^T = \frac{1}{2} r_1$

Notice what this geometry reveals: across these three properties, the variation in living area is proportional to the variation in market price. All feature profile vectors point in the exact same direction in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Dual PCA finds this dominant direction in sample space. To find this principal axis, we construct the $n \times n$ Sample Gram Matrix ($X^T X$), which computes the pairwise dot products between our observation columns:

$$\begin{aligned} X^T X &= \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \cdot x_1 & x_1 \cdot x_2 & x_1 \cdot x_3 \\ x_2 \cdot x_1 & x_2 \cdot x_2 & x_2 \cdot x_3 \\ x_3 \cdot x_1 & x_3 \cdot x_2 & x_3 \cdot x_3 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] &= \begin{bmatrix} \phantom{-}45 & -15 & -30 \\ -15 & \phantom{-}5 & \phantom{-}10 \\ -30 & \phantom{-}10 & \phantom{-}20 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3} \end{aligned}$$

The eigenvalues of $X^T X$ are $\mu_1 = 70, \mu_2 = 0, \mu_3 = 0$. The non-zero eigenvalue is identically $70$, exactly matching $X X^T$!

The leading unit eigenvector of $X^T X$ is:

$$\vec{\beta} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{14}} \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ \phantom{-}1 \\ \phantom{-}2 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} -0.802 \\ \phantom{-}0.267 \\ \phantom{-}0.535 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$$
The Dual Interpretation: A Recipe Over Samples

The eigenvector $\vec{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a recipe over Samples:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Property Archetype } \vec{\beta} &= -0.802 \times (\text{Property 1}) + 0.267 \times (\text{Property 2}) \\ &\quad + 0.535 \times (\text{Property 3}) \end{aligned}$$

Diagonalizing $X^T X$ eliminates sample redundancy. All three properties actively participate in defining the dominant market archetype.

The Duality Bridge: Connecting Both Geometries

We have uncovered two completely different geometric spaces:

  1. The Primal Space ($\mathbb{R}^2$) produces an axis $w \in \mathbb{R}^2$ that blends features to uncorrelate measurements.
  2. The Dual Space ($\mathbb{R}^3$) produces an axis $\vec{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ that blends samples to uncorrelate observations.

Are these two formulations disconnected, or is there an exact mathematical bridge between them?

To turn the sample mixture $\vec{\beta}$ into the feature principal axis $w$, we scale $\vec{\beta}$ by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_1}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{70}}$:

$$\vec{\alpha} = \frac{\vec{\beta}}{\sqrt{70}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{980}} \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ \phantom{-}1 \\ \phantom{-}2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{14\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ \phantom{-}1 \\ \phantom{-}2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Now multiply the centered dataset $X$ by the dual weights $\vec{\alpha}$:

$$\begin{aligned} w = X \vec{\alpha} &= \begin{bmatrix} -6 & 2 & 4 \\ -3 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \left( \frac{1}{14\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ \phantom{-}1 \\ \phantom{-}2 \end{bmatrix} \right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{1}{14\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} 28 \\ 14 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

Multiplying the dataset by the sample recipe $\vec{\alpha}$ recovers the exact same principal component $w$ computed directly from the feature covariance matrix $X X^T$.

The High-Dimensional Breakthrough ($d \gg n$)

When sample observations are abundant and feature counts are modest ($n \gg d$), standard Primal PCA is computationally trivial. If we have $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ samples and $d = 50$ features:

  • The covariance matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{50 \times 50}$ requires only $2{,}500$ floating-point numbers ($\approx 10\text{ KB}$).
  • Eigendecomposing $C$ scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^3) = 50^3 = 125{,}000$ operations—finishing in under a millisecond.

In this classical regime, using Dual PCA would be absurd: it would require building a $1{,}000{,}000 \times 1{,}000{,}000$ Gram matrix.

However, modern computer vision, genomics, and signal processing invert this regime: $d \gg n$.

Case Study: The Extended Yale Face Dataset

Consider facial recognition on the Extended Yale Face Dataset. Each photograph is a grayscale image of size $192 \times 168$ pixels. Flattening each 2D image into a 1D vector gives:

$$d = 192 \times 168 = 32{,}256 \quad \text{features (pixels)}, \qquad n = 200 \quad \text{images}$$

Attempting standard Primal PCA on this dataset leads to immediate computational collapse:

  • Storage: The covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ has size $32{,}256 \times 32{,}256$ ($1{,}040{,}449{,}536$ entries), requiring over $4.16\text{ GB}$ of RAM just to hold in memory.
  • Compute: Eigendecomposing a $32{,}256 \times 32{,}256$ matrix scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^3) \approx (32{,}256)^3 \approx 3.35 \times 10^{13}$ operations—taking hours or crashing memory buffers.

The Subspace Dimension & Effective Rank ($n - 1$)

Consider what is happening geometrically. We have $n = 200$ data points situated inside a $32{,}256$-dimensional space.

Because the data is mean-centered, the sum of all centered sample vectors is zero ($\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \mathbf{0} \iff X \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{0}$). This introduces an exact linear dependency among the sample columns, constraining all $200$ points to lie within an at most $(n - 1) = 199$-dimensional linear subspace.

The remaining $32{,}057$ dimensions are pure null space where no data variance exists. Over $99.4\%$ of the eigenvalues of the $32{,}256 \times 32{,}256$ covariance matrix are identically zero. Computing $X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{200 \times 200}$ bypasses the null space entirely, solving PCA strictly within the $199$-dimensional subspace where variance actually lives.

Reconstructing Out-of-Distribution Data & Representation Capacity

When PCA is trained on face images, each leading principal component $w_k \in \mathbb{R}^{32,256}$ can be reshaped back into a $192 \times 168$ image, known as an Eigenface:

  • $w_1$ captures global illumination (lighting gradients across the canvas).
  • $w_2$ and $w_3$ capture coarse structural layout (eye spacing, nose bridge width, jawline).
  • Higher-order components capture localized edge transitions and high-frequency textures.

Any training face is reconstructed with high fidelity using only $k \approx 20\text{--}50$ leading components: $\text{Face} \approx \bar{x} + \sum_{j=1}^k c_j w_j$.

What happens if you project an out-of-distribution image—such as a dog—onto these eigenfaces?

If you retain only the first $k = 10$ components, the reconstructed image resembles a distorted human face. Because the top components capture variance patterns unique to human facial anatomy, they constrain the dog's pixels to human facial templates.

However, the eigenbasis $\{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_{n-1}\}$ spans an $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace of image patterns. As you increase $k$ and include higher-order components, they capture generic localized edges, line angles, and contrast boundaries common to natural images. With a large enough component budget, the representation resolves the dog's contours, ears, and snout.

The Principle of Representation Efficiency

PCA does not make out-of-distribution data impossible to represent—it makes it inefficient to represent:

  • In-distribution data (faces): Variance is concentrated in the top $k \ll n$ components. High-fidelity reconstruction requires only a few representations.
  • Out-of-distribution data (a dog): Variance is scattered across the long tail of higher-order components. It requires substantially more components to achieve acceptable reconstruction fidelity.

The Mathematical Derivation of Dual PCA

We now derive the algebraic equivalence that allows us to obtain exact principal components from the $n \times n$ Gram matrix for any high-dimensional dataset $X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$.

1. Outer Product Representation of Covariance

Let $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ be the centered data matrix. The sample covariance matrix is:

$$C = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T$$

By column-by-row matrix multiplication:

$$\begin{aligned} X X^T &= \begin{bmatrix} \mid & \dots & \mid \\ x_1 & \dots & x_n \\ \mid & \dots & \mid \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \text{---} & x_1^T & \text{---} \\ & \vdots & \\ \text{---} & x_n^T & \text{---} \end{bmatrix} = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T \\[10pt] &\implies C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T \end{aligned}$$

2. The Representer Property of PCA

The eigenvector equation for $C$ is:

$$C w_k = \lambda_k w_k \iff \left(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T\right) w_k = \lambda_k w_k$$

Using associativity of matrix multiplication, $x_i x_i^T w_k = x_i (x_i^T w_k)$. Because $x_i^T w_k$ is a scalar:

$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i (x_i^T w_k) = \lambda_k w_k$$

For any non-zero eigenvalue ($\lambda_k > 0$), dividing by $\lambda_k$ yields:

$$w_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \frac{x_i^T w_k}{n \lambda_k} \right) x_i$$

Setting the scalar dual coefficient $\alpha_{ki} = \frac{x_i^T w_k}{n \lambda_k}$ and assembling $\vec{\alpha}_k = [\alpha_{k1}, \dots, \alpha_{kn}]^T \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

The Representer Property of PCA
$$w_k = X \vec{\alpha}_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} x_i$$

Every principal component $w_k$ corresponding to a non-zero eigenvalue is an exact linear combination of the training observations. The optimal projection directions lie entirely within $\operatorname{span}\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$.

3. Projecting Test Points Without Materializing $w_k$

For any query point $x_{\text{test}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, its projection coordinate onto $w_k$ is:

$$x_{\text{test}}^T w_k = x_{\text{test}}^T \left( \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} x_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} (x_{\text{test}}^T x_i)$$

We can compute the exact compressed coordinate of any query point using only the dual weights $\vec{\alpha}_k$ and pairwise inner products $(x_{\text{test}}^T x_i)$ with the training samples, without ever forming the $d$-dimensional vector $w_k$.

4. Deriving the Gram Matrix Eigenvalue Problem

Substitute $w_k = X \vec{\alpha}_k$ into the primal eigenvector equation $\frac{1}{n} X X^T w_k = \lambda_k w_k$:

$$\frac{1}{n} X X^T (X \vec{\alpha}_k) = \lambda_k (X \vec{\alpha}_k) \iff X X^T X \vec{\alpha}_k = n \lambda_k X \vec{\alpha}_k$$

Premultiplying both sides by $X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$:

$$(X^T X)(X^T X) \vec{\alpha}_k = n \lambda_k (X^T X) \vec{\alpha}_k$$

Defining the $n \times n$ Gram matrix $K = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ where $K_{ij} = x_i^T x_j$:

$$K^2 \vec{\alpha}_k = (n \lambda_k) K \vec{\alpha}_k$$

This relationship is satisfied if $\vec{\alpha}_k$ solves the standard $n \times n$ eigenvalue problem:

$$K \vec{\alpha}_k = (n \lambda_k) \vec{\alpha}_k$$

5. Dual Normalization

In standard PCA, the principal component must have unit Euclidean norm: $\|w_k\|^2 = w_k^T w_k = 1$. Expressed in terms of $\vec{\alpha}_k$:

$$w_k^T w_k = (X \vec{\alpha}_k)^T (X \vec{\alpha}_k) = \vec{\alpha}_k^T (X^T X) \vec{\alpha}_k = \vec{\alpha}_k^T K \vec{\alpha}_k = 1$$

A standard numerical eigensolver applied to $K$ produces eigenvectors $\vec{\beta}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with standard unit Euclidean norm ($\|\vec{\beta}_k\|^2 = \vec{\beta}_k^T \vec{\beta}_k = 1$), satisfying $K \vec{\beta}_k = \mu_k \vec{\beta}_k$ with $\mu_k = n \lambda_k$.

Evaluating the quadratic form for $\vec{\beta}_k$:

$$\vec{\beta}_k^T K \vec{\beta}_k = \vec{\beta}_k^T (\mu_k \vec{\beta}_k) = \mu_k (\vec{\beta}_k^T \vec{\beta}_k) = \mu_k = n \lambda_k$$

To ensure $\vec{\alpha}_k^T K \vec{\alpha}_k = 1$, we scale $\vec{\beta}_k$ by $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_k}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}}$:

The Dual Normalization Formula
$$\vec{\alpha}_k = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}} = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{\mu_k}}$$

where $\vec{\beta}_k$ is the standard unit eigenvector of $K$ ($\|\vec{\beta}_k\| = 1$) and $\mu_k = n \lambda_k$ is the corresponding eigenvalue.

Verification: Check that $\vec{\alpha}_k$ satisfies both constraints

Verifying the norm constraint:

$$\begin{aligned} \vec{\alpha}_k^T K \vec{\alpha}_k &= \left(\frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}}\right)^T K \left(\frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}}\right) \\[8pt] &= \frac{\vec{\beta}_k^T K \vec{\beta}_k}{n \lambda_k} = \frac{n \lambda_k}{n \lambda_k} = 1 \quad \checkmark \end{aligned}$$

Verifying the eigenvalue equation:

$$\begin{aligned} K \vec{\alpha}_k &= K \left(\frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}}\right) = \frac{K \vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}} \\[8pt] &= \frac{(n \lambda_k) \vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}} = (n \lambda_k) \vec{\alpha}_k \quad \checkmark \end{aligned}$$

The Procedure

Given a centered dataset $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$ with $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ where $d \gg n$:

  1. Calculate the Gram Matrix:
    $$K = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, \qquad K_{ij} = x_i^T x_j$$
  2. Eigendecompose $K$:
    $$K \to \{\vec{\beta}_1, \dots, \vec{\beta}_\ell\}, \quad \vec{\beta}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n, \quad \|\vec{\beta}_k\|^2 = 1, \quad n\lambda_1 \ge n\lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge n\lambda_\ell > 0$$
  3. Normalize Dual Vectors:
    $$\vec{\alpha}_k = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}} = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{\mu_k}}, \quad \vec{\alpha}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$$
  4. Recover Directions or Project Queries:
    $$\begin{aligned} \text{Component: } &\quad w_k = X \vec{\alpha}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d \quad (\|w_k\|^2 = 1) \\[6pt] \text{Projection: } &\quad x_{\text{test}}^T w_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} (x_{\text{test}}^T x_i) \end{aligned}$$

Key Advantages & The Gateway to Kernel PCA

1. Computational and Memory Scaling

By replacing the $d \times d$ covariance matrix with the $n \times n$ Gram matrix, eigendecomposition complexity drops from $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ to $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$.

For the Yale Face Dataset ($d = 32{,}256, n = 200$):

  • Primal PCA: Decomposing a $32{,}256 \times 32{,}256$ matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^3) \approx 3.35 \times 10^{13}$ operations and $4.16\text{ GB}$ of RAM.
  • Dual PCA: Forming $K$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d n^2) \approx 1.29 \times 10^9$ operations; decomposing $K$ takes $\mathcal{O}(n^3) \approx 8 \times 10^6$ operations ($<320\text{ KB}$ of RAM).

The eigensolver runs over $4{,}000{,}000\times$ faster, yielding an overall $26{,}000\times$ speedup while cutting memory by four orders of magnitude.

2. The Gateway to Kernel PCA

Notice that the entire dual formulation depends solely on inner products between data vectors: $x_i^T x_j$ and $x_{\text{test}}^T x_i$. At no point does the algorithm require individual feature coordinates.

This allows us to replace the standard dot product with a non-linear kernel function:

$$k(x_i, x_j) = \langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle$$

This direct bridge unlocks Kernel PCA, allowing us to perform dimensionality reduction in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces without ever computing high-dimensional feature coordinates explicitly.

Connection to the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

This duality is the direct reflection of the Thin SVD of the data matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$:

$$X = U \Sigma V^T$$

where $U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ contains the orthonormal left singular vectors, $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ contains the orthonormal right singular vectors, and $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r)$.

$$C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T = \frac{1}{n} U \Sigma^2 U^T \qquad \text{and} \qquad K = X^T X = V \Sigma^2 V^T$$

Both $X X^T$ and $X^T X$ share the exact same non-zero eigenvalues $\mu_k = \sigma_k^2 = n \lambda_k$:

  • Primal PCA directly computes the left singular vectors $U$ ($w_k = u_k$).
  • Dual PCA computes the right singular vectors $V$ ($\vec{\beta}_k = v_k$), from which left singular vectors are recovered via $w_k = X \vec{\alpha}_k = X \left(\frac{v_k}{\sigma_k}\right) = (U \Sigma V^T) \frac{v_k}{\sigma_k} = u_k$.
