The Matrix View: Orthogonal Coordinate Rotation

In the previous article, we discovered Principal Component Analysis vector-by-vector from first principles—grounded in geometric projections, constrained optimization via Lagrange multipliers, and Pythagorean variance conservation. We saw how finding the direction of greatest spread naturally led us to the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the sample covariance matrix.

Now, we take a profound step forward: looking at the entire operation through matrix algebra. How do computers actually perform PCA simultaneously across thousands of dimensions without laboriously searching for one component at a time? And how do matrix factorizations reveal the deeper geometric and computational structure of our data?

Geometrically, we are not changing or distorting the data cloud. We are simply rotating the coordinate system until the axes line up with the natural directions in which the data varies.

Assembling the Rotation Matrix

From our eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix $C$, we obtained $d$ orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_d$. Let us place these vectors as the columns of a matrix $V$:

$V = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & & | \\ v_1 & v_2 & \cdots & v_d \\ | & | & & | \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$

Because the eigenvectors are mutually perpendicular and normalized to unit length ($v_i^T v_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$, and $v_i^T v_i = 1$), the matrix $V$ satisfies:

$$\begin{aligned} V^T V &= I_d \\[6pt] V V^T &= I_d \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, $V$ is an orthogonal matrix ($V^{-1} = V^T$). Geometrically, multiplying by an orthogonal matrix performs a pure rigid coordinate rotation (and possible reflection) of $\mathbb{R}^d$—aligning our data along the axes of maximum variance without stretching, compressing, or shearing any distances.

Rotating the Data Cloud

Suppose our centered dataset of $n$ observations is stacked into an $n \times d$ matrix $X$ (where each row $x_i^T \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a centered sample). To express every data point in this new orthonormal coordinate system, we multiply by $V$:

The PCA Coordinate Transformation
$Z = X V$

The matrix $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ is the PCA-transformed dataset.

Let us inspect the rows and columns of $Z$:

$Z = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^T \\ x_2^T \\ \vdots \\ x_n^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} v_1 & v_2 & \cdots & v_d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^T v_1 & x_1^T v_2 & \cdots & x_1^T v_d \\ x_2^T v_1 & x_2^T v_2 & \cdots & x_2^T v_d \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_n^T v_1 & x_n^T v_2 & \cdots & x_n^T v_d \end{bmatrix}$

The $j$-th column of $Z$ contains the coordinates of every data point along the $j$-th principal component axis $v_j$. Each entry is a scalar projection onto a PCA axis.

Diagonalizing the Covariance Matrix

What is the sample covariance matrix of our transformed data $Z$? Let us calculate it directly:

$\begin{aligned} \text{Cov}(Z) &= \frac{1}{n} Z^T Z \\ &= \frac{1}{n} (X V)^T (X V) \\ &= \frac{1}{n} V^T X^T X V \\ &= V^T \left( \frac{1}{n} X^T X \right) V \\ &= V^T C V \end{aligned}$

Recall the fundamental matrix eigenvector equation: $C V = V \Lambda$, where $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d)$.

Multiplying both sides on the left by $V^T$ (since $V^T V = I_d$):

Diagonalization of the Covariance Matrix
$\text{Cov}(Z) = V^T C V = \Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_d \end{bmatrix}$

Because $\Lambda$ is strictly diagonal, the covariance between any two distinct coordinates $z_i$ and $z_j$ is zero:

$\text{Cov}(Z_i, Z_j) = 0 \qquad \text{for all } i \neq j$

The transformed features $Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_d$ are completely uncorrelated, and their individual variances are precisely the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_d$.

The SVD View: Factoring the Data Matrix Directly

Up to this point, we constructed PCA by first forming the covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X^T X$ and finding its eigenvectors. But there is an even more direct, purely matrix-algebraic way to view PCA: through the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the centered data matrix itself.

Any centered data matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ can be factored directly as:

$X = U \Sigma V^T$

where $U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ have orthonormal columns, and $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ contains the singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$ along its main diagonal.

Substitute this factorization directly into the sample covariance matrix:

$\begin{aligned} C &= \frac{1}{n} X^T X \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n} (U \Sigma V^T)^T (U \Sigma V^T) \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n} V \Sigma^T (U^T U) \Sigma V^T \\[6pt] &= V \left( \frac{\Sigma^T \Sigma}{n} \right) V^T \end{aligned}$

Because $U^T U = I_n$ and $\frac{\Sigma^T \Sigma}{n}$ is a diagonal matrix, this is the exact eigendecomposition $C = V \Lambda V^T$. This reveals the direct relationship between singular values and covariance eigenvalues:

$\lambda_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{n}$

The right singular vectors $v_i$ (the columns of $V$) are identically the principal component directions. The transformed coordinates $Z = XV$ follow immediately from the SVD:

$Z = X V = (U \Sigma V^T) V = U \Sigma$

The $i$-th column of $Z$ is simply $\sigma_i u_i$. The singular value $\sigma_i$ scales the column, while the left singular vector $u_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ contains the normalized coordinates of all $n$ samples along the $i$-th principal axis.

PCA as a Sum of Rank-One Matrices

Every matrix product can be expanded as a sum of outer products. Writing $X = U \Sigma V^T$ term-by-term decomposes the entire dataset into a sum of rank-one matrices:

$\begin{aligned} X &= \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T \\[6pt] &= \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^T + \dots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^T \end{aligned}$

Each term $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ is an independent, rank-one piece of the data:

  • $v_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the feature pattern (the direction in feature space).
  • $u_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the sample profile (how much each observation exhibits that pattern).
  • $\sigma_i$ is the amplitude, measuring the total spread carried by this component.

When we keep only the top $k$ principal components, our low-rank reconstruction is simply the truncated sum:

$X_k = \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$
The Eckart–Young–Mirsky Theorem

For any rank $k < r$, the truncated SVD matrix $X_k$ is the globally optimal rank-$k$ approximation to $X$. It minimizes the total squared reconstruction error over all possible rank-$k$ matrices $B$:

$\begin{aligned} \min_{\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k} \|X - B\|_F^2 &= \|X - X_k\|_F^2 \\[6pt] &= \sum_{i=k+1}^d \sigma_i^2 \\[6pt] &= n \sum_{i=k+1}^d \lambda_i \end{aligned}$

The total reconstruction error across the entire matrix is exactly $n$ times the sum of the discarded eigenvalues.

How Software Actually Computes PCA: The Computational Reality

In introductory linear algebra, we learn to solve eigenvalues by hand using the characteristic equation $\det(C - \lambda I) = 0$. But in production machine learning with thousands of dimensions, computing determinants or forming $X^T X$ directly is both mathematically impossible and numerically destructive.

Deep Dive: Why Characteristic Polynomials Fail & How Production Solvers Compute PCA

The Breakdown of the Characteristic Polynomial

Why does $\det(C - \lambda I) = 0$ fail in production? Three mathematical and computational barriers stand in the way:

  • The Abel–Ruffini Impossibility: By the famous Abel–Ruffini theorem, there is no general algebraic solution in radicals for polynomial equations of degree five or higher ($d \ge 5$). Closed-form formulas simply do not exist. All eigenvalue solutions for $d \ge 5$ must be approximated iteratively.
  • Wilkinson's Catastrophic Instability: Polynomial root-finding is one of the most notoriously ill-conditioned problems in numerical analysis. In floating-point arithmetic, changing a single coefficient of a high-degree polynomial by a minuscule amount—even by $10^{-14}$ due to machine roundoff—can perturb the calculated roots by orders of magnitude (a phenomenon famously demonstrated by James H. Wilkinson).
  • Combinatorial Determinant Explosion: Expanding $\det(C - \lambda I)$ naively requires $O(d!)$ operations. Even using Gaussian elimination to find polynomial coefficients takes $O(d^3)$ operations and leaves behind wildly unstable coefficients.

If computers never solve characteristic polynomials, how do libraries like NumPy, SciPy, LAPACK, and PyTorch actually compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors?

1. The Intuitive Engine: Power Iteration

The simplest and most intuitive way to extract an eigenvector without ever touching a determinant is the Power Method.

Suppose we pick an arbitrary, non-zero random vector $b_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Because the orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_d$ form a complete basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$, we can express $b_0$ as a linear combination of them:

$$b_0 = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_d v_d$$

Now, multiply $b_0$ by the covariance matrix $C$. Because $C v_i = \lambda_i v_i$, each eigenvector component is scaled by its corresponding eigenvalue:

$$\begin{aligned} C b_0 &= c_1 C v_1 + c_2 C v_2 + \dots + c_d C v_d \\[6pt] &= c_1 \lambda_1 v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2 v_2 + \dots + c_d \lambda_d v_d \end{aligned}$$

In every iteration $k$, the algorithm multiplies by $C$ and normalizes the vector by its Euclidean length $\|w\| = \sqrt{\sum w_i^2}$:

$$\begin{aligned} w &= C b_k \\[6pt] b_{k+1} &= \frac{w}{\|w\|} \end{aligned}$$

Notice a vital practical detail: Normalizing simply divides $w$ by its Euclidean length. You do not need to know $\lambda_1$ to run this loop!

Why does this converge? Because mathematically, repeating this multiplication $k$ times expands to:

$$\begin{aligned} C^k b_0 &= c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k v_2 + \dots + c_d \lambda_d^k v_d \\[8pt] &= \lambda_1^k \left[ c_1 v_1 + c_2 \left(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right)^k v_2 + \dots + c_d \left(\frac{\lambda_d}{\lambda_1}\right)^k v_d \right] \end{aligned}$$

Because $\lambda_1$ is the strictly dominant eigenvalue ($\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$), the ratio $(\lambda_j / \lambda_1)^k$ decays exponentially toward zero as $k$ increases:

$$\lim_{k \to \infty} \left(\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}\right)^k = 0 \quad (\text{for all } j \ge 2)$$

Every other eigenvector component dies out! The normalized vector converges directly onto the first principal component:

$$\lim_{k \to \infty} b_k = v_1$$

Its eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ is then discovered in a single dot product via the Rayleigh quotient:

$$\lambda_1 = \frac{v_1^T C v_1}{v_1^T v_1} = v_1^T C v_1$$

To find the second principal component $v_2$, we simply remove the influence of $v_1$ from the covariance matrix through Hotelling deflation:

$$C_{\text{deflated}} = C - \lambda_1 v_1 v_1^T$$

Running power iteration on $C_{\text{deflated}}$ converges to $v_2$, and repeating this deflation sequence extracts each successive principal component in descending order of variance.

2. The Dense Workhorse: The QR Algorithm with Shifts

While power iteration is conceptually intuitive, extracting all $d$ eigenvectors one-by-one via deflation accumulates numerical error. For dense symmetric matrices of moderate size ($d \le 5{,}000$), modern linear algebra packages (such as LAPACK's dsyev and NumPy's np.linalg.eigh) use Francis's shifted QR algorithm.

The QR algorithm does not calculate roots. Instead, it repeatedly factors the matrix into an orthogonal matrix $Q$ and an upper-triangular matrix $R$, and then multiplies them in reverse:

$$\begin{aligned} A_k &= Q_k R_k \\[6pt] A_{k+1} &= R_k Q_k = Q_k^T A_k Q_k \end{aligned}$$

Notice that $A_{k+1}$ is an orthogonal similarity transformation of $A_k$, which means it shares the exact same eigenvalues. With every iteration, the off-diagonal entries shrink toward zero at an astonishing cubic rate. After a handful of iterations, $A_k$ converges to a purely diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ containing all eigenvalues along its diagonal, while the accumulated product of transformations $Q_1 Q_2 \cdots Q_k$ directly yields the eigenvector matrix $V$!

3. High Dimensions & Truncated PCA: The Lanczos Iteration

In modern machine learning, datasets often feature tens of thousands of dimensions ($d = 50{,}000$). However, we almost never need all $50{,}000$ eigenvectors; we typically only want the top $k = 20$ or $k = 50$ principal components.

Factoring a $50{,}000 \times 50{,}000$ matrix using the full QR algorithm would cost $O(d^3) \approx 1.25 \times 10^{14}$ operations—taking hours or running out of memory.

Instead, algorithms like the Lanczos method (implemented in ARPACK and SciPy's scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh) construct a low-dimensional Krylov subspace:

$$\mathcal{K}_m(C, b) = \operatorname{span}\{b, Cb, C^2 b, \dots, C^{m-1} b\}$$

By projecting the gigantic matrix $C$ into an $m$-dimensional Krylov subspace (where $m \ll d$, often just $2k$ or $3k$), the problem is reduced to finding eigenvalues of a tiny tridiagonal matrix. Lanczos extracts the extreme largest eigenvalues and eigenvectors with pinpoint precision in $O(k \cdot d^2)$ operations, without ever computing or diagonalizing the rest of the matrix.

4. The Production Standard: SVD Directly on the Data Matrix $X$

Yet even with advanced iterative eigensolvers, production libraries like scikit-learn rarely form the covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X^T X$ at all. Instead, they compute the Singular Value Decomposition directly on the centered data matrix $X$.

The decisive reason is numerical stability. Forming the matrix product $X^T X$ squares the condition number:

$$\kappa(X^T X) = (\kappa(X))^2$$

In standard 64-bit IEEE floating-point arithmetic, numbers have roughly $16$ decimal digits of precision (machine epsilon $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 10^{-16}$). Consider the consequences:

  • If a dataset $X$ has a condition number of $\kappa(X) = 10^4$, the covariance matrix $X^T X$ has $\kappa(X^T X) = 10^8$, losing half of its available precision.
  • If $X$ has $\kappa(X) = 10^8$ (common in datasets with collinear features or varying units), then $\kappa(X^T X) = 10^{16}$. At this point, the matrix product wipes out all precision—the smaller singular values (which represent minor variance directions) are entirely rounded down to zero and lost in machine noise.

Production engines (like LAPACK's dgesdd, which powers scikit-learn's PCA) bypass $X^T X$ entirely using a bidiagonal divide-and-conquer SVD. They apply Householder reflections directly to $X$ to reduce it to a bidiagonal matrix, and then recursively diagonalize it. This guarantees that singular values and principal directions are calculated with full floating-point precision, even for components with minuscule variances.

Two Matrix Conventions: Columns vs Rows

In machine learning and linear algebra literature, you will encounter two matrix conventions. Both describe the exact same mathematics:

The Two Matrix Conventions
Concept Samples as Columns ($X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$) Samples as Rows ($X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$)
Feature Covariance ($C$) $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ $C = \frac{1}{n} X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$
Sample Gram Matrix ($K$) $K = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ $K = X X^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$
Projected Data $Y = V^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}$ $Z = X V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$

Where This Leads

Standard PCA provides an optimal linear compression, but real datasets present two immediate challenges:

  1. High Dimensions ($d \gg n$): When features greatly outnumber samples (such as $10{,}000$ pixels across $500$ photos), the $d \times d$ covariance matrix is massive and rank-deficient. Because $n$ samples span at most $n$ dimensions, we can invert the problem to an $n \times n$ matrix of sample comparisons to compute the exact same principal components far more efficiently.
  2. Non-Linear Patterns: PCA only finds flat hyperplanes of spread. When data lies along curved structures—such as concentric rings—linear projection collapses the inner and outer circles on top of each other, destroying the cluster structure entirely (Figure 2).

Overcoming this non-linear breakdown leads to Kernel PCA: lifting data into a higher-dimensional feature space where curved manifolds straighten out into flat hyperplanes.

Connecting the Dots: The Grand Synthesis

Principal Component Analysis is often presented as an isolated machine learning recipe. But from first principles, it is the profound intersection where Euclidean geometry, linear algebra, and constrained optimization converge into a single unified theory:

The Four Lenses of Principal Component Analysis
Perspective Core Question Foundational Mechanism Linear Algebra Connection
Geometric Projection How do we compress a point onto a line or hyperplane with minimal loss? Pythagorean conservation: preserved variance plus reconstruction error equals total data energy. Maximizing variance minimizes squared error. Orthogonal Projections ensure error vectors are perpendicular to the representation subspace.
Matrix Operator & Spectral Theory Which axes naturally capture the spread of the data cloud? The covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ is symmetric ($C^T = C$) and positive semidefinite ($w^T C w \ge 0$). The Spectral Theorem guarantees an orthonormal eigenbasis with non-negative real eigenvalues that literally equal the variance along each axis.
Constrained Optimization Can any oblique or hybrid direction retain more variance than the eigenvectors? Maximizing the Rayleigh quotient $w^T C w$ subject to $w^T w = 1$ via Lagrange multipliers forces $C w = \lambda w$. Quadratic Forms prove the stationary points of unit ellipsoids are precisely the eigenvectors, ordered by eigenvalue magnitude.
Decoupling & Factorization How do we eliminate cross-feature redundancy and compute components stably? Rotating coordinates via $Y = W^T X$ completely diagonalizes covariance ($C_Y = \Lambda$), eliminating linear cross-talk. Singular Value Decomposition factors $X = U \Sigma V^T$ directly without squaring the condition number.

Notice how every concept connects to the mathematical foundations we built:

  • We did not invent orthogonality: Nature and matrix symmetry gave it to us. Because the covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ is symmetric, the eigenvectors are guaranteed to be mutually perpendicular without any ad-hoc constraints.
  • We did not guess the ranking of components: Because $C$ is positive semidefinite, its eigenvalues are real and non-negative, and their magnitudes literally equal the variance along each eigenvector axis ($\operatorname{Var}(w_i^T x) = \lambda_i$). Sorting the eigenvalues from largest to smallest ($\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$) naturally arranges our principal components in descending order of informational importance.
  • Linearity is both our power and our limitation: When data lies on or near a flat hyperplane, PCA achieves the theoretical optimum for linear dimensionality reduction. But when the underlying manifold curves or loops—as in concentric rings—linear projection reaches its geometric limit.

This brings us directly to the next frontier: What if we lift our data into a higher-dimensional feature space where non-linear curves straighten into hyperplanes, and what if we can evaluate inner products there without ever calculating the high-dimensional coordinates? That breakthrough is the Kernel Trick, and its application to representation learning is Kernel PCA.
