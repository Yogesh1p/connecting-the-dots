The Quest to Eliminate Redundant Information

The entire foundation upon which we have built Principal Component Analysis is the pursuit of eliminating redundant information.

When you collect measurements from the real world—sensor readings, physical dimensions, pixel brightness, or financial indicators—the raw features are rarely independent. When one feature increases, another might consistently increase or decrease alongside it. There is an internal relation tying them together.

If two features mirror each other, recording both is wasteful. To compress the data without losing its essence, we want to transform our original coordinate system into a new set of features where every newly formed axis captures information that is completely independent from the others.

To find these independent axes, standard linear PCA relies on covariance—which is simply the algebraic measure of collinearity.

These are not two separate mechanisms; they are the geometric and algebraic faces of the exact same concept:

  • Collinearity is the geometric concept: Two vectors are collinear if they lie along the exact same straight line in sample space—one vector is simply a scalar multiple of the other ($v_2 = c \cdot v_1$).
  • Covariance is the algebraic tool: To quantify whether two centered feature vectors $x_j$ and $x_k$ are collinear, linear algebra computes their inner product across all $n$ samples:
    $$\text{Cov}(x_j, x_k) = \frac{1}{n} \langle x_j, x_k \rangle = \frac{1}{n} \|x_j\| \|x_k\| \cos \theta$$
    Normalizing by their individual magnitudes yields the Pearson correlation, which is literally the cosine of the angle $\theta$ between the two feature vectors in sample space:
    $$\text{Corr}(x_j, x_k) = \cos \theta = \frac{\langle x_j, x_k \rangle}{\|x_j\| \|x_k\|}$$

When two features are perfectly collinear, they point in the exact same (or exact opposite) direction ($\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$), yielding $|\cos \theta| = 1$. When they are orthogonal in sample space ($\theta = 90^\circ$), $\cos \theta = 0$ and their covariance is identically zero.

Therefore, computing the sample covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ and finding its eigenvectors is simply the process of rotating the coordinate axes until all off-diagonal collinearity is eliminated.

Deep Dive: Are Collinearity, Correlation, and Covariance the Same Thing?

It is very common in machine learning literature to see authors use collinearity, correlation, and covariance almost interchangeably, which can make them seem like competing or distinct mechanisms. In reality, they are simply three different perspectives on the exact same linear geometric phenomenon:

Terminology Perspective What It Conveys
Collinearity Geometric State Do two centered feature vectors lie along the exact same straight line in sample space? ($v_2 = c \cdot v_1$, meaning angle $\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$).
Correlation Normalized Metric The pure cosine of the angle between the vectors ($\cos \theta = \frac{\langle v_1, v_2 \rangle}{\|v_1\| \|v_2\|}$). It is dimensionless, bounded strictly between $-1$ and $+1$, and measures directional alignment independent of scale.
Covariance Unnormalized Metric The scaled inner product in sample space ($\frac{1}{n}\langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{n}\|v_1\|\|v_2\|\cos\theta$). It measures collinearity weighted by the actual physical spread (variances) of each feature.

The Key Takeaway: All three are strictly confined to straight-line relationships. None of them can detect whether one variable curves non-linearly into another ($Y = X^2$, $Y = \sin X$). When PCA diagonalizes the covariance matrix, it is doing nothing more than rotating the coordinate axes until all off-diagonal collinearity drops to zero.

The Breakdown: Redundancy Is Not Always Linear

Suppose we describe homes in a real estate market using three features:

  • Width ($w$)
  • Area ($A$)
  • Price ($p$)

Consider a simplified world where every house is built on a square foundation. If the width of a house is $w$, its floor area is:

$$A = w^2$$

Under this geometric rule, width and area are completely, deterministically dependent. If an algorithm knows the width, it already knows the floor area with absolute certainty. Storing both measurements is entirely redundant.

Yet, notice the mathematical obstruction: the relationship between width and area is quadratic and non-linear ($A = w^2$).

What does ordinary PCA do with this dataset? Standard PCA constructs new principal component axes as linear combinations of the original features:

$$z_1 = a_1 w + a_2 A + a_3 p$$

Because linear PCA is confined to finding straight hyperplanes of variance in the raw coordinate system, it attempts to fit a straight line across a parabolic curve. It cannot recognize that area is simply the square of width, treating them as separate dimensions of variation and failing to compress the redundant information.

This forces us to confront a fundamental question:

If we do not know ahead of time which features are dependent, and whether their relationship is linear, quadratic, cubic, or some other non-linear curve, how can an algorithm discover and eliminate this redundant information?

The Blind Spot of Covariance: Zero Correlation $\neq$ Independence

Here is the fundamental limitation of standard linear PCA:

A straight line is not the only relationship two variables can share.

Two variables can be deeply, deterministically dependent on each other, yet have zero linear correlation and zero covariance. Because the covariance formula only integrates linear co-movements, non-linear curves cancel out across symmetric distributions.

The Circle Experiment

Consider a simple 2D dataset where every observation lies on the circumference of a circle of radius $R$ centered at the origin:

$$f_1^2 + f_2^2 = R^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{cases} f_1 = R \cos \theta \\ f_2 = R \sin \theta \end{cases} \quad \text{for } \theta \in [0, 2\pi)$$

Are $f_1$ and $f_2$ independent? Absolutely not! If you know $f_1$, you know $f_2$ up to a sign ($f_2 = \pm \sqrt{R^2 - f_1^2}$). The dataset has only one degree of freedom: every point is uniquely identified by its angle $\theta$. It is fundamentally a 1-dimensional manifold curved inside a 2-dimensional plane.

Now let us see what standard linear PCA calculates:

  1. Rotational Symmetry: Because the points are distributed uniformly around the perimeter, for every positive coordinate there is an equal negative counterpart. The sample covariance is zero:
    $$\text{Cov}(f_1, f_2) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f_{i1} f_{i2} = 0$$
  2. Equal Variance: The spread along the horizontal axis equals the spread along the vertical axis:
    $$\text{Var}(f_1) = \text{Var}(f_2) = \frac{R^2}{2} = \sigma^2$$
  3. Eigendecomposition: The covariance matrix is a multiple of the identity:
    $$C = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \sigma^2$$

Because $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$, each component explains exactly $50\%$ of the variance. Linear PCA reports that the data is strictly 2-dimensional and achieves zero compression, completely blind to the rigid circular dependency between the variables!

Whether non-linear redundancy takes the form of an open curve like the housing parabola ($A = w^2$) or a closed symmetric manifold like the circle ($f_1^2 + f_2^2 = R^2$), standard linear PCA is fundamentally thwarted: it either produces heavy reconstruction errors along flat hyperplanes or fails to find any principal direction at all.

The Breakthrough: Higher-Order Feature Combinations

How can we enable linear methods to capture non-linear dependencies?

To capture quadratic, cubic, or higher-order dependencies between features, we face two possible paths:

  • Path A: Abandon linear algebra and attempt to invent brand-new, complex non-linear statistical measures that quantify curved co-movements directly. This forfeits all the clean geometric properties of symmetric matrices, orthogonal projections, and eigendecompositions.
  • Path B: Keep linear algebra intact, but expand the feature space into higher-degree polynomial combinations ($f_1^2, f_2^2, f_1 f_2, \dots$) where those curved dependencies manifest as standard linear relationships among the extended features.

Return to our real estate example:

$$A = w^2$$

In the original 3D feature space $[w, A, p]^T$, the relationship between width $w$ and area $A$ is quadratic.

However, suppose we expand our feature representation to include quadratic and cross-product terms:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= \begin{bmatrix} w \\ A \\ p \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3 \\[8pt] \phi(x) &= \begin{bmatrix} w & A & p & w^2 & A^2 & p^2 & wA & wp & Ap \end{bmatrix}^T \in \mathbb{R}^9 \end{aligned}$$

Look at what has happened to the relationship between area and width inside this 9-dimensional expanded space:

$$A - 1 \cdot (w^2) = 0$$

This is an exact, straight linear dependency between the fourth feature ($w^2$) and the second feature ($A$)!

In real-world machine learning, we are given arbitrary features $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d$. We do not know ahead of time which feature acts like "width", which acts like "area", or what complex polynomial links them.

Instead of hand-crafting specific formulas, we expand the feature space systematically! We define a non-linear mapping $\phi(x)$ that lifts our data into a higher-dimensional feature space containing polynomial powers and cross-products ($x_1^2, x_1 x_2, x_2^2, \dots$).

Conceptually, the dimensionality reduction pipeline becomes:

$$\begin{aligned} x &\;\longrightarrow\; \phi(x) \\[6pt] &\;\longrightarrow\; \text{PCA in Feature Space} \\[6pt] &\;\longrightarrow\; \text{Lower-Dimensional Representation} \end{aligned}$$

This is the foundational intuition behind Kernel PCA: perform PCA in a non-linear, higher-dimensional feature space where curved manifolds straighten out.

What Does PCA Do in Combination Space?

When non-linear dependencies turn into linear relationships among these extended features, PCA does what it does best: it forms linear combinations of the extended features to construct new orthogonal principal component axes that decorrelate the space and eliminate redundant information.

Let us see this in action on our circular dataset $f_1^2 + f_2^2 = R^2$.

Define the quadratic combination features $F_1 = f_1^2$ and $F_2 = f_2^2$. In terms of these combination features, the circle equation becomes:

$$F_1 + F_2 = R^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_2 = R^2 - F_1$$

Look at what just happened:

  • In the original 2D space $(f_1, f_2)$, the points formed a curved, non-linear circle with zero linear covariance ($\text{Cov}(f_1, f_2) = 0$).
  • In the combination feature space $(F_1, F_2)$, every single data point lies on a perfectly straight diagonal line with slope $-1$!

When we run PCA in this combination space:

  1. The first principal component axis ($\text{PC}_1$) aligns directly along the diagonal line, capturing $100\%$ of the variance.
  2. The second orthogonal axis ($\text{PC}_2$) points perpendicular to the line ($F_1 + F_2 = \text{constant}$), where the variance is identically zero.

PCA rotates the coordinate system in combination space, isolates the single true degree of freedom along $\text{PC}_1$, and discards $\text{PC}_2$ as redundant noise—achieving perfect dimensionality reduction!

The Computational Trap: The Curse of Explicit Combinations

If creating higher-order feature combinations turns curves into lines, why can't we simply transform every data point into $\phi(x)$ and run standard PCA on the resulting matrix?

The bottleneck is combinatorial explosion. If you have $d$ input features and construct all polynomial combinations up to degree $p$, the total number of features $D$ explodes rapidly:

$$D = \dim(\phi(x)) = \binom{d + p}{p}$$
  • For a modest dataset of $d = 100$ features with degree $p = 5$:
    $$D = \binom{105}{5} = \frac{105 \times 104 \times 103 \times 102 \times 101}{120} \approx \mathbf{96.5 \text{ million features}}$$
  • For smooth, infinitely rich feature maps (such as Gaussian Radial Basis Functions), the number of combination features is literally infinite ($D = \infty$).

You cannot compute, store, or calculate covariance matrices for 96 million combination features, let alone infinite dimensions. Explicitly generating combination features is impossible in practice.

The Dual Bridge & The Kernel Trick

How can we perform PCA in an infinite-dimensional feature space without ever calculating the infinite coordinates?

Here is where the Dual Representation of PCA provides the critical breakthrough. Recall what we discovered when solving PCA in the dual space:

  1. Finding the Principal Components: The dual coefficients $\vec{\alpha}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ are obtained by solving the $n \times n$ Gram matrix eigenvalue problem:
    $$K \vec{\alpha}_k = n \lambda_k \vec{\alpha}_k$$
    where each entry $K_{ij}$ is simply the inner product between two mapped data points:
    $$K_{ij} = \langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle = \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j)$$
  2. Projecting a New Point: To extract the $k$-th coordinate of any point $x$, we compute:
    $$\begin{aligned} y_k(x) &= \phi(x)^T w_k = \phi(x)^T \left( \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{kj} \phi(x_j) \right) \\[6pt] &= \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{kj} \left( \phi(x)^T \phi(x_j) \right) = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{kj} \langle \phi(x), \phi(x_j) \rangle \end{aligned}$$

Notice the astonishing simplicity: Dual PCA never needs the individual feature coordinates of $\phi(x)$! It only ever requires the scalar inner product between pairs of vectors: $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$.

Can we compute the inner product of all these high-dimensional combination features $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$ directly from our raw low-dimensional inputs $x$ and $x'$, without ever generating $\phi(x)$?

Algebraic Proof: Evaluating 6D Combinations in 2D

Let us test this on two 2D vectors $x = [f_1, f_2]^T$ and $x' = [g_1, g_2]^T$.

Consider this simple scalar operation computed purely in 2D:

$$k(x, x') = (x^T x' + 1)^2$$

Expand this scalar expression algebraically:

$$\begin{aligned} (x^T x' + 1)^2 &= (f_1 g_1 + f_2 g_2 + 1)^2 \\[6pt] &= (f_1 g_1)^2 + (f_2 g_2)^2 + 1^2 \\[6pt] &\quad\; + 2(f_1 g_1)(f_2 g_2) + 2(f_1 g_1)(1) + 2(f_2 g_2)(1) \\[6pt] &= f_1^2 g_1^2 + f_2^2 g_2^2 + 1 + 2 f_1 f_2 g_1 g_2 + 2 f_1 g_1 + 2 f_2 g_2 \end{aligned}$$

Now take the 6-dimensional feature vectors that contain all polynomial combinations:

$$\begin{aligned} \phi(x) &= \begin{bmatrix} f_1^2 \\ f_2^2 \\ 1 \\ \sqrt{2}f_1 f_2 \\ \sqrt{2}f_1 \\ \sqrt{2}f_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \\[12pt] \phi(x') &= \begin{bmatrix} g_1^2 \\ g_2^2 \\ 1 \\ \sqrt{2}g_1 g_2 \\ \sqrt{2}g_1 \\ \sqrt{2}g_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \end{aligned}$$

Take their direct Euclidean inner product $\phi(x)^T \phi(x')$ in $\mathbb{R}^6$:

$$\begin{aligned} \phi(x)^T \phi(x') &= (f_1^2)(g_1^2) + (f_2^2)(g_2^2) + (1)(1) \\[6pt] &\quad\; + (\sqrt{2}f_1 f_2)(\sqrt{2}g_1 g_2) + (\sqrt{2}f_1)(\sqrt{2}g_1) + (\sqrt{2}f_2)(\sqrt{2}g_2) \\[6pt] &= f_1^2 g_1^2 + f_2^2 g_2^2 + 1 + 2 f_1 f_2 g_1 g_2 + 2 f_1 g_1 + 2 f_2 g_2 \end{aligned}$$

The two expressions are identically equal! Evaluating $(x^T x' + 1)^2$ in 2D gives the exact inner product across the 6-dimensional combination space, with zero high-dimensional overhead.

The Kernel Trick

A function $k(x, x')$ that computes the inner product between two points in some higher-dimensional feature space directly from their original coordinates—$k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$—is called a kernel function.

Executing linear algorithms in high-dimensional or infinite-dimensional spaces purely through $k(x, x')$ without ever constructing the combination features explicitly is known as the Kernel Trick.

Standard Kernel Functions

  • Polynomial Kernel:
    $$k(x, x') = (x^T x' + c)^p$$
    Implicitly computes inner products across all polynomial feature combinations up to degree $p$.
  • Gaussian Radial Basis Function (RBF) Kernel:
    $$k(x, x') = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - x'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

    The RBF kernel implicitly maps data into an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. To see why, recall the Taylor series expansion:

    $$\begin{aligned} \exp\left(\frac{x^T x'}{\sigma^2}\right) &= \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{1}{m!} \left(\frac{x^T x'}{\sigma^2}\right)^m \\[6pt] &= 1 + \frac{x^T x'}{\sigma^2} + \frac{(x^T x')^2}{2!\sigma^4} + \frac{(x^T x')^3}{3!\sigma^6} + \dots \end{aligned}$$

    Because the exponential contains terms of every degree from $0$ to $\infty$, evaluating a single Gaussian function in $O(d)$ time calculates an inner product across infinite polynomial combinations!

Demystifying the Notation: What Are $x$ and $x'$?

Before constructing the full matrix, let us clarify a common source of confusion in mathematical literature: the symbols $x$ and $x'$.

Suppose our dataset contains $N$ complete observations: $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N$. Each observation $x_i$ is a vector containing all measured features for that particular sample. In our real estate example:

$$x_i = \begin{bmatrix} \text{width}_i \\ \text{area}_i \\ \text{price}_i \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3$$

When we write the kernel function as $K(x, x')$:

  • $x$ and $x'$ simply represent two distinct sample observations (for example, $x = x_i$ and $x' = x_j$).
  • The prime symbol on $x'$ does not mean differentiation! In calculus, $f'(x)$ is a derivative, but in geometry and machine learning, $x'$ is standard notation simply used to label a second vector without having to introduce another letter.

Therefore, the kernel evaluation $K(x_i, x_j)$ asks a direct question:

"How geometrically similar are observation $x_i$ and observation $x_j$ when mapped into the high-dimensional combination feature space?"

A Concrete Numerical Example: Constructing the Kernel Matrix $K$

To see the arithmetic in action with real numbers, let us build a concrete kernel matrix from scratch.

Suppose we inspect $N = 3$ houses, each described by $d = 3$ features: $\text{width}$ ($w$), $\text{area}$ ($A$), and $\text{price}$ ($p$). As established, each house has a square layout, so floor area is the square of width ($A = w^2$):

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{House 1: width}=1, \;\text{area}=1^2=1, \;\text{price}=1) \\[8pt] x_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{House 2: width}=2, \;\text{area}=2^2=4, \;\text{price}=2) \\[8pt] x_3 &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 9 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{House 3: width}=3, \;\text{area}=3^2=9, \;\text{price}=3) \end{aligned}$$

We choose the quadratic polynomial kernel ($p = 2$, $c = 1$):

$$K(x_i, x_j) = (x_i^T x_j + 1)^2$$

Let us evaluate this pairwise operation across our three observations:

Step 1: Compute Raw Input Inner Products $x_i^T x_j$

We calculate the Euclidean dot product between each pair of 3D vectors:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1^T x_1 &= (1)(1) + (1)(1) + (1)(1) = 3 \\[6pt] x_1^T x_2 &= (1)(2) + (1)(4) + (1)(2) = 2 + 4 + 2 = 8 \\[6pt] x_1^T x_3 &= (1)(3) + (1)(9) + (1)(3) = 3 + 9 + 3 = 15 \\[6pt] x_2^T x_2 &= (2)(2) + (4)(4) + (2)(2) = 4 + 16 + 4 = 24 \\[6pt] x_2^T x_3 &= (2)(3) + (4)(9) + (2)(3) = 6 + 36 + 6 = 48 \\[6pt] x_3^T x_3 &= (3)(3) + (9)(9) + (3)(3) = 9 + 81 + 9 = 99 \end{aligned}$$

Step 2: Apply the Kernel Function to Every Pair

We now plug each dot product into our kernel formula $K(x_i, x_j) = (x_i^T x_j + 1)^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} K_{11} &= K(x_1, x_1) = (3 + 1)^2 = 4^2 = 16 \\[6pt] K_{12} &= K(x_1, x_2) = (8 + 1)^2 = 9^2 = 81 \\[6pt] K_{13} &= K(x_1, x_3) = (15 + 1)^2 = 16^2 = 256 \\[6pt] K_{22} &= K(x_2, x_2) = (24 + 1)^2 = 25^2 = 625 \\[6pt] K_{23} &= K(x_2, x_3) = (48 + 1)^2 = 49^2 = 2401 \\[6pt] K_{33} &= K(x_3, x_3) = (99 + 1)^2 = 100^2 = 10{,}000 \end{aligned}$$

Because inner products are symmetric, $K_{21} = K_{12} = 81$, $K_{31} = K_{13} = 256$, and $K_{32} = K_{23} = 2401$.

Step 3: Construct the $N \times N$ Kernel Matrix

Arranging these evaluations into a grid yields the complete $3 \times 3$ Kernel Matrix (Gram Matrix) $K$:

$$K = \begin{bmatrix} K(x_1, x_1) & K(x_1, x_2) & K(x_1, x_3) \\[6pt] K(x_2, x_1) & K(x_2, x_2) & K(x_2, x_3) \\[6pt] K(x_3, x_1) & K(x_3, x_2) & K(x_3, x_3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 16 & 81 & 256 \\[6pt] 81 & 625 & 2401 \\[6pt] 256 & 2401 & 10{,}000 \end{bmatrix}$$

The Fundamental Dimensional Shift: $N \times N$ vs. $d \times d$

Notice something profound about this matrix:

  • In standard linear PCA, we construct a covariance matrix of size $d \times d$ (here $3 \times 3$, measuring relationships between features).
  • If we had explicitly expanded into degree-2 polynomial combinations, we would have had $D = \binom{3+2}{2} = 10$ combination features, creating a massive $10 \times 10$ covariance matrix.
  • In Kernel PCA, the kernel matrix $K$ is strictly $N \times N$ ($3 \times 3$, because we have $N = 3$ houses).

The matrix $K$ represents pairwise relationships between data points in the implicit feature space. Even if the feature mapping $\phi(x)$ has 10, 1 million, or infinitely many dimensions, the kernel matrix size remains locked at $N \times N$!

A Crucial Conceptual Correction: What Kernel PCA Actually Does

At this point, it is very tempting to make an intuitive leap and assume:

"Does Kernel PCA explicitly expand all polynomial terms, inspect which features are collinear (like finding $A = w^2$), manually delete the redundant features, and then perform PCA?"

No. That is not what Kernel PCA does.

Kernel PCA does not search through all individual combination features ($w^2, wA, A^2, \dots$), calculate cross-correlations between each pair, or prune away collinear terms one by one.

Instead, Kernel PCA operates on the entire geometric cloud at once:

  1. It defines an implicit high-dimensional feature space $\phi(x)$.
  2. It uses a kernel function $K(x_i, x_j) = \langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle$ to evaluate distances and angles between sample points in that space without ever constructing $\phi(x)$.
  3. It forms the $N \times N$ kernel matrix $K$ representing all pairwise geometric interactions.
  4. It centers this data cloud directly in Hilbert space ($K_c = H K H$).
  5. It solves the eigenvalue problem on $K_c$ to discover the orthogonal directions of maximum variance in the high-dimensional space.
  6. It projects the data points onto these dominant principal component directions.

The core philosophy is not: "Find every non-linear dependency and delete it."

The true breakthrough is: Change the representation of the data so that non-linear manifold structures become accessible to linear variance maximization, then perform PCA in that new representation to extract an optimal lower-dimensional encoding.

What Makes a Function a Valid Kernel?

What makes a formula $k(x, x')$ a valid kernel? Can we choose any arbitrary mathematical function?

Suppose you invent a function—for example, $k(x, x') = -(x^T x' + 1)^2$ or $k(x, x') = \sin(x - x')$. Can you simply plug it into Dual PCA and claim you are performing non-linear analysis in some higher-dimensional space?

The answer is no. A function $k(x, x')$ cannot be just any arbitrary formula. For the kernel trick to work, $k(x, x')$ must correspond to a genuine Euclidean inner product between vectors in an actual geometric vector space:

$$k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$$

In physical geometry, every valid vector space and inner product must obey two non-negotiable physical laws:

1. Symmetry: Order Cannot Change the Angle

In Euclidean space, the angle and dot product between vector $A$ and vector $B$ is identical to the dot product between vector $B$ and vector $A$:

$$\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle = \langle \phi(x'), \phi(x') \rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k(x, x') = k(x', x)$$

If a function produces different values when you swap its inputs ($k(x, x') \neq k(x', x)$), it cannot represent a geometric dot product.

2. Non-Negative Length: Vectors Cannot Have Imaginary Length

In physical reality, the length of any arrow in space must be a real, non-negative number. A vector cannot have negative squared length ($\|v\|^2 < 0$), because $\sqrt{\text{negative number}}$ would mean the vector has an imaginary length—which is geometrically impossible.

Now, consider our transformed data points $\{\phi(x_1), \phi(x_2), \dots, \phi(x_n)\}$ in feature space. Suppose we construct an arbitrary vector $v$ by taking a weighted linear combination of these points with real coefficients $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n$:

$$v = c_1 \phi(x_1) + c_2 \phi(x_2) + \dots + c_n \phi(x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \phi(x_i)$$

What is the squared length $\|v\|^2$ of this combined vector? By definition of the inner product:

$$\begin{aligned} \|v\|^2 &= \langle v, v \rangle \\[6pt] &= \left\langle \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \phi(x_i), \;\sum_{j=1}^n c_j \phi(x_j) \right\rangle \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n c_i c_j \underbrace{\langle \phi(x_i), \phi(x_j) \rangle}_{k(x_i, x_j) \,=\, K_{ij}} \\[8pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n c_i c_j K_{ij} = c^T K c \end{aligned}$$

Because the physical squared length of $v$ must be non-negative ($\|v\|^2 \geq 0$), this algebraic sum must satisfy:

$$c^T K c \geq 0 \quad \text{for every possible choice of coefficient vector } c \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

In linear algebra, a symmetric matrix where $c^T K c \geq 0$ for all non-zero vectors $c$ is precisely the definition of a Positive Semidefinite (PSD) Matrix!

This brings us to the famous theorem established by mathematician James Mercer in 1909:

Mercer's Theorem (Kernel Validity)

A continuous function $k(x, x')$ is a valid kernel corresponding to an inner product $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$ in some Hilbert space if and only if for every finite dataset of points $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, the resulting $n \times n$ Gram matrix $K$ (where $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$) satisfies two strict mathematical conditions:

  1. Symmetry: The matrix must equal its transpose ($K = K^T$), meaning $k(x_i, x_j) = k(x_j, x_i)$ for all points (reflecting order-invariance of geometric inner products).
  2. Positive Semidefiniteness (PSD): The matrix must satisfy the quadratic form condition:
    $$c^T K c \geq 0 \quad \text{for all } c \in \mathbb{R}^n$$
    or equivalently, all its eigenvalues must be non-negative ($\lambda_i \geq 0$), guaranteeing that every vector in the Hilbert space has a non-negative squared physical length ($\|v\|^2 \geq 0$).

Mercer's theorem gives us complete mathematical peace of mind: as long as $k(x, x')$ produces a PSD Gram matrix, a real geometric feature space $\phi(x)$ is guaranteed to exist, and we are guaranteed that no vectors in that space will ever have impossible, negative squared lengths.

Centering in Hilbert Space: The $HKH$ Discovery

We now encounter a critical mathematical obstacle.

PCA strictly requires the dataset to be centered at the origin: $\sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i) = \mathbf{0}$. If the data cloud is not centered, the first principal component simply points from the origin toward the center of mass rather than capturing the direction of maximum variance.

Here is the problem: even if your raw input data $x_i$ was centered in $\mathbb{R}^d$ ($\sum x_i = \mathbf{0}$), the combination features $\phi(x_i)$ in Hilbert space are almost never centered. For example, if $x$ has zero mean, squared features like $x^2$ are strictly positive, shifting the center of mass away from zero.

How can we center data points in an infinite-dimensional feature space when we cannot calculate or see the combination vectors $\phi(x_i)$?

Deriving the Centered Gram Matrix $K_c$

Let the true, unobserved centered feature vector for sample $i$ be:

$$\tilde{\phi}(x_i) = \phi(x_i) - \bar{\phi} = \phi(x_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r)$$

We want to compute the entry $(K_c)_{ij}$ at row $i$ and column $j$ of the centered kernel matrix, which is the inner product of these centered vectors:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = \langle \tilde{\phi}(x_i), \tilde{\phi}(x_j) \rangle = \tilde{\phi}(x_i)^T \tilde{\phi}(x_j)$$

Substitute the definition of centered vectors into this inner product:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = \left( \phi(x_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r) \right)^T \left( \phi(x_j) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_s) \right)$$

Notice that this has the familiar algebraic form $(A - B)^T (C - D)$. Expanding this product using standard distributive multiplication yields four distinct terms:

$$\begin{aligned} (A - B)^T (C - D) &= \underbrace{A^T C}_{\text{Term 1}} \;-\; \underbrace{A^T D}_{\text{Term 2}} \\[10pt] &\quad\; -\; \underbrace{B^T C}_{\text{Term 3}} \;+\; \underbrace{B^T D}_{\text{Term 4}} \end{aligned}$$

To understand what these four terms mean, recall how our observable $n \times n$ Gram matrix $K$ is indexed:

$$K = \begin{bmatrix} K_{11} & K_{12} & \dots & K_{1j} & \dots & K_{1n} \\ K_{21} & K_{22} & \dots & K_{2j} & \dots & K_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ K_{i1} & K_{i2} & \dots & K_{ij} & \dots & K_{in} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ K_{n1} & K_{n2} & \dots & K_{nj} & \dots & K_{nn} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{where } K_{\text{row}, \text{col}} = \phi(x_{\text{row}})^T \phi(x_{\text{col}})$$

In any matrix entry $K_{a, b}$, the first index $a$ is the row number, and the second index $b$ is the column number. Now look at each of the four terms:

  1. Term 1 ($A^T C$): The Original Point-to-Point Value
    $$\phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j) = K_{ij}$$
    This is simply the raw, uncentered kernel evaluation between sample $i$ and sample $j$—the single number at row $i$, column $j$ of our matrix $K$.
  2. Term 2 ($A^T D$): The Average of Row $i$
    $$\begin{aligned} \phi(x_i)^T \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_s) \right) &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_s) \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n K_{is} \\[6pt] &= \frac{K_{i1} + K_{i2} + \dots + K_{in}}{n} = \mu_i^{\text{row}} \end{aligned}$$
    Look at the indices: the row index is fixed at $i$, while the column index $s$ runs from $1$ to $n$. You are walking horizontally across row $i$ from left to right, adding up all $n$ entries in that row, and dividing by $n$. This is the arithmetic mean of row $i$ in $K$!
  3. Term 3 ($B^T C$): The Average of Column $j$
    $$\begin{aligned} \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r) \right)^T \phi(x_j) &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r)^T \phi(x_j) \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n K_{rj} \\[6pt] &= \frac{K_{1j} + K_{2j} + \dots + K_{nj}}{n} = \mu_j^{\text{col}} \end{aligned}$$
    Look at the indices: the column index is fixed at $j$, while the row index $r$ runs from $1$ to $n$. You are walking vertically down column $j$ from top to bottom, adding up all $n$ entries in that column, and dividing by $n$. This is the arithmetic mean of column $j$ in $K$!
  4. Term 4 ($B^T D$): The Grand Matrix Average
    $$\begin{aligned} \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r) \right)^T \left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_s) \right) &= \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_r)^T \phi(x_s) \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n K_{rs} \\[6pt] &= \mu^{\text{grand}} \end{aligned}$$
    Here, both $r$ and $s$ run from $1$ to $n$. You are summing every single entry across the entire $n \times n$ matrix $K$ and dividing by $n^2$. This is the overall grand average of the whole matrix $K$.

Putting all four pieces together, the centered kernel value is:

$$\begin{aligned} (K_c)_{ij} &= \underbrace{K_{ij}}_{\text{Original}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mu_i^{\text{row}}}_{\text{Row Average}} \\[10pt] &\quad\; -\; \underbrace{\mu_j^{\text{col}}}_{\text{Column Average}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mu^{\text{grand}}}_{\text{Grand Average}} \\[10pt] &= K_{ij} \;-\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n K_{is} \;-\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n K_{rj} \;+\; \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n K_{rs} \end{aligned}$$

Why do we add the grand average back at the end? Because when you subtract the row average and subtract the column average, the overall matrix mean gets subtracted twice! Adding it back once corrects for this double-counting—the exact same principle used in classical table double-centering.

From Scalar Arithmetic to the Matrix Formula: $K_c = H K H$

Instead of calculating row and column averages one entry at a time in a loop, linear algebra allows us to perform this entire centering operation across the whole $n \times n$ matrix in a single step using the centering matrix $H$:

$$H = I_n - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_n \mathbf{1}_n^T$$

where $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix and $\mathbf{1}_n$ is an $n \times 1$ column vector of ones. Look at how multiplying by $H$ behaves:

  • Multiplying on the Left ($H K$): $$(H K) = \left(I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T\right) K = K - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^T K)$$ Because $\mathbf{1}^T K$ computes the sum of each column, $\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}(\mathbf{1}^T K)$ creates a matrix where every row is the column means. Subtracting it subtracts the column averages from $K$.
  • Multiplying on the Right ($(H K) H$): Multiplying the result on the right by $\left(I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T\right)$ subtracts the row averages from each row.

Expanding the full matrix product $H K H$ algebraically reveals the exact same four terms:

$$\begin{aligned} H K H &= \left(I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T\right) K \left(I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T\right) \\[8pt] &= K \;-\; \frac{1}{n} K \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T \;-\; \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T K \;+\; \frac{1}{n^2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T K \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T \end{aligned}$$
The Centered Kernel Matrix
$$K_c = H K H$$

The matrix product $H K H$ centers the data in the unobservable, infinite-dimensional Hilbert space purely through $n \times n$ matrix multiplications on the observable Gram matrix $K$, without ever calculating the coordinates of $\phi(x)$.

Numerical Walkthrough: Centering the 3-House Kernel Matrix

Let us apply this centering operation directly to the $3 \times 3$ Kernel matrix $K$ we constructed for our housing dataset:

$$K = \begin{bmatrix} 16 & 81 & 256 \\[6pt] 81 & 625 & 2401 \\[6pt] 256 & 2401 & 10{,}000 \end{bmatrix}$$

We compute the four components required for double-centering:

  1. Row Averages ($\mu_i^{\text{row}}$):
    $$\begin{aligned} \mu_1^{\text{row}} &= \frac{16 + 81 + 256}{3} = \frac{353}{3} \approx 117.67 \\[6pt] \mu_2^{\text{row}} &= \frac{81 + 625 + 2401}{3} = \frac{3107}{3} \approx 1035.67 \\[6pt] \mu_3^{\text{row}} &= \frac{256 + 2401 + 10{,}000}{3} = \frac{12{,}657}{3} = 4219.00 \end{aligned}$$
  2. Column Averages ($\mu_j^{\text{col}}$): Because the kernel matrix is symmetric ($K = K^T$), column averages equal the corresponding row averages:
    $$\begin{aligned} \mu_1^{\text{col}} &= \mu_1^{\text{row}} \approx 117.67 \\[6pt] \mu_2^{\text{col}} &= \mu_2^{\text{row}} \approx 1035.67 \\[6pt] \mu_3^{\text{col}} &= \mu_3^{\text{row}} = 4219.00 \end{aligned}$$
  3. Grand Matrix Average ($\mu^{\text{grand}}$): Summing all 9 entries and dividing by $N^2 = 9$:
    $$\mu^{\text{grand}} = \frac{353 + 3107 + 12{,}657}{9} = \frac{16{,}117}{9} \approx 1790.78$$
  4. Evaluate Centered Entries $(K_c)_{ij} = K_{ij} - \mu_i^{\text{row}} - \mu_j^{\text{col}} + \mu^{\text{grand}}$:
    $$\begin{aligned} (K_c)_{11} &= 16 - 117.67 - 117.67 + 1790.78 \approx 1571.44 \\[6pt] (K_c)_{12} &= 81 - 117.67 - 1035.67 + 1790.78 \approx 718.44 \\[6pt] (K_c)_{13} &= 256 - 117.67 - 4219.00 + 1790.78 \approx -2289.89 \\[8pt] (K_c)_{22} &= 625 - 1035.67 - 1035.67 + 1790.78 \approx 344.44 \\[6pt] (K_c)_{23} &= 2401 - 1035.67 - 4219.00 + 1790.78 \approx -1062.89 \\[8pt] (K_c)_{33} &= 10{,}000 - 4219.00 - 4219.00 + 1790.78 \approx 3352.78 \end{aligned}$$

Putting these evaluations together gives the centered kernel matrix:

$$K_c \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1571.44 & 718.44 & -2289.89 \\[6pt] 718.44 & 344.44 & -1062.89 \\[6pt] -2289.89 & -1062.89 & 3352.78 \end{bmatrix}$$

Notice the verification check: sum across row 1:

$$1571.44 + 718.44 - 2289.89 = 0.00$$

Every single row and column of $K_c$ sums identically to zero! Without ever calculating or observing the 10-dimensional combination feature coordinates $\phi(x)$, our $3 \times 3$ sample data points have been centered at the origin of Hilbert space.

The Complete Kernel PCA Algorithm

Connecting all the pieces, we arrive at the complete, rigorous algorithm for non-linear dimensionality reduction:

  1. Compute the Uncentered Gram Matrix: Choose a valid Mercer kernel (such as Gaussian RBF) and compute the $n \times n$ matrix:
    $$K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$$
  2. Center the Kernel Matrix: Apply the double-sided projection:
    $$K_c = H K H = \left(I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T\right) K \left(I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T\right)$$
  3. Solve the Eigenvalue Problem: Perform eigendecomposition on $K_c$ to obtain eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_k > 0$ and unit eigenvectors $\vec{\beta}_1, \dots, \vec{\beta}_k$:
    $$K_c \vec{\beta}_k = \lambda_k \vec{\beta}_k \qquad \text{with} \quad \|\vec{\beta}_k\| = 1$$
  4. Normalize Dual Eigenvectors: To guarantee that the unobserved primal principal components $w_k$ have unit norm ($\|w_k\|^2 = 1$), scale each dual eigenvector:
    $$\vec{\alpha}_k = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{\lambda_k}}$$
  5. Project Unseen Query Points: To extract the $k$-th non-linear coordinate $y_k(x^*)$ of a new test point $x^*$:
    $$y_k(x^*) = \tilde{\phi}(x^*)^T w_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} \tilde{k}_i(x^*)$$
    where $\tilde{k}_i(x^*)$ is the centered kernel evaluation between query point $x^*$ and training sample $x_i$:
    $$\begin{aligned} \tilde{k}_i(x^*) &= k(x^*, x_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n k(x^*, x_j) \\[6pt] &\quad\; - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n K_{ri} + \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n K_{rs} \end{aligned}$$

Connecting the Dots: The Unified Landscape of PCA

Let us step back and appreciate the entire journey across the representations of Principal Component Analysis:

Method Central Matrix Matrix Size When to Use Core Geometric Breakthrough
Primal Linear PCA Sample Covariance $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ $d \times d$ $n \gg d$ (many samples, few features) Maximizing preserved projection variance is mathematically identical to minimizing reconstruction error.
Dual Linear PCA Linear Gram Matrix $K = X^T X$ $n \times n$ $d \gg n$ (high dimensions, images, genomics) Principal components must live in the span of the data ($w = X\alpha$), collapsing $O(d^3) \to O(n^3)$.
Kernel PCA Centered Gram Matrix $K_c = H K H$ $n \times n$ Non-linear manifolds (circles, Swiss rolls, spirals) Higher-order combinations turn non-linear relationships into flat linear manifolds; the Kernel Trick evaluates infinite combinations in $O(d)$.

Every breakthrough in this journey emerged from a single fundamental desire: to eliminate redundant information:

  • In standard Primal PCA, we eliminate linear redundancies among our raw features by diagonalizing the $d \times d$ covariance matrix.
  • In high dimensions or infinite combinations, computing a $D \times D$ covariance matrix is physically impossible ($D = \infty$).
  • In Kernel PCA, the dual representation rescues us: by computing scalar inner products through $k(x, x')$, we capture non-linear relationships across infinite combination dimensions using nothing more than an $n \times n$ sample matrix.

By uniting the Spectral Theorem, orthogonal projection matrices, and positive definiteness, linear algebra allows us to unravel complex non-linear geometry in infinite dimensions using pure $n \times n$ matrix arithmetic.
