Where We Left Off — Finding Order in High Dimensions

In our study of the Spectral Theorem, we uncovered a profound geometric truth: every symmetric matrix possesses a complete set of mutually perpendicular eigenvectors that decouple space into independent scaling axes. When you view a matrix through its eigenvectors, there is no cross-talk, no skewing, and no distortion—only pure, independent scaling along orthogonal directions.

Now, let us turn this powerful machinery toward the messiness of real-world data.

Suppose you collect measurements from a complex system: thousands of gene expressions, high-resolution camera pixels, or sensor feeds from an aircraft. Each data point arrives as a vector in an intimidating $d$-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^d$. But real physical phenomena rarely use all $d$ dimensions independently. Features correlate, sensors record overlapping echoes of the same underlying cause, and most directions in the space contain little more than random noise.

This brings us to the fundamental question of dimensionality reduction:

Can we rotate our perspective to discover the few, true orthogonal directions along which the data actually varies—and discard the rest without throwing away the underlying signal?

This is the purpose of Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

The One-Line PCA Mental Model

PCA rotates your coordinate axes to align perfectly with the directions of greatest spread. Because total variance is conserved under orthogonal projection, maximizing the variance you preserve is mathematically identical to minimizing the information you lose.

However, standard linear PCA has two primary limitations:

  1. High Dimensions ($d \gg n$): When each sample has $32{,}000$ features (e.g., image pixels) but we only have $200$ samples, standard PCA requires computing and diagonalizing a $32{,}000 \times 32{,}000$ matrix.
  2. Non-Linear Data: Linear PCA only finds flat lines and hyperplanes. If the data lies along a curved shape (such as a circle or spiral), linear PCA fails to capture it.

In this article, we will build PCA from geometric first principles, derive why variance maximization and error minimization are one and the same, and then address both limitations: first by discovering how to invert the dimensional bottleneck when $d \gg n$, and second by using inner products to linearize curved data in higher dimensions without high computational cost.

The Linear PCA Formulation

Let us start from scratch. We are given a dataset of $n$ observations $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$, where each data point $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a column vector of $d$ features.

Step 1: Centering the Cloud

Before looking for directions of spread, we must eliminate arbitrary coordinate offsets. We calculate the sample mean vector:

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$

and center every observation by shifting the center of mass to the origin: $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \bar{x}$. From this point onward, our dataset satisfies $\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \mathbf{0}$. With the mean at the origin, the squared length $\|x_i\|^2$ represents the pure variance of point $x_i$ from the center of the dataset.

Step 2: Projecting onto a Candidate Direction

Suppose we want to compress every $d$-dimensional vector down to a single number along a unit direction vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (with $\|w\| = 1$).

What is the shadow that $x_i$ casts onto the line spanned by $w$?

  • The scalar coordinate (length of the shadow) is the dot product: $x_i^T w$.
  • The vector projection (the reconstructed point on the line) is: $(x_i^T w)w$.
  • The error vector (the piece perpendicular to $w$ that gets thrown away) is: $e_i = x_i - (x_i^T w)w$.

Notice that the original point decomposes cleanly into two orthogonal components:

$$x_i = \underbrace{(x_i^T w)w}_{\text{preserved signal}} \;+\; \underbrace{\left(x_i - (x_i^T w)w\right)}_{\text{discarded error } e_i}$$

Step 3: The Pythagorean Conservation Law

Because the projection vector $(x_i^T w)w$ and the error vector $e_i$ are perpendicular by construction, the Pythagorean theorem holds exactly:

$$\|x_i\|^2 = \|(x_i^T w)w\|^2 + \|x_i - (x_i^T w)w\|^2 = (x_i^T w)^2 + \|x_i - (x_i^T w)w\|^2$$

Now, sum this equation across all $n$ data points and divide by $n$:

$$\underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2}_{\text{Total Variance of Dataset (Constant)}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^T w)^2}_{\text{Preserved Projection Variance}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - (x_i^T w)w\|^2}_{\text{Mean Squared Reconstruction Error}}$$

Look closely at what this equation is saying. The left-hand side is the total sum of squared distances of all data points from the origin—a constant value that depends only on the raw data, completely independent of our choice of $w$.

Because their sum is fixed, any increase in preserved variance forces an identical decrease in reconstruction error:

The Equivalence Theorem of PCA
$$\max_{\|w\|=1} \text{Var}(\text{Projections}) \;\iff\; \min_{\|w\|=1} \text{Reconstruction Error}$$

Maximizing the preserved projection variance is mathematically equivalent to minimizing the mean squared reconstruction error.

Step 4: From Variance to Eigendecomposition

Let us write the preserved variance in matrix form. For any centered data vector $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the projection scalar is $x_i^T w = w^T x_i$. Its square is:

$$(x_i^T w)^2 = (w^T x_i)(x_i^T w) = w^T (x_i x_i^T) w$$

Summing across all $n$ points and dividing by $n$:

$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^T w)^2 = w^T \left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T \right) w = w^T C w$$

where $C \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the sample covariance matrix:

$$C = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$$

Here, $X = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ is the data matrix whose columns are the centered data vectors. Because $C$ is a sum of symmetric rank-1 outer products $x_i x_i^T$, $C$ is symmetric ($C = C^T$) and positive semidefinite ($w^T C w \geq 0$).

To find the first principal component $w_1$, we maximize the preserved variance subject to $w$ being a unit vector:

$$\max_{w} \; w^T C w \quad \text{subject to} \quad w^T w = 1$$

We formulate the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = w^T C w - \lambda(w^T w - 1)$. Taking the gradient with respect to $w$ and setting it to zero:

$$\nabla_w \mathcal{L} = 2 C w - 2 \lambda w = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C w = \lambda w$$

Multiplying on the left by $w^T$:

$$w^T C w = w^T (\lambda w) = \lambda (w^T w) = \lambda$$

The variance captured along direction $w$ is precisely the eigenvalue $\lambda$. To maximize variance, we pick the eigenvector $w_1$ corresponding to the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = \lambda_{\max}(C)$.

To find the second component $w_2$, we maximize variance subject to unit norm ($w_2^T w_2 = 1$) and orthogonality to $w_1$ ($w_2^T w_1 = 0$):

$$\mathcal{L}(w_2, \lambda, \mu) = w_2^T C w_2 - \lambda(w_2^T w_2 - 1) - 2\mu (w_2^T w_1)$$

Setting the gradient with respect to $w_2$ to zero:

$$\nabla_{w_2} \mathcal{L} = 2 C w_2 - 2 \lambda w_2 - 2 \mu w_1 = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C w_2 = \lambda w_2 + \mu w_1$$

Multiplying on the left by $w_1^T$:

$$w_1^T C w_2 = \lambda (w_1^T w_2) + \mu (w_1^T w_1) = 0 + \mu(1) = \mu$$

Because $C$ is symmetric ($C = C^T$) and $C w_1 = \lambda_1 w_1$, the left-hand side is:

$$w_1^T C w_2 = (C w_1)^T w_2 = (\lambda_1 w_1)^T w_2 = \lambda_1 (w_1^T w_2) = 0$$

Therefore, $\mu = 0$, which reduces the equation back to $C w_2 = \lambda w_2$. The second principal component $w_2$ is simply the eigenvector corresponding to the second-largest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$.

By the Spectral Theorem, because $C$ is a real symmetric matrix, it possesses an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_d$ with real eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$. Writing any unit vector $w = \sum_{j=1}^d c_j w_j$ (with $\sum c_j^2 = 1$), the variance expands as:

$$w^T C w = \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j c_j^2 \;\le\; \lambda_1 \sum_{j=1}^d c_j^2 = \lambda_1$$

Equality holds if and only if $c_1 = 1$ and $c_{j > 1} = 0$, confirming that the principal directions of variance are exactly the orthogonal eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix $C$.

Step 5: Total Variance Conservation & "Explaining $X\%$ of Variance"

Now that we have established that the orthogonal eigenvectors $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_d$ are the principal directions of variance, we can understand how they partition the information in our dataset.

Since $w_1, \dots, w_d$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^d$, any centered data vector $x_i$ can be written exactly as a linear combination of these eigenvectors:

$$x_i = \sum_{k=1}^d (x_i^T w_k) w_k$$

Because these basis vectors are orthonormal ($w_j^T w_k = \delta_{jk}$), the Pythagorean theorem holds in $d$ dimensions:

$$\|x_i\|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^d (x_i^T w_k)^2$$

To find the average spread of our entire dataset, we sum this squared length across all $n$ data points and divide by $n$. By exchanging the order of summation:

$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^d (x_i^T w_k)^2 = \sum_{k=1}^d \left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^T w_k)^2 \right)$$

Notice that the term inside the parenthesis on the right is exactly the sample variance of our projected data points along the direction $w_k$, which is equal to $w_k^T C w_k = \lambda_k$. This gives us the Total Variance Conservation Law:

$$\text{Total Variance} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^d \lambda_k$$

This conservation law reveals that the total variance in the original coordinate system is completely preserved when we rotate our axes to the principal component coordinates—the sum of the variances along each eigenvector (eigenvalue) is exactly equal to the total variance of the raw features.

Decomposing Variance and Residue Iteratively

This allows us to view the dimensionality reduction process as an iterative decomposition of variance and residue. For instance, if we only project our data onto the first principal component $w_1$, the Pythagorean theorem yields:

$$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i^T w_1)^2 + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i - (x_i^T w_1) w_1\|^2$$

In words:

$$\text{Total Variance} = \text{variance along } w_1 + \text{mean squared residue along } w_1$$

Since the residue $x_i - (x_i^T w_1)w_1$ lives in the $(d-1)$-dimensional orthogonal complement of $w_1$, we can repeat this process and project the residue onto the second component $w_2$, stripping away another orthogonal variance layer:

$$\text{Mean squared residue along } w_1 = \text{variance along } w_2 + \text{mean squared residue along } w_2$$

Continuing this process down to $d$ dimensions yields the full decomposition:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \|x_i\|^2 &= \text{variance along } w_1 + \text{mean squared residue along } w_1 \\ &= \text{variance along } w_1 + \left(\text{variance along } w_2 + \text{mean squared residue along } w_2\right) \\ &= \dots \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^d \text{variance along } w_k \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^d \lambda_k \end{aligned}$$

Intuition: What does it mean to "explain $X\%$ of variance"?

When we compress our dataset by projecting it onto the first $m$ principal components ($m < d$), we retain only the variance along those $m$ directions. The rest is discarded as reconstruction error.

The fraction of the total dataset variance captured by these $m$ components is called the Explained Variance Ratio:

$$\text{Explained Variance Ratio} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^m \lambda_k}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j}$$

When a scientist or algorithm states that, "with this eigenface, we are explaining $X\%$ of the covariance/variance," it means that:

$$\frac{\lambda_1}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j} = \frac{X}{100}$$

Geometrically, it signifies that $X\%$ of the total squared distance of the centered data points from their mean is preserved along that single eigenvector axis, while the remaining $(100-X)\%$ of the spread lies in the perpendicular directions (residues) that we are choosing to discard.

The High-Dimensional Bottleneck ($d \gg n$)

The linear formulation is complete. But when the number of features $d$ is much larger than the number of data points $n$, standard PCA becomes computationally impractical.

Case Study: The Extended Yale Face Dataset

Consider the classic computer vision problem of facial recognition using the Yale Face Dataset. Each photograph is a grayscale image of size $192 \times 168$ pixels.

To feed these images into a linear model, we flatten each 2D image into a single 1D vector:

$$d = 192 \times 168 = 32{,}256 \quad \text{features (pixels)}, \qquad n = 200 \quad \text{images}$$

Let us calculate what standard PCA requires on this dataset:

  • Matrix Size: The covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ has size $d \times d = 32{,}256 \times 32{,}256$. That is $1{,}040{,}449{,}536$ floating-point entries—over $4 \text{ GB}$ of memory just to store the matrix.
  • Computational Cost: Finding the eigenvalues of a $d \times d$ matrix scales as $O(d^3)$. Here, $(32{,}256)^3 \approx 3.35 \times 10^{13}$ floating-point operations.

The Subspace Dimension

Consider what is happening geometrically. We have $n = 200$ data points in a $32{,}256$-dimensional space. Because the data has been mean-centered ($\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \mathbf{0}$), the $n$ vectors satisfy a linear dependency, meaning they can span at most an $(n - 1) = 199$-dimensional subspace.

In this $32{,}256$-dimensional space, all $200$ images lie inside an at most $199$-dimensional subspace. The remaining $32{,}057$ dimensions are pure null space where no variation occurs.

Because $99.38\% \approx 99.4\%$ of the eigenvalues of the $32{,}256 \times 32{,}256$ covariance matrix are identically zero, solving the problem in the $n$-dimensional sample space rather than the $d$-dimensional feature space saves immense computation.

Reconstructing Out-of-Distribution Data

When PCA is trained on face images, each leading principal component $w_k$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{32,256}$ that can be reshaped back into a $192 \times 168$ image—commonly called an Eigenface:

  • $w_1$ typically captures overall lighting (illumination changes across the face).
  • $w_2$ and $w_3$ capture structural features like eye spacing, nose width, and jawlines.

Any face in the dataset is approximated as a linear combination: $\text{Face} \approx \bar{x} + c_1 w_1 + c_2 w_2 + \dots + c_k w_k$.

Now, consider what happens if you project an image of a dog into this face-trained model:

Because the projection is restricted to the subspace spanned by $\{w_1, \dots, w_k\}$, any component outside this face subspace is removed. The reconstructed image is forced to be a linear combination of the eigenfaces, producing a face-like approximation.

This illustrates a fundamental property of PCA: the learned components can only represent directions present in the training data.

The Dual Representation

To eliminate the $O(d^3)$ bottleneck, let us look closely at the eigenvector equation $C w_k = \lambda_k w_k$.

Substitute $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$:

$$\left(\frac{1}{n} X X^T\right) w_k = \lambda_k w_k$$

For any non-zero eigenvalue ($\lambda_k > 0$), we can divide by $\lambda_k$ and regroup terms:

$$w_k = X \left( \frac{1}{n \lambda_k} X^T w_k \right)$$

Look at what the bracketed term represents. $X^T w_k$ is an $n \times 1$ column vector containing the dot products of $w_k$ with each of our $n$ data points. The scalar $\frac{1}{n\lambda_k}$ merely scales it.

Define this $n$-dimensional coefficient vector as:

$$\vec{\alpha}_k = \frac{1}{n \lambda_k} X^T w_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

Substituting $\vec{\alpha}_k$ back gives:

The Representer Property of PCA
$$w_k = X \vec{\alpha}_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} x_i$$

Every principal component $w_k$ is guaranteed to be a linear combination of the training data points. The optimal projection axes must live entirely inside the subspace spanned by the data.

Deriving the Dual Eigenvalue Problem

Now substitute $w_k = X \vec{\alpha}_k$ back into the original eigenvector equation $\frac{1}{n} X X^T w_k = \lambda_k w_k$:

$$\frac{1}{n} X X^T (X \vec{\alpha}_k) = \lambda_k (X \vec{\alpha}_k)$$

Premultiply both sides by $X^T$:

$$\frac{1}{n} (X^T X)(X^T X) \vec{\alpha}_k = \lambda_k (X^T X) \vec{\alpha}_k$$

Notice that the matrix $X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is the Gram matrix (or inner product matrix) $K$, whose entries are the pairwise dot products between all data points:

$$K = X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, \qquad K_{ij} = x_i^T x_j$$

In terms of $K$, our equation becomes:

$$K^2 \vec{\alpha}_k = (n \lambda_k) K \vec{\alpha}_k$$

If we solve the standard, smaller $n \times n$ eigenvalue problem:

$$K \vec{\alpha}_k = (n \lambda_k) \vec{\alpha}_k$$

then multiplying both sides by $K$ immediately satisfies the squared equation. Thus, solving the $n \times n$ eigenvalue problem of $K$ gives us the exact eigenvectors!

Normalizing the Dual Eigenvectors

In standard PCA, we require the principal component to have unit length: $\|w_k\|^2 = w_k^T w_k = 1$. What does this demand of our coefficient vector $\vec{\alpha}_k$?

$$w_k^T w_k = (X \vec{\alpha}_k)^T (X \vec{\alpha}_k) = \vec{\alpha}_k^T (X^T X) \vec{\alpha}_k = \vec{\alpha}_k^T K \vec{\alpha}_k = 1$$

When you pass the symmetric matrix $K$ to a standard numerical eigensolver, it returns eigenvectors $\vec{\beta}_k$ that have standard Euclidean unit norm: $\|\vec{\beta}_k\|^2 = \vec{\beta}_k^T \vec{\beta}_k = 1$, satisfying $K \vec{\beta}_k = \mu_k \vec{\beta}_k$ with eigenvalue $\mu_k = n \lambda_k$.

Let us check the value of $\vec{\beta}_k^T K \vec{\beta}_k$:

$$\vec{\beta}_k^T K \vec{\beta}_k = \vec{\beta}_k^T (\mu_k \vec{\beta}_k) = \mu_k (\vec{\beta}_k^T \vec{\beta}_k) = \mu_k = n \lambda_k$$

To scale $\vec{\beta}_k$ into an $\vec{\alpha}_k$ that satisfies $\vec{\alpha}_k^T K \vec{\alpha}_k = 1$, we simply divide by $\sqrt{\mu_k} = \sqrt{n \lambda_k}$:

The Dual Normalization Formula
$$\vec{\alpha}_k = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{n \lambda_k}} = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{\mu_k}}$$

where $\vec{\beta}_k$ is the standard unit eigenvector of $K$ satisfying $K \vec{\beta}_k = \mu_k \vec{\beta}_k$ with $\|\vec{\beta}_k\| = 1$.

The Computational Payoff: Primal vs. Dual Complexity

Compare the two routes for the Yale Face Dataset ($d = 32{,}256$, $n = 200$):

  • Primal PCA: Eigendecompose a $32{,}256 \times 32{,}256$ covariance matrix $\implies O(d^3) \approx 3.35 \times 10^{13}$ operations.
  • Dual PCA: Compute the $200 \times 200$ Gram matrix $K$ in $O(d n^2) \approx 1.29 \times 10^9$ operations, then eigendecompose $K$ in $O(n^3) \approx 8 \times 10^6$ operations (total $\approx 1.30 \times 10^9$ operations).

While the eigensolver itself runs over $4{,}000{,}000$ times faster ($3.35 \times 10^{13} / 8 \times 10^6$), the overall end-to-end speedup including matrix construction is approximately $26{,}000$ times faster ($3.35 \times 10^{13} / 1.30 \times 10^9$), reducing memory consumption from $4\text{ GB}$ to less than a megabyte.

Connection to the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

This duality is not a coincidence—it is the direct reflection of the SVD. If the data matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$ has thin SVD $X = U \Sigma V^T$, then:

$$C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T = \frac{1}{n} U \Sigma^2 U^T \qquad \text{and} \qquad K = X^T X = V \Sigma^2 V^T$$

Both $X X^T$ and $X^T X$ share the exact same non-zero eigenvalues $\sigma_k^2$. Primal PCA computes the left singular vectors $U$ directly; Dual PCA computes the right singular vectors $V$ and converts them via $U = X V \Sigma^{-1}$.

The Non-Linear Bottleneck

The second limitation of linear PCA is that it only finds flat subspaces (lines, planes, and hyperplanes).

The Circle Experiment

Consider a simple 2D dataset where all data points lie on the circumference of a circle with center $(a, b)$ and radius $R$:

$$(f_1 - a)^2 + (f_2 - b)^2 = R^2$$

Let us trace step-by-step what happens when standard linear PCA is applied to this dataset:

  1. Centering: Subtracting the mean shifts the origin to the center of the circle: $(a, b) \to (0, 0)$. The equation simplifies to $f_1^2 + f_2^2 = R^2$.
  2. Rotational Symmetry: Because the points are distributed uniformly around the perimeter, the spread along the $x$-axis is identical to the spread along the $y$-axis:
    $$\text{Var}(f_1) = \text{Var}(f_2) = \frac{R^2}{2}, \qquad \text{Cov}(f_1, f_2) = 0$$
  3. Eigendecomposition: The covariance matrix is a multiple of the identity:
    $$C = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma^2 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \sigma^2$$
  4. Dimensionality Retention: Because $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$, each component explains exactly $50\%$ of the total variance. If we apply the standard rule of thumb to preserve $95\%$ of the variance, PCA forces us to retain both dimensions ($k = 2$).

Linear PCA concludes that the data is 2-dimensional and achieves no dimensionality reduction, even though the points lie on a 1-dimensional curve.

Linearizing Curves in Higher Dimensions

How can linear algebra handle curved data? The key idea:

We can linearize non-linear relationships by mapping the data into a higher-dimensional space.

Let us expand the circle equation algebraically:

$$f_1^2 + f_2^2 - 2a f_1 - 2b f_2 + (a^2 + b^2 - R^2) = 0$$

Now define a non-linear feature map $\phi(x)$ that lifts any 2D point $x = [f_1, f_2]^T$ into a 6-dimensional feature space $\mathbb{R}^6$:

$$\phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ f_1^2 \\ f_2^2 \\ f_1 f_2 \\ f_1 \\ f_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6$$

and define a constant parameter vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^6$ based on the circle's parameters:

$$u = \begin{bmatrix} a^2 + b^2 - R^2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ -2a \\ -2b \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6$$

Take the dot product between $\phi(x)$ and $u$:

$$\phi(x)^T u = (a^2 + b^2 - R^2)(1) + (1)f_1^2 + (1)f_2^2 + (0)f_1 f_2 - 2a f_1 - 2b f_2 = 0$$
The Geometric Insight of Non-Linear Lifting

In 2D, the circle is non-linear and curved. But in 6D, every mapped point $\phi(x)$ lies on a perfectly flat hyperplane orthogonal to $u$.

In this 6D space, the variance in the direction of $u$ is exactly zero. Standard linear PCA in the lifted space will immediately detect this flat linear subspace and compress the data cleanly!

Implicit Inner Products & Kernels

Mapping data into a higher-dimensional space makes curved relationships linear. However, explicitly computing and storing high-dimensional feature vectors is often computationally impractical.

If you have input vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and want to consider all polynomial interactions up to degree $p$, the dimension of the mapped feature vector $\phi(x)$ explodes combinatorially:

$$\dim(\phi(x)) = \binom{d + p}{p}$$

For a modest input of $d = 100$ features with degree $p = 5$, $\dim(\phi(x)) = \binom{105}{5} \approx 96.5\text{ million}$. And for richer, smooth mappings, the dimension can be literally infinite ($\infty$). You cannot compute, store, or manipulate vectors with 96 million components.

The Dual Bridge

Here is where our earlier work on the Dual Representation pays off. Look back at what we discovered when we solved PCA in the dual space:

  1. To find the principal components, we solve $K \vec{\alpha}_k = n \lambda_k \vec{\alpha}_k$. The matrix $K$ requires only the pairwise dot products between samples: $K_{ij} = \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j)$.
  2. To project a new query point $x$, we compute:
    $$y_k(x) = \phi(x)^T w_k = \phi(x)^T \left( \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{kj} \phi(x_j) \right) = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_{kj} \left( \phi(x)^T \phi(x_j) \right)$$

Look at both equations carefully. The dual representation never needs the individual coordinates of $\phi(x)$! It only ever needs the inner product between pairs of vectors: $\phi(x)^T \phi(x')$.

This leads directly to the central question:

Can we compute the high-dimensional inner product $\phi(x)^T \phi(x')$ directly from our original low-dimensional inputs $x$ and $x'$, without ever calculating or storing the vectors $\phi(x)$?

Algebraic Proof: Evaluating 6D Inner Products in 2D

Let us test this idea on a concrete example. Take two 2D vectors $x = [f_1, f_2]^T$ and $x' = [g_1, g_2]^T$.

Instead of mapping $x$ and $x'$ into 6D, consider the following simple scalar operation computed purely using their 2D dot product:

$$f(x, x') = (x^T x' + 1)^2$$

Let us expand this scalar expression algebraically:

$$\begin{aligned} (x^T x' + 1)^2 &= (f_1 g_1 + f_2 g_2 + 1)^2 \\ &= (f_1 g_1)^2 + (f_2 g_2)^2 + 1^2 + 2(f_1 g_1)(f_2 g_2) + 2(f_1 g_1)(1) + 2(f_2 g_2)(1) \\ &= f_1^2 g_1^2 + f_2^2 g_2^2 + 1 + 2 f_1 f_2 g_1 g_2 + 2 f_1 g_1 + 2 f_2 g_2 \end{aligned}$$

Now consider the 6-dimensional feature vectors that represent all quadratic interactions:

$$\phi(x) = \begin{bmatrix} f_1^2 \\ f_2^2 \\ 1 \\ \sqrt{2}f_1 f_2 \\ \sqrt{2}f_1 \\ \sqrt{2}f_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6, \qquad \phi(x') = \begin{bmatrix} g_1^2 \\ g_2^2 \\ 1 \\ \sqrt{2}g_1 g_2 \\ \sqrt{2}g_1 \\ \sqrt{2}g_2 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6$$

Take their direct Euclidean dot product $\phi(x)^T \phi(x')$:

$$\phi(x)^T \phi(x') = f_1^2 g_1^2 + f_2^2 g_2^2 + 1 + 2 f_1 f_2 g_1 g_2 + 2 f_1 g_1 + 2 f_2 g_2$$

The two expressions are identically equal! Computing $(x^T x' + 1)^2$ in 2D gives the exact inner product in $\mathbb{R}^6$, with zero high-dimensional overhead.

Naming the Mechanism: Kernel Functions & The Kernel Trick

We have just evaluated an inner product in a 6-dimensional feature space using nothing more than a simple scalar operation in 2-dimensional space.

Now that the purpose and mechanism are clear, we give it its formal name:

The Kernel Function & The Kernel Trick

A function $k(x, x')$ that computes the inner product between two points in some higher-dimensional feature space directly from their original coordinates—$k(x, x') = \langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$—is called a kernel function.

Performing linear algorithms in high-dimensional (or even infinite-dimensional) spaces purely through kernel evaluations without ever visiting those spaces explicitly is known as the Kernel Trick.

Common Kernel Functions

  • Polynomial Kernel: $k(x, x') = (x^T x' + c)^p$. Maps data into the space of all monomial combinations up to degree $p$.
  • Radial Basis Function (RBF) / Gaussian Kernel:
    $$k(x, x') = \exp\left(-\frac{\|x - x'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

    The RBF kernel maps data into an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. To see why, recall the Taylor series of the exponential function:

    $$\exp\left(\frac{x^T x'}{\sigma^2}\right) = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \frac{1}{m!} \left(\frac{x^T x'}{\sigma^2}\right)^m$$

    The exponential contains polynomial terms of every integer degree from $0$ to $\infty$!

Mercer's Theorem: What Makes a Kernel Valid?

Can you pick any arbitrary mathematical function $k(x, x')$ and call it a kernel? No. Because $k(x, x')$ represents an inner product $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle$ in some real Hilbert space, it must obey the geometric laws of inner products:

  1. Symmetry: $\langle \phi(x), \phi(x') \rangle = \langle \phi(x'), \phi(x) \rangle \implies k(x, x') = k(x', x)$.
  2. Non-Negative Lengths (Positive Semidefiniteness): For any set of points $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ and any choice of real coefficients $c_1, \dots, c_n$, the squared length of the vector $v = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \phi(x_i)$ must be non-negative:
    $$\|v\|^2 = \left\langle \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \phi(x_i), \sum_{j=1}^n c_j \phi(x_j) \right\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n c_i c_j k(x_i, x_j) = c^T K c \geq 0$$

This connects directly back to our lesson on Positive Definite Matrices:

Mercer's Theorem

A continuous symmetric function $k(x, x')$ is a valid kernel corresponding to an inner product in some feature space if and only if the Gram matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ (with $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$) is positive semidefinite (PSD) for every possible finite dataset.

Centering in Feature Space

Standard PCA requires the data to be centered at the origin: $\sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i) = \mathbf{0}$. If the data is not centered, the first component simply points toward the center of mass rather than capturing the direction of maximum variance.

Recall that standard PCA strictly requires the data to be centered at the origin: $\sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i) = \mathbf{0}$. If the data is not centered, the first "principal component" simply points from the origin toward the center of mass rather than capturing the axes of variance.

Even if your raw input data $x_i$ is centered in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the mapped features $\phi(x_i)$ in Hilbert space are almost never centered (e.g., if $\bar{x} = 0$, $x^2$ is strictly positive, so the mean of $\phi(x)$ is non-zero).

The Dilemma: We cannot compute the mean vector $\bar{\phi} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \phi(x_i)$ directly because we cannot access the infinite-dimensional vectors $\phi(x_i)$. How do we center points that we cannot see?

Deriving the Centered Gram Matrix $K_c$

Let the true, unobserved centered feature vector for sample $i$ be:

$$\tilde{\phi}(x_i) = \phi(x_i) - \bar{\phi} = \phi(x_i) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r)$$

We want to compute the entry $(K_c)_{ij} = \tilde{\phi}(x_i)^T \tilde{\phi}(x_j)$ of the centered Gram matrix. Let us expand the inner product directly:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = \left( \phi(x_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r) \right)^T \left( \phi(x_j) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_s) \right)$$

Multiplying out the four terms:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = \underbrace{\phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_j)}_{K_{ij}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_i)^T \phi(x_s)}_{\text{Row } i \text{ average}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n \phi(x_r)^T \phi(x_j)}_{\text{Col } j \text{ average}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n \phi(x_r)^T \phi(x_s)}_{\text{Grand matrix average}}$$

Notice that every single term is an average of entries from our original, uncentered kernel matrix $K$:

$$(K_c)_{ij} = K_{ij} - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{s=1}^n K_{is} - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n K_{rj} + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n K_{rs}$$

The Centering Projector Matrix

We can write this entire operation in a single, beautiful matrix equation. Define the $n \times n$ centering projection matrix $H$:

$$H = I_n - \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}_n \mathbf{1}_n^T$$

where $I_n$ is the $n \times n$ identity matrix and $\mathbf{1}_n$ is an $n \times 1$ column vector of all ones. Notice the geometric properties of $H$:

  • $H$ is symmetric: $H^T = H$.
  • $H$ is an orthogonal projector (idempotent): $H^2 = H$. It projects any vector onto the subspace orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}_n$, subtracting its mean.

Multiplying $K$ on both sides by $H$ gives:

The Centered Gram Matrix
$$K_c = H K H$$

Multiplying on the left subtracts column means; multiplying on the right subtracts row means; and their composition automatically adds back the grand mean. We have centered the infinite-dimensional feature space using only $n \times n$ matrix multiplications!

Kernel PCA & Synthesis

Putting all our pieces together, here is the complete, rigorous algorithm for non-linear dimensionality reduction:

The 5 Steps of Kernel PCA

  1. Compute the Kernel Matrix: Choose a valid Mercer kernel (such as Gaussian RBF) and compute the $n \times n$ Gram matrix:
    $$K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$$
  2. Center the Kernel Matrix: Apply the centering projection:
    $$K_c = H K H = K - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T K - K\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T + \frac{1}{n^2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T K \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$$
  3. Solve the Eigenvalue Problem: Compute the eigenvectors $\vec{\beta}_1, \dots, \vec{\beta}_k$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_1', \dots, \lambda_k'$ of $K_c$:
    $$K_c \vec{\beta}_k = \lambda_k' \vec{\beta}_k \qquad \text{with} \quad \|\vec{\beta}_k\| = 1$$
  4. Normalize Dual Coefficients: Scale each eigenvector to satisfy $\vec{\alpha}_k^T K_c \vec{\alpha}_k = 1$:
    $$\vec{\alpha}_k = \frac{\vec{\beta}_k}{\sqrt{\lambda_k'}}$$
  5. Project New Query Points: To extract the $k$-th non-linear coordinate $y_k(x^*)$ of any new data point $x^*$:
    $$y_k(x^*) = \tilde{\phi}(x^*)^T w_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ki} \tilde{k}_i(x^*)$$
    where $\tilde{k}_i(x^*)$ is the centered kernel vector between the test point and the training data:
    $$\tilde{k}_i(x^*) = k(x^*, x_i) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n k(x^*, x_j) - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{r=1}^n K_{ri} + \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{r=1}^n \sum_{s=1}^n K_{rs}$$

Connecting the Dots: Summary of Representations

Let us step back and appreciate the unified progression we have constructed across this article:

Method Central Matrix Dimensions When to Use Key Insight
Primal Linear PCA $C = \frac{1}{n} X X^T$ $d \times d$ $n \gg d$ (many samples, few features) Maximizing projection variance is identical to minimizing reconstruction error.
Dual Linear PCA $K = X^T X$ $n \times n$ $d \gg n$ (images, genomics, sensor arrays) Eigenvectors must live in the span of the data ($w = X\alpha$), collapsing $O(d^3) \to O(n^3)$.

Every breakthrough in this lesson emerged from answering a single geometric or computational bottleneck. By connecting the Spectral Theorem, orthogonal projections, Gram matrices, and positive definiteness, linear algebra allows us to unravel non-linear structures in infinite dimensions using nothing more than simple $n \times n$ matrices.
