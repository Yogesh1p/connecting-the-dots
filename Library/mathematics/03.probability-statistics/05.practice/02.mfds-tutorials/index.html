Lectures show you a method once. Tutorials are where you find out whether you can pick the right method on your own, when the question doesn't tell you which chapter it belongs to. This page works through all 27 problems from the three DA5000 tutorials in that spirit.

Every problem follows the same pattern. First, which tool: the clue in the question that tells you what to reach for. Then set it up: the formula with the problem's numbers plugged in, ready for you to finish. The full solution sits under Check your work, so try before you open it. Wherever the handwritten tutorial solutions contain a slip, it is pointed out, so you don't copy it into an exam.

The problem statements below are reconstructed from the worked tutorial notes. All numerical answers were re-checked by computer.

Before you calculate anything: what is random, what distribution does it follow, and is the question asking for a probability, an expectation, an interval or a decision?

T1 Q1: How Worrying Is a Positive Covid Test?

Tutorial 1 · Q1
Problem Statement

1% of the population has Covid. The test is positive for 96% of people who have Covid, and (falsely) positive for 2% of people who don't.

(a) A person tests positive. What is the probability they actually have Covid? (b) A person tests negative. What is the probability they have Covid anyway?

Which tool: you are given $P(\text{test} \mid \text{disease})$ and asked for $P(\text{disease} \mid \text{test})$. Reversing a conditional is Bayes' theorem.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} P(C) &= 0.01, \quad P(C^c) = 0.99 \\[6pt] P(+ \mid C) &= 0.96, \quad P(+ \mid C^c) = 0.02 \\[6pt] P(- \mid C) &= 1 - 0.96 = 0.04 \\[6pt] P(- \mid C^c) &= 1 - 0.02 = 0.98 \end{aligned}$$

The test's two error rates have names. $P(+ \mid C^c)$ is the false-positive rate; $P(- \mid C)$ is the false-negative rate.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(+) &= 0.96(0.01) + 0.02(0.99) \\[4pt] &= 0.0096 + 0.0198 = 0.0294 \\[8pt] P(C \mid +) &= \frac{0.0096}{0.0294} \approx 0.327 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} P(-) &= 0.04(0.01) + 0.98(0.99) \\[4pt] &= 0.0004 + 0.9702 = 0.9706 \\[8pt] P(C \mid -) &= \frac{0.0004}{0.9706} \approx 0.00041 \end{aligned}$$

(a) Only about 32.7%. Because the disease is rare, most positives come from the 99% of healthy people. (b) About 0.04%: a negative result is very reassuring.

T1 Q2: The Density $k/x^3$

Tutorial 1 · Q2
Problem Statement

$f(x) = 0$ for $x < 1$ and $f(x) = k/x^3$ for $x \ge 1$.

(a) Find $k$. (b) Find $P(1.5 \le X \le 3)$. (c) Find $P(X = 3)$. (d) Find the CDF of $X$.

Which tool: "find the constant" always means "make the total probability 1". After that, probabilities of ranges are areas under $f$, and the CDF is the area from the left up to $x$.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} \int_1^\infty \frac{k}{x^3}\,dx &= 1 \\[8pt] P(a \le X \le b) &= \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \\[8pt] F(x) &= P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt \end{aligned}$$

Useful antiderivative: $\int x^{-3}\,dx = -\dfrac{1}{2x^2}$.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \text{(a)} \quad k \Big[-\frac{1}{2x^2}\Big]_1^\infty &= k\Big(0 + \frac{1}{2}\Big) = \frac{k}{2} = 1 \\[4pt] k &= 2 \\[10pt] \text{(b)} \quad \int_{1.5}^{3} \frac{2}{x^3}\,dx &= \Big[-\frac{1}{x^2}\Big]_{1.5}^{3} \\[4pt] &= \frac{1}{2.25} - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{1}{3} \end{aligned}$$

(c) $P(X = 3) = 0$. For a continuous variable, a single point has no width, so it has no area.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{(d)} \quad F(x) &= 0, \quad x < 1 \\[6pt] F(x) &= \int_1^x \frac{2}{t^3}\,dt = 1 - \frac{1}{x^2}, \quad x \ge 1 \end{aligned}$$

Check: $F(1) = 0$, $F(\infty) = 1$, and $F(3) - F(1.5) = \frac{8}{9} - \frac{5}{9} = \frac{1}{3}$, matching (b).

T1 Q3: An Expectation Over a Joint Density

Tutorial 1 · Q3
Problem Statement

$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{5x^2}{2}$ for $-1 \le x \le 1$ and $0 \le y \le x^2$, and $0$ otherwise. Find $E\big[7X^2 + 8Y^2 + 2X + 8Y\big]$.

Which tool: an expectation of a sum. Linearity of expectation says $E[aU + bV] = aE[U] + bE[V]$, always (no independence needed). So split the big expectation into four small ones.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} E[\,\cdot\,] &= 7E[X^2] + 8E[Y^2] + 2E[X] + 8E[Y] \\[8pt] E[g(X,Y)] &= \int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{x^2} g(x,y)\,\frac{5x^2}{2}\,dy\,dx \end{aligned}$$

Do the inner $dy$ integral first: its upper limit $x^2$ depends on $x$.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} E[X] &= \int_{-1}^{1} x \cdot \frac{5x^2}{2} \cdot x^2\,dx = \frac{5}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} x^5\,dx = 0 \\[8pt] E[X^2] &= \frac{5}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} x^6\,dx = \frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{7} = \frac{5}{7} \\[8pt] E[Y] &= \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x^4}{2}\cdot\frac{5x^2}{2}\,dx = \frac{5}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{7} = \frac{5}{14} \\[8pt] E[Y^2] &= \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{x^6}{3}\cdot\frac{5x^2}{2}\,dx = \frac{5}{6}\cdot\frac{2}{9} = \frac{5}{27} \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} E[\,\cdot\,] &= 7\cdot\frac{5}{7} + 8\cdot\frac{5}{27} + 0 + 8\cdot\frac{5}{14} \\[6pt] &= 5 + \frac{40}{27} + \frac{20}{7} \\[6pt] &= \frac{945 + 280 + 540}{189} = \frac{1765}{189} \approx 9.339 \end{aligned}$$

Note on the tutorial notes: they reach 1765/189 correctly but then mis-simplify it to 588/63 ≈ 9.333. 1765/189 does not reduce; the answer is ≈ 9.339.

$E[X] = 0$ because $x^5$ is an odd function on a symmetric interval: positive and negative halves cancel.

T1 Q4: How Many Rods to Reach Length 100?

Tutorial 1 · Q4
Problem Statement

Rod lengths $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are independent and Exponential with mean 20 ($\lambda = 1/20$). Rods are laid end to end until the total length first reaches $t = 100$. Let $N$ be the number of rods used. Find the distribution of $N$, $E[N]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(N)$.

Which tool: exponential gaps laid end to end are a Poisson process. The rod ends are "events" along a line, at rate $\lambda$ per unit length. Counting events in a stretch of length $t$ gives a Poisson($\lambda t$) count.
Set it up

The number of rod ends that fall before 100 is Poisson with mean $\lambda t$. You need one more rod to cross 100, so:

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda t &= \frac{1}{20}\times 100 = 5 \\[6pt] N - 1 &\sim \text{Poisson}(5) \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(N = n) &= \frac{e^{-5}\,5^{\,n-1}}{(n-1)!}, \quad n = 1, 2, \dots \\[8pt] E[N] &= 1 + 5 = 6 \\[6pt] \operatorname{Var}(N) &= \operatorname{Var}(N - 1) = 5 \end{aligned}$$

Adding the constant 1 shifts the mean but not the variance.

T1 Q5: The Hat-Check Problem

Tutorial 1 · Q5
Problem Statement

$n$ people throw their hats in a pile, and each picks up a hat at random. Let $X$ be the number of people who get their own hat back. Find $E[X]$.

Which tool: "expected number of …" with messy dependence between people. Write the count as a sum of indicator variables, each 1 if something happens and 0 if not, then use linearity. The dependence between people doesn't matter for the mean.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} I_i &= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{person } i \text{ gets their own hat} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\[8pt] X &= I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_n \\[6pt] E[I_i] &= P(I_i = 1) \end{aligned}$$
Check your work

Person $i$ is equally likely to get any of the $n$ hats, so $P(I_i = 1) = 1/n$.

$$E[X] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{n} = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$$

On average exactly one person gets their own hat back, whether there are 3 people or 3,000.

T1 Q6: Connection Time and a Maintenance Window

Tutorial 1 · Q6
Problem Statement

A user connects at a time $X$ hours after 8 pm, with $X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 2)$. (a) Write the PDF. (b) Find the mean and variance. (c) There is a 15-minute maintenance window somewhere inside the two hours. What is the probability the user connects during it?

Which tool: "equally likely at any time in an interval" is the continuous uniform. Probability is length of the window divided by length of the interval.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} f(x) &= \frac{1}{b-a} \text{ on } [a, b] \\[6pt] E[X] &= \frac{a+b}{2}, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12} \\[6pt] 15 \text{ min} &= 0.25 \text{ hours} \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} f(x) &= \tfrac{1}{2}, \quad 0 \le x \le 2 \\[6pt] E[X] &= 1 \text{ hour (9 pm)} \\[6pt] \operatorname{Var}(X) &= \frac{4}{12} = \frac{1}{3} \text{ hours}^2 \\[8pt] P(a \le X \le a + 0.25) &= \int_a^{a+0.25}\frac{1}{2}\,dx = \frac{0.25}{2} = 0.125 \end{aligned}$$

12.5%, wherever the window sits inside the two hours. Note the variance's unit is hours², not hours.

T1 Q7: Uncorrelated but Not Independent

Tutorial 1 · Q7
Problem Statement

$X_1 \sim N(0, 1)$. $Z$ is independent of $X_1$ and takes values $+1$ and $-1$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each. Let $X_2 = Z X_1$. (a) What is the distribution of $X_2$? (b) Find $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2)$. Are $X_1$ and $X_2$ independent?

Which tool: covariance measures how two variables move together linearly: $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]$. Independence is a much stronger statement: knowing one tells you nothing about the other.
Set it up

(a) Flipping the sign of a standard normal gives a standard normal, because $N(0,1)$ is symmetric. (b) Use independence of $Z$ and $X_1$ to split the expectation:

$$\begin{aligned} E[X_1 X_2] &= E[Z X_1^2] = E[Z]\,E[X_1^2] \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} X_2 &\sim N(0, 1) \\[6pt] E[Z] &= \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(-1) = 0 \\[6pt] \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) &= E[Z]\,E[X_1^2] - 0 \cdot 0 = 0 \cdot 1 = 0 \end{aligned}$$

Zero covariance, yet they are not independent: $|X_2| = |X_1|$ always. If you know $X_1 = 2$, then $X_2$ must be $2$ or $-2$. Every point $(X_1, X_2)$ lies on one of the two lines in Figure 1, which has no linear trend overall.

Lesson: independence implies zero covariance, but zero covariance does not imply independence.

T1 Q8: Visitors With a Falling Mean

Tutorial 1 · Q8
Problem Statement

Visitors in minute $t$ of a one-hour offer, $X_t$, are Normal with mean $\mu_t = 8000 - t^2$ and SD $\sigma = 800$. Write down the distribution and find the mean at $t = 0$, $30$ and $60$.

Which tool: this is the same set-up as the simulation problem from session 3. A random variable whose parameter is a function of time: plug each $t$ into the mean formula. Variance is $\sigma^2$, not $\sigma$.
Set it up $$X_t \sim N\big(8000 - t^2,\; 800^2\big)$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \sigma^2 &= 800^2 = 640{,}000 \\[6pt] \mu_0 &= 8000 \\[6pt] \mu_{30} &= 8000 - 900 = 7100 \\[6pt] \mu_{60} &= 8000 - 3600 = 4400 \end{aligned}$$

The spread stays the same all hour, while the centre slides down. With a 9000-per-minute capacity cap, overflow is plausible only early in the hour (9000 is $1.25\sigma$ above $\mu_0$ but $2.4\sigma$ above $\mu_{30}$).

T1 Q9: Up to Date or Behind? A Markov Chain

Tutorial 1 · Q9
Problem Statement

Each week a student is either Up to date (U) or Behind (B). If U this week: next week U with probability 0.8, B with 0.2. If B this week: next week U with 0.6, B with 0.4. The student starts up to date. What is the probability of being up to date after 1, 2 and 3 weeks?

Which tool: next week depends only on this week. That is a Markov chain. Put the probabilities in a transition matrix $P$ (row = current state, column = next state, each row sums to 1) and multiply the current probability vector by $P$ once per week.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} P &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.2 \\ 0.6 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix} \\[8pt] v_0 &= (1,\; 0) \\[6pt] v_{k+1} &= v_k\,P \end{aligned}$$

Each new entry is "probability of being in each state now" times "chance of moving to the target state", summed: the law of total probability.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} v_1 &= (0.8,\; 0.2) \\[6pt] v_2 &= (0.8 \cdot 0.8 + 0.2 \cdot 0.6,\; 0.8 \cdot 0.2 + 0.2 \cdot 0.4) \\[4pt] &= (0.76,\; 0.24) \\[6pt] v_3 &= (0.76 \cdot 0.8 + 0.24 \cdot 0.6,\; 0.76 \cdot 0.2 + 0.24 \cdot 0.4) \\[4pt] &= (0.752,\; 0.248) \end{aligned}$$

Up to date after 3 weeks: 0.752. The numbers are settling down. In the long run the chain reaches a steady state $\pi$ with $\pi P = \pi$: solving $\pi_U = 0.8\pi_U + 0.6\pi_B$ with $\pi_U + \pi_B = 1$ gives $\pi_U = 0.75$.

Tutorial 2: First Tests, Power, and More Distributions

Tutorial 2 brings in hypothesis testing ($t$-tests and a binomial test with its power) and mixes it with more probability: a noisy communication channel, joint densities, Bessel's correction and the Poisson/exponential pair.

Vocabulary for This Tutorial
  • Significance level $\alpha$: $P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true})$, the Type I error rate.
  • Power: $P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ false})$ $= 1 - \beta$. The chance the test catches a real effect.
  • Critical value: the cut-off for the test statistic. Beyond it, you reject. Comparing to the critical value and comparing the p-value to $\alpha$ always give the same decision.
  • Degrees of freedom (df): the parameter that picks which $t$ (or $\chi^2$, $F$) curve to use. Fewer df means heavier tails and bigger critical values.

T2 Q1: Do Two Fitness Apps Give Different Step Counts?

Tutorial 2 · Q1
Problem Statement

Users of App X: $n_x = 30$, mean 8150 steps/day, SD 900. Users of App Y: $n_y = 25$, mean 7600, SD 1100. At $\alpha = 0.05$, do average daily steps differ?

Which tool: two independent groups, compare means, population SDs unknown and not assumed equal → Welch's two-sample $t$-test. "Differ" (no direction) → two-tailed.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} H_0 &: \mu_x = \mu_y, \quad H_1: \mu_x \ne \mu_y \\[8pt] SE &= \sqrt{\frac{s_x^2}{n_x} + \frac{s_y^2}{n_y}} = \sqrt{\frac{900^2}{30} + \frac{1100^2}{25}} \\[8pt] t &= \frac{\bar{x} - \bar{y}}{SE} \\[8pt] \nu &= \frac{\big(\frac{s_x^2}{n_x} + \frac{s_y^2}{n_y}\big)^2}{\frac{(s_x^2/n_x)^2}{n_x - 1} + \frac{(s_y^2/n_y)^2}{n_y - 1}} \end{aligned}$$

$\nu$ (the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom) is usually not a whole number. That's fine.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} SE &= \sqrt{27000 + 48400} = \sqrt{75400} = 274.6 \\[6pt] t &= \frac{550}{274.6} = 2.003 \\[6pt] \nu &\approx 46.3 \\[6pt] p &= 2\,P(T_{46.3} > 2.003) \approx 0.051 \end{aligned}$$

$p = 0.051 > 0.05$: fail to reject. Not enough evidence that the apps differ, though it is right on the edge.

Note: the tutorial notes write $n_x = 360$ at the top but use 30 in every calculation; 30 is what gives $\nu = 46.3$.

T2 Q2: Does the Drug Lower Blood Pressure by More Than 15?

Tutorial 2 · Q2
Problem Statement

A company claims its drug lowers blood pressure by more than 15 mmHg on average. In a sample of $n = 25$ patients the mean reduction is 17 mmHg with SD 6 mmHg. Test the claim at $\alpha = 0.05$.

Which tool: one sample, one mean, $\sigma$ unknown (only the sample SD) → one-sample $t$-test with $n - 1$ df. The claim "more than 15" has no equality, so it goes in $H_1$ → right-tailed.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} H_0 &: \mu = 15, \quad H_1: \mu > 15 \\[6pt] SE &= \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{25}} \\[6pt] t &= \frac{\bar{x} - 15}{SE}, \quad \nu = 24 \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} SE &= 1.2 \\[6pt] t &= \frac{2}{1.2} = 1.667 \\[6pt] t_{0.05,\,24} &= 1.711 \\[6pt] p &= P(T_{24} > 1.667) \approx 0.054 \end{aligned}$$

$1.667 < 1.711$ (equivalently $p = 0.054 > 0.05$): fail to reject. The sample is suggestive, but not strong enough to back the claim at the 5% level.

T2 Q3: Error Probability of a Noisy Signal

Tutorial 2 · Q3
Problem Statement

A transmitter sends $X = +1$ or $X = -1$ (equally likely). The receiver gets $Y = X + N$, where noise $N \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$. It decides $+1$ if $Y \ge 0$ and $-1$ if $Y < 0$. Find the probability of error.

Which tool: a normal probability. Translate "error" into an inequality on $N$, then standardise with $Z = N/\sigma$ and use $\Phi$, the standard normal CDF.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} \text{Send } +1: \text{ error if } Y < 0 &\iff 1 + N < 0 \iff N < -1 \\[6pt] \text{Send } -1: \text{ error if } Y \ge 0 &\iff -1 + N \ge 0 \iff N \ge 1 \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(N < -1) &= P\Big(Z < -\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big) = \Phi\Big(-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big) \\[8pt] P(N \ge 1) &= 1 - \Phi\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big) = \Phi\Big(-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big) \end{aligned}$$

Both errors have the same probability (the noise is symmetric), so with equal priors:

$$P_{\text{error}} = \Phi\Big(-\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big) = 1 - \Phi\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big)$$

Sanity check: as $\sigma \to 0$ the error goes to 0; as $\sigma \to \infty$ it goes to $\tfrac{1}{2}$ (pure guessing).

T2 Q4: Significance Level and Power of a Binomial Test

Tutorial 2 · Q4
Problem Statement

$X \sim \text{Binomial}(20, p)$. We test $H_0: p = 0.75$ against $H_1: p < 0.75$, and reject $H_0$ if $X \le 10$. (a) Find the significance level $\alpha$. (b) Find the power if in fact $p = 0.6$.

Which tool: the rejection rule is already given, so there is no test statistic to compute. $\alpha$ and power are both just "probability the rule fires", under two different values of $p$.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} \alpha &= P(X \le 10 \mid p = 0.75) = \sum_{k=0}^{10}\binom{20}{k}0.75^k\,0.25^{20-k} \\[8pt] \text{Power} &= P(X \le 10 \mid p = 0.6) = \sum_{k=0}^{10}\binom{20}{k}0.6^k\,0.4^{20-k} \end{aligned}$$

Use a binomial CDF: Excel =BINOM.DIST(10, 20, p, TRUE), R pbinom(10, 20, p).

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \alpha &\approx 0.0139 \\[6pt] \text{Power} &\approx 0.245 \\[6pt] \beta &= 1 - 0.245 = 0.755 \end{aligned}$$

The test rarely raises a false alarm (1.4%), but it only catches a true $p = 0.6$ about a quarter of the time. With $n = 20$, 0.6 and 0.75 are hard to tell apart. A bigger sample would raise the power.

T2 Q5: The Joint Density $c\,x^2 y$ on a Triangle

Tutorial 2 · Q5
Problem Statement

$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = c\,x^2 y$ for $0 \le y \le x \le 1$, and 0 otherwise.

(a) Describe the region $R_{XY}$. (b) Find $c$. (c) Find the marginal PDFs. (d) Find $P(Y \le X/2)$. (e) Find $P(Y \le X/4 \mid Y \le X/2)$.

Which tool: joint densities on a non-rectangular region. Draw the region first: it fixes the limits of every integral. Marginal = integrate out the other variable; probability of an event = integrate the density over that part of the region.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} \int_0^1\!\int_0^x c\,x^2 y\,dy\,dx &= 1 \\[8pt] f_X(x) &= \int_0^x f(x,y)\,dy \\[8pt] f_Y(y) &= \int_y^1 f(x,y)\,dx \\[8pt] P(Y \le X/2) &= \int_0^1\!\int_0^{x/2} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx \end{aligned}$$

For (e), use the definition of conditional probability: $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$. Here $A$ ("under $x/4$") sits inside $B$ ("under $x/2$"), so $A \cap B = A$.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \text{(b)} \quad \int_0^1 c\,x^2\cdot\frac{x^2}{2}\,dx &= \frac{c}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{5} = \frac{c}{10} = 1 \\[4pt] c &= 10 \\[10pt] \text{(c)} \quad f_X(x) &= \int_0^x 10x^2 y\,dy = 5x^4, \quad 0 \le x \le 1 \\[8pt] f_Y(y) &= \int_y^1 10x^2 y\,dx = \frac{10}{3}\,y\,(1 - y^3), \quad 0 \le y \le 1 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \text{(d)} \quad \int_0^1 10x^2\cdot\frac{(x/2)^2}{2}\,dx &= \int_0^1 \frac{5}{4}x^4\,dx = \frac{1}{4} \\[10pt] P(Y \le X/4) &= \int_0^1 10x^2\cdot\frac{(x/4)^2}{2}\,dx = \int_0^1 \frac{5}{16}x^4\,dx = \frac{1}{16} \\[8pt] \text{(e)} \quad P(Y \le X/4 \mid Y \le X/2) &= \frac{1/16}{1/4} = \frac{1}{4} \end{aligned}$$

Check (c): $\int_0^1 5x^4\,dx = 1$ ✓.

T2 Q6: Why Divide by $n - 1$? Bessel's Correction

Tutorial 2 · Q6
Problem Statement

$X_1, \dots, X_n$ is an i.i.d. sample (independent, identically distributed) with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Show that $S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2$ satisfies $E[S^2] = \sigma^2$, i.e. it is an unbiased estimator (right on average).

Which tool: a proof with expectations. The trick is to rewrite deviations from $\bar{X}$ in terms of deviations from $\mu$, whose expectations you know.
Set it up

Add and subtract $\mu$ inside the square and expand. The cross term collapses because $\sum (X_i - \mu) = n(\bar{X} - \mu)$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{X} - \mu)^2$$

Two facts you need: $E[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $E[(\bar{X} - \mu)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2/n$.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} E\Big[\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2\Big] &= \sum E[(X_i - \mu)^2] - n\,E[(\bar{X} - \mu)^2] \\[6pt] &= n\sigma^2 - n\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{n} \\[6pt] &= (n - 1)\,\sigma^2 \\[8pt] E[S^2] &= \frac{1}{n-1}(n-1)\,\sigma^2 = \sigma^2 \end{aligned}$$

Dividing by $n$ instead would give $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$: always a little too small, because $\bar{X}$ sits closer to the data than $\mu$ does.

T2 Q7: One Event Every 10 Days

Tutorial 2 · Q7
Problem Statement

Events occur as a Poisson process with an average interarrival time of 10 days. (a) What is the expected waiting time until the next event? (b) What is the probability of at least one event in the next half day?

Which tool: "time between events" in a Poisson process is Exponential with rate $\lambda = 1/\text{mean}$. "At least one event by time $t$" is the same as "waiting time $\le t$", which is the exponential CDF.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} \lambda &= \frac{1}{10} = 0.1 \text{ per day} \\[6pt] f(t) &= \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \quad E[T] = \frac{1}{\lambda} \\[6pt] P(T \le t) &= 1 - e^{-\lambda t} \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \text{(a)} \quad E[T] &= 10 \text{ days} \\[6pt] \text{(b)} \quad P(T \le 0.5) &= 1 - e^{-0.05} \approx 0.0488 \end{aligned}$$

About 4.9%. Because the exponential is memoryless, this is true no matter how long you've already waited.

(b) Given that 8 calls arrived, and each call independently is of a particular type (say, urgent) with probability 0.2, find the probability that exactly 2 of them are urgent.

Tutorial 3: Two-Sample Tests, Variances and $\chi^2$

Tutorial 3 is a tour of every two-sample situation: means with known $\sigma$, proportions, unequal and equal variances, paired data, comparing variances directly, and finally counts with $\chi^2$. The hard part is rarely the arithmetic. It's choosing the right test, so the decision path comes first.

Choosing a Two-Sample Test
  1. Same subjects measured twice (before/after)? → Paired $t$ on the differences.
  2. Comparing proportions (fraction defect-free, fraction who buy)? → Two-proportion $z$.
  3. Comparing variability (consistency, repeatability)? → $F$-test, $F = s_1^2/s_2^2$.
  4. Comparing means, $\sigma$'s known? → Two-sample $z$.
  5. Comparing means, $\sigma$'s unknown? Run an $F$-test first (or use judgement). Equal variances → pooled $t$ ($n_1 + n_2 - 2$ df). Unequal → Welch $t$.
  6. Counts in categories? One categorical variable vs a claimed distribution → $\chi^2$ goodness of fit. Two categorical variables → $\chi^2$ test of independence.
Pooled vs Unpooled, and the Margin of Error

For two proportions, the test and the interval use different standard errors:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Test (assumes } p_1 = p_2\text{): } SE &= \sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\Big(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\Big)} \\[8pt] \hat{p} &= \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2} \\[8pt] \text{CI (no assumption): } SE &= \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}} \end{aligned}$$

Why? A test is computed as if $H_0$ were true, so it pools both samples into one shared $p$. An interval makes no such assumption. Every interval has the form: point estimate $\pm$ margin of error, where margin of error = critical value × SE.

For a two-sided $F$-test: p-value $= 2 \times \min\{P(F \le f),\, P(F \ge f)\}$.

T3 Q1: Does the New Process Add 25 Hours of Lifetime?

Tutorial 3 · Q1
Problem Statement

Old process: $n = 16$, mean lifetime 5000 h, $\sigma = 40$. New process: $n = 25$, mean 5050 h, $\sigma = 30$. At $\alpha = 0.01$, is there evidence that the new process increases mean lifetime by more than 25 hours?

Which tool: two means, population $\sigma$'s known → two-sample $z$. The twist: the hypothesised difference is $d_0 = 25$, not 0. "More than 25" → right-tailed.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} H_0 &: \mu_{\text{new}} - \mu_{\text{old}} \le 25 \\[4pt] H_1 &: \mu_{\text{new}} - \mu_{\text{old}} > 25 \\[8pt] z &= \frac{(\bar{x}_{\text{new}} - \bar{x}_{\text{old}}) - 25}{\sqrt{\frac{30^2}{25} + \frac{40^2}{16}}} \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} z &= \frac{50 - 25}{\sqrt{36 + 100}} = \frac{25}{11.66} = 2.144 \\[6pt] z_{0.01} &= 2.326 \\[6pt] p &= P(Z > 2.144) \approx 0.016 \end{aligned}$$

$2.144 < 2.326$ (and $p = 0.016 > 0.01$): fail to reject at the 1% level. It would be significant at 5%.

Note on the tutorial notes: they give the p-value as 0.1603. The correct value is 0.016 (a misplaced decimal). The conclusion is unchanged.

T3 Q2: Which Solution Gives More Defect-Free Units?

Tutorial 3 · Q2
Problem Statement

Solution A: 253 of 300 units defect-free. Solution B: 196 of 300. (a) Test whether the defect-free proportions differ ($\alpha = 0.05$). (b) Give a 95% CI for $p_A - p_B$.

Which tool: two proportions → two-proportion $z$-test with the pooled SE; CI with the unpooled SE.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} \hat{p}_A &= \tfrac{253}{300} = 0.8433, \quad \hat{p}_B = \tfrac{196}{300} = 0.6533 \\[6pt] \hat{p} &= \tfrac{449}{600} = 0.7483 \\[6pt] H_0 &: p_A = p_B, \quad H_1: p_A \ne p_B \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} SE_{\text{pooled}} &= \sqrt{0.7483(0.2517)\tfrac{2}{300}} = 0.0354 \\[6pt] z &= \frac{0.19}{0.0354} = 5.36, \quad p < 0.0001 \\[10pt] SE_{\text{unpooled}} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{0.1321}{300} + \tfrac{0.2265}{300}} = 0.0346 \\[6pt] \text{CI} &= 0.19 \pm 1.96(0.0346) \\[4pt] &= 0.19 \pm 0.068 \;\Rightarrow\; (0.122,\; 0.258) \end{aligned}$$

Reject $H_0$. Solution A's defect-free rate is between about 12 and 26 percentage points higher. The interval excludes 0, consistent with rejecting.

Note: the tutorial notes write the lower limit as 0.132; it is 0.122.

T3 Q3: Failure Stress Under Two Conditions

Tutorial 3 · Q3
Problem Statement

Failure stress under condition 1: $n_1 = 9$, $\bar{x}_1 = 16.356$, $s_1 = 2.070$. Under condition 2: $n_2 = 6$, $\bar{x}_2 = 11.483$, $s_2 = 2.370$. (a) What assumptions are needed for inference? (b) Find a 99% CI for $\mu_1 - \mu_2$.

Which tool: small samples, $\sigma$'s unknown, no reason to assume equal variances → Welch interval: $(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2,\nu}\cdot SE$.
Set it up

(a) Assumptions: independence (the two samples are independent of each other, and observations within each sample are independent) and normality (failure stress under each condition is normally distributed; with $n = 9$ and $6$ the CLT can't rescue us).

$$\begin{aligned} SE &= \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} \\[6pt] \nu &= \text{Welch df (formula in T2 Q1)} \\[6pt] \text{CI} &= (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{0.005,\,\nu}\cdot SE \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 &= 4.872 \\[6pt] SE &= \sqrt{0.476 + 0.936} = 1.188 \\[6pt] \nu &\approx 9.79 \\[6pt] t_{0.005,\,9.79} &\approx 3.18 \\[6pt] \text{MOE} &= 3.18 \times 1.188 = 3.78 \\[6pt] \text{CI} &\approx (1.09,\; 8.66) \end{aligned}$$

We are 99% confident that mean failure stress under condition 1 exceeds condition 2 by between about 1.1 and 8.7 units. The tutorial notes use 3.169 (the value for 10 df) and get (1.11, 8.64); essentially the same.

T3 Q4: Two Vendors, Equal Variances, Different Means?

Tutorial 3 · Q4
Problem Statement

Resistance of parts from vendor 1: $n_1 = 25$, $\bar{x}_1 = 98$, $s_1 = 2.1$. Vendor 2: $n_2 = 35$, $\bar{x}_2 = 104$, $s_2 = 1.9$. (1) Test whether the variances are equal. (2) Test whether the mean resistances differ. Use $\alpha = 0.05$.

Which tool: a two-step problem. Step 1 is an $F$-test; its result decides step 2: pooled $t$ if variances look equal, Welch if not.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} F &= \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}, \quad \text{df} = (24,\; 34) \\[8pt] s_p^2 &= \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2} \\[8pt] t &= \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}, \quad \text{df} = 58 \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} F &= \frac{4.41}{3.61} = 1.222 \\[6pt] p_F &= 2\min\{0.709,\; 0.291\} = 0.582 \end{aligned}$$

Fail to reject equal variances → use the pooled $t$-test.

$$\begin{aligned} s_p^2 &= \frac{24(4.41) + 34(3.61)}{58} = 3.941 \\[6pt] s_p &= 1.985 \\[6pt] SE &= 1.985\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{25} + \tfrac{1}{35}} = 0.520 \\[6pt] t &= \frac{98 - 104}{0.520} = -11.54 \end{aligned}$$

p-value is essentially 0. Reject $H_0$: the mean resistances differ, with vendor 2 higher.

Be careful with wording: failing to reject equal variances doesn't prove they are equal; it means the data give no reason to doubt it.

T3 Q5: Blood Pressure Before and After

Tutorial 3 · Q5
Problem Statement

15 patients' blood pressure is measured before and after a drug. The mean reduction is $\bar{d} = 10$ mmHg and the SD of the reductions is $s_d = 4$ mmHg. At $\alpha = 0.01$, does the drug reduce blood pressure?

Which tool: the same people measured twice → paired $t$-test. Work with the 15 differences as if they were one sample; the two raw samples are not independent, so a two-sample test would be wrong.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} H_0 &: \mu_d = 0, \quad H_1: \mu_d > 0 \\[6pt] t &= \frac{\bar{d} - 0}{s_d/\sqrt{n}}, \quad \text{df} = n - 1 = 14 \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} t &= \frac{10}{4/\sqrt{15}} = \frac{10}{1.033} = 9.68 \\[6pt] t_{0.01,\,14} &= 2.624 \\[6pt] p &\approx 7 \times 10^{-8} \end{aligned}$$

Reject $H_0$: strong evidence the drug reduces blood pressure.

Note: the notes label the critical value "$t_{0.995,14}$". For a one-sided test at $\alpha = 0.01$ you need the 99th percentile, $t_{0.99,14} = 2.624$; the number used is right but the label is wrong ($t_{0.995,14}$ is 2.977).

T3 Q6: Are Two Variances Equal?

Tutorial 3 · Q6
Problem Statement

Sample 1: $n = 10$, $\bar{x} = 50.2$, $s = 6.3$. Sample 2: $n = 8$, $\bar{x} = 48.1$, $s = 4.6$. At $\alpha = 0.01$, test whether the population variances are equal.

Which tool: compare variances → $F$-test. The means are a distraction here.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} H_0 &: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2, \quad H_1: \sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2 \\[6pt] F &= \frac{6.3^2}{4.6^2}, \quad \text{df} = (9,\; 7) \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} F &= \frac{39.69}{21.16} = 1.876 \\[6pt] p &= 2\min\{P(F \le 1.876),\; P(F \ge 1.876)\} \approx 0.42 \end{aligned}$$

$0.42 > 0.01$: fail to reject. No evidence the variances differ.

T3 Q7: Maricopa vs Pima

Tutorial 3 · Q7
Problem Statement

In Maricopa county, 385 of 500 sampled people have a certain characteristic; in Pima county, 267 of 400. At 95% confidence, test whether the proportions differ and give a CI for the difference.

Which tool: same as T3 Q2: two-proportion $z$, pooled SE for the test and unpooled for the CI. $\alpha = 1 - \text{confidence} = 0.05$.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} \hat{p}_1 &= 0.770, \quad \hat{p}_2 = 0.6675 \\[6pt] \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 &= 0.1025 \\[6pt] \hat{p} &= \tfrac{652}{900} = 0.7244 \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} SE_{\text{pooled}} &= \sqrt{0.7244(0.2756)\big(\tfrac{1}{500} + \tfrac{1}{400}\big)} = 0.0300 \\[6pt] z &= \frac{0.1025}{0.0300} = 3.42, \quad p \approx 0.0006 \\[10pt] SE_{\text{unpooled}} &= \sqrt{\tfrac{0.77(0.23)}{500} + \tfrac{0.6675(0.3325)}{400}} = 0.0302 \\[6pt] \text{CI} &= 0.1025 \pm 1.96(0.0302) \\[4pt] &= 0.1025 \pm 0.059 \;\Rightarrow\; (0.043,\; 0.162) \end{aligned}$$

Reject $H_0$: the proportion is between about 4.3 and 16.2 percentage points higher in Maricopa. (The notes write the interval with the endpoints in reverse order; the lower limit comes first.)

T3 Q8: Repeatability of Assembly Times

Tutorial 3 · Q8
Problem Statement

Assembly times: men $n_1 = 25$, $s_1 = 0.98$; women $n_2 = 21$, $s_2 = 1.02$. At $\alpha = 0.02$, is there a difference in repeatability?

Which tool: repeatability means how consistent repeated measurements are: high repeatability = low variability. So "difference in repeatability" is a question about variances → $F$-test.
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} H_0 &: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2, \quad H_1: \sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2 \\[6pt] F &= \frac{0.98^2}{1.02^2}, \quad \text{df} = (24,\; 20) \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} F &= \frac{0.9604}{1.0404} = 0.923 \\[6pt] p &\approx 0.84 \end{aligned}$$

Critical values at $\alpha = 0.02$ are $F_{0.99} = 2.86$ and $F_{0.01} = 0.365$; 0.923 lies between them. Fail to reject: no significant difference in repeatability. An $F$ close to 1 always gives a large p-value.

T3 Q9: Do the Flower Colours Fit the Expected Ratio?

Tutorial 3 · Q9
Problem Statement

Observed flower colours: red 62, white 38, pink 23. Expected under the theory: red 60, white 40, pink 23. At $\alpha = 0.05$, do the data fit?

Which tool: one categorical variable compared against a claimed distribution → $\chi^2$ goodness-of-fit test. Same $\sum (O - E)^2/E$ statistic as the independence test, but df = (number of categories − 1).
Set it up $$\begin{aligned} H_0 &: \text{the colours follow the expected distribution} \\[6pt] \chi^2 &= \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}, \quad \text{df} = 3 - 1 = 2 \end{aligned}$$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \chi^2 &= \frac{(62-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(38-40)^2}{40} + \frac{0^2}{23} \\[6pt] &= 0.067 + 0.100 + 0 = 0.167 \\[6pt] \chi^2_{0.05,\,2} &= 5.99 \\[6pt] p &\approx 0.92 \end{aligned}$$

Fail to reject: the observed colours are consistent with the expected distribution.

T3 Q10: Is Nausea Independent of Drug Type?

Problem Statement
NauseaNo nauseaTotal
Drug A154560
Drug B103040

At $\alpha = 0.05$, is nausea independent of drug type?

Which tool: two categorical variables in a contingency table → $\chi^2$ test of independence with $E = \frac{\text{row total} \times \text{column total}}{\text{grand total}}$.
Set it up $$E_{A,\text{nausea}} = \frac{60 \times 25}{100} = 15$$

Compute the other three expected counts the same way and compare with the observed table.

Check your work

Expected counts: A: 15 and 45; B: 10 and 30. They equal the observed counts exactly.

Connecting the Dots

Look across the three tutorials and the same few ideas carry every problem. Bayes (T1 Q1), Markov chains (T1 Q9), the conditional probability in T2 Q5 and the error probability in T2 Q3 all come from conditioning and the law of total probability. Linearity of expectation does the heavy lifting in the joint-density expectation (T1 Q3), the hat-check problem (T1 Q5) and Bessel's correction (T2 Q6). And every test in tutorials 2 and 3 is the same move: an estimate minus its hypothesised value, divided by a standard error, compared to a known distribution.
