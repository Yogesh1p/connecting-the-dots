A mini-quiz gives you about fifteen minutes and a box for each answer. There is no time to rediscover a method. You have to recognise, within a few seconds of reading, which tool the question wants, and then run it without slipping. These four quizzes from 2024 are good training for exactly that, because together they touch almost every tool in the course: counting, conditioning, Bayes, distributions of functions of random variables, Markov chains, the multivariate normal, inequalities and even constrained optimisation.

Each problem below is set out the same way. First the question as it appeared on the paper. Then the tools it needs, with every term defined. Then a short set-up that gets you started. The full worked solution stays hidden in Check your work, so try it yourself first. (Two of the papers carry the CS6015 header; they are included because the questions are the same material.)

Before writing anything, ask: what is random, what do I know about it, and which single tool turns that into the number the box wants?

Mini-Quiz 4 (MQ5): Counting, Conditioning and Bayes

Counting Tools
  • Equally likely outcomes: if every outcome in the sample space $S$ is equally likely, then $P(E) = \dfrac{|E|}{|S|}$, where $|E|$ is the number of outcomes in $E$.
  • Multiplication rule: if a choice is made in stages, with $m_1$ options at stage 1, $m_2$ at stage 2, and so on, the number of complete choices is $m_1 \times m_2 \times \cdots$.
  • Combinations: $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ ("n choose k") counts the ways to pick $k$ items from $n$ when order does not matter.

A 5-card hand is an unordered set of 5 cards from 52, so $|S| = \binom{52}{5}$, and every hand is equally likely. Every poker question is then "count the hands of this type, divide by $\binom{52}{5}$". Leave the answer as a ratio of combinations; there is no need to multiply out. The counting trick is to build one hand of the type in stages: first choose the ranks, then choose the suits.

Problem 1: Poker Hands

MQ5 · Q1 · 4 points

You are dealt 5 cards from the top of a thoroughly shuffled deck of 52 cards – 4 suits (Spades, Clubs, Hearts, and Diamonds) and 13 cards with values or ranks ranging from 2 to 10 plus J,Q,K,A per suit. A card is denoted as {suit}{value}. E.g. DK corresponding to the King in the Diamonds suit. A always has a value/rank above K for simplicity. You may write your answer as a ratio of product of integers.

Set it up

For each type, ask: how many ways to choose the rank(s)? Then how many ways to choose the suits for those ranks? Multiply the stages together and divide by $\binom{52}{5}$.

i.
What is the probability that your hand is a Royal Flush? (Royal flush corresponds to you getting A,K,Q,J,10 from the same suit. e.g. SA, SK, SQ, SJ, S10 ).
Check your work

The ranks are fixed (A, K, Q, J, 10), so only the suit is a choice: 4 hands.

$$P = \frac{4}{\binom{52}{5}}$$
ii.
What is the probability that your hand is a Straight Flush ?(Straight flush corresponds to you getting 5 consecutive ranks from the same suit, that is not a Royal Flush. e.g. C8, C7, C6, C5, C4).
Check your work

A run of 5 can start at 2, 3, …, 10 (A is only high), so 9 runs per suit. Remove the royal run (10 to A): 8 per suit.

$$P = \frac{4 \times 8}{\binom{52}{5}}$$
iii.
What is the probability that your hand is a Four of Kind ? (Four of a kind corresponds to 4 out of the five cards being the same value, and the fifth can be any card. e.g. H3,D3,C3,S3,HJ)
Check your work $$P = \frac{\overbrace{13}^{\text{rank}}\;\overbrace{\binom{4}{4}}^{\text{all 4 suits}}\;\overbrace{\binom{48}{1}}^{\text{fifth card}}}{\binom{52}{5}}$$
iv.
What is the probability that your hand is a Full House ? (Full House corresponds to 3 out of the 5 cards having same value and the other two also have same value. e.g S4, C4, H4, H6,C6. ). (4 points)
Check your work $$P = \frac{\overbrace{13\binom{4}{3}}^{\text{the triple}}\;\overbrace{12\binom{4}{2}}^{\text{the pair}}}{\binom{52}{5}}$$

The triple's rank and the pair's rank play different roles, so choose them in order: 13 options, then 12. Using $\binom{13}{2}$ instead would undercount by half.

Conditional Probability and Three Relations Between Events
  • Conditional probability: $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$. Knowing $B$ happened shrinks the sample space to $B$.
  • Mutually exclusive: $A$ and $B$ cannot both happen, $A \cap B = \varnothing$.
  • Exhaustive: between them they cover every outcome, $A \cup B = S$. At least one must happen.
  • Independent: knowing one does not change the chance of the other, $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$.

With three coins there are only $2^3 = 8$ equally likely outcomes, so the safest method is to write them all down and mark which events each one belongs to. Every question then becomes counting rows.

Problem 2: Three Coins, Conditioned

MQ5 · Q2 · 3 points

3 fair coins are thrown independently. Consider the five events A, B, C, D and E below. A is the event that at least one of the coins show heads. B is the event that at least one of the coins show tails. C is the event that 2 or more coins show heads. D is the event that 2 or more coins show tails. E is the event that all three coins show the same face. Give the probabilities of the following: $P(A)P(A|B)$, $P(A|C)$, $P(A|D)$, $P(A|E)$. . (3 points)

Set it up

For $P(A \mid X)$, restrict the table to the rows where X has a tick, and count how many of those rows also have A ticked.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(A) &= \tfrac{7}{8} \\[8pt] P(A \mid B) &= \frac{|A \cap B|}{|B|} = \frac{6}{7} \\[8pt] P(A \mid C) &= \frac{4}{4} = 1 \quad (C \subset A) \\[8pt] P(A \mid D) &= \frac{3}{4} \quad (\text{only TTT in } D \text{ has no head}) \\[8pt] P(A \mid E) &= \frac{1}{2} \quad (E = \{HHH, TTT\}) \end{aligned}$$

For $P(A \mid B)$: B has 7 rows (everything except HHH), and among them A fails only on TTT, leaving 6.

Problem 2′: Exclusive, Exhaustive, Independent

MQ5 · Q2 (variant) · 3 points

3 fair coins are thrown independently. (The probability function, is given by the counting function with $P(S) = |S|/8$). Consider the five events A, B, C, D and E below. A is the event that at least one of the coins show heads. B is the event that at least one of the coins show tails. C is the event that 2 or more coins show heads. D is the event that 2 or more coins show tails. E is the event that all three coins show the same face.

(3 points)

Set it up

Use the table of 8 outcomes above. Exclusive: no row ticks both. Exhaustive: every row ticks at least one. Independent: (rows ticking both)/8 equals (count₁/8)(count₂/8).

i.
Give all pairs of events (among the 5 choose 2 pairs) that are mutually exclusive.
Check your work

{C, D} only. No outcome has both 2+ heads and 2+ tails with only 3 coins. Every other pair shares at least one row (A and E share HHH, B and E share TTT, and so on).

ii.
Give all pairs of events (among the 5 choose 2 pairs) that are exhaustive.
Check your work

{A, B}, {A, D}, {A, E}, {B, C}, {B, E}, {C, D}.

Quick check: A misses only TTT, so A is exhaustive with any event containing TTT (B, D, E). B misses only HHH, so B pairs with C and E (B with A is already listed). C and D split the 8 rows between them. The pairs that fail: {A, C} misses TTT, {B, D} misses HHH, {C, E} misses HTT, {D, E} misses HHT.

iii.
Give all pairs of events (among the 5 choose 2 pairs) that are independent.
Check your work

{C, E}, {D, E}.

$$\begin{aligned} P(C \cap E) &= P(\{HHH\}) = \tfrac18 \\[6pt] P(C)\,P(E) &= \tfrac12 \times \tfrac14 = \tfrac18 \;\checkmark \end{aligned}$$

D with E works the same way through TTT. Every other pair fails, e.g. $P(A \cap B) = \tfrac68$ but $P(A)P(B) = \tfrac{49}{64}$. Exclusive events with positive probability, like C and D, are never independent: knowing one happened tells you the other did not.

Bayes' Theorem with Total Probability
$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c)\,P(D^c)}$$

$D$ = has the disease, $D^c$ = does not. The true positive rate (sensitivity) is $P(+ \mid D)$. The false positive rate is $P(+ \mid D^c)$. The prevalence $P(D)$ is the prior.

Problem 3: Reading a COVID Test

MQ5 · Q3 · 2 + 2 points

COVID is present in a given population with a probability of 0.01. One person is chosen at random from this population and administered an RT-PCR test. The PCR test has a true positive rate of 96% and a false positive rate of 2% – i.e. on truly COVID infected people the test correctly shows positive with probability 0.96 and on people with no COVID it wrongly outputs positive with probability 0.02.

Set it up

Draw the four branches: infected & positive, infected & negative, healthy & positive, healthy & negative. The false negative rate is $1 - 0.96 = 0.04$ and the true negative rate is $1 - 0.02 = 0.98$.

i.
Given that the chosen person had a positive test result, what is the probability that they truly have COVID?
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(D \mid +) &= \frac{0.96 \times 0.01}{0.96 \times 0.01 + 0.02 \times 0.99} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.0096}{0.0294} \\[6pt] &\approx 0.327 \end{aligned}$$

Only a third of positives are real: healthy people outnumber infected ones 99 to 1, so even a 2% false alarm rate produces twice as many false positives as true ones.

ii.
Given that the chosen person had a negative test result, what is the probability that they truly have COVID? (2+2 points)
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(D \mid -) &= \frac{0.04 \times 0.01}{0.04 \times 0.01 + 0.98 \times 0.99} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.0004}{0.9706} \\[6pt] &\approx 0.00041 \end{aligned}$$

A negative result is very reassuring.

Problem 4: min, max and Sum of Two Uniforms

MQ6 · Q1 · 4 points

Let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be two independent, discrete random variables that are uniformly distributed over $\{-3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$. Give the PMF of the following random variables:

The CDF Trick for max and min

"The max is at most $y$" means "both are at most $y$". "The min is at least $w$" means "both are at least $w$". With independence, "both" is a product:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\max \le y) &= P(X_1 \le y)\,P(X_2 \le y) \\[6pt] P(\min \ge w) &= P(X_1 \ge w)\,P(X_2 \ge w) \end{aligned}$$

Then get the PMF by differencing: $P(Y = y) = P(Y \le y) - P(Y \le y - 1)$.

Set it up

There are $7 \times 7 = 49$ equally likely pairs. $P(X_1 \ge w) = \frac{4 - w}{7}$ and $P(X_1 \le y) = \frac{y + 4}{7}$. For the sum, picture the 7 × 7 grid of pairs: the pairs with a fixed sum lie on one diagonal.

i.
$W = \min(X_1, X_2)$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(W = w) &= P(W \ge w) - P(W \ge w + 1) \\[6pt] &= \frac{(4-w)^2 - (3-w)^2}{49} \\[6pt] &= \frac{7 - 2w}{49}, \quad w = -3, \dots, 3 \end{aligned}$$

ii.
$Y = \max(X_1, X_2)$
Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(Y = y) &= P(Y \le y) - P(Y \le y - 1) \\[6pt] &= \frac{(y+4)^2 - (y+3)^2}{49} \\[6pt] &= \frac{2y + 7}{49}, \quad y = -3, \dots, 3 \end{aligned}$$

It is the min's PMF mirrored, as symmetry demands: $\max(X_1, X_2) = -\min(-X_1, -X_2)$.

Check your work $$P(Z = z) = \frac{7 - |z|}{49}, \quad z = -6, \dots, 6$$

A triangle: 1/49 at $\pm 6$ rising to 7/49 at 0, because the main diagonal of the 7 × 7 grid has 7 cells and the corner diagonals have 1. Check: $7 + 2(6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1) = 49$.

Law of Total Expectation (First-Step Analysis)

If $A$ and $A^c$ split the possibilities, then

$$E[X] = E[X \mid A]\,P(A) + E[X \mid A^c]\,P(A^c)$$

For "how long until…" problems, condition on what happens in the first step. If the first step sends you back to the start, the remaining wait looks exactly like the original, so $E[X]$ appears on both sides of the equation, and you solve for it.

Problem 5: Waiting for Two 6s in a Row

MQ6 · Q2 · 4 points

A fair 6-sided die is thrown repeatedly till you get two consecutive 6s. Let $X$ be the number of times the dice is thrown. What is $E[X]$? Give some steps.

(Hint: Let $A$ denote the event that the first roll is a 6. Think about what is $E[X|A^c]$?. Express both $E[X]$ and $E[X|A]$ in terms of $E[X]$ and $E[X|A]$ and solve for both.)

Set it up
  • If the first roll is not a 6, one throw is wasted and you are back at the start: $E[X \mid A^c] = 1 + E[X]$.
  • If it is a 6, look at the second roll. Another 6 finishes in exactly 2 throws. Anything else wastes 2 throws and resets.
Check your work

Write $E = E[X]$ and $E_A = E[X \mid A]$.

$$\begin{aligned} E_A &= \tfrac16 \cdot 2 + \tfrac56\,(2 + E) \\[6pt] &= 2 + \tfrac56 E \\[10pt] E &= \tfrac16\,E_A + \tfrac56\,(1 + E) \\[6pt] &= \tfrac16\left(2 + \tfrac56 E\right) + \tfrac56 + \tfrac56 E \\[6pt] &= \tfrac76 + \tfrac{35}{36}E \\[10pt] \tfrac{1}{36}E &= \tfrac76 \\[6pt] E &= 42 \\[6pt] E_A &= 2 + 35 = 37 \end{aligned}$$

Compare: waiting for a single 6 takes $1/p = 6$ throws on average. Needing two in a row is far more than double, because every near-miss sends you back to the beginning.

Markov Chain and Stationary Distribution
  • A Markov chain is a sequence $X_0, X_1, \dots$ where the next state depends only on the current state, not on the history.
  • The transition matrix here is written column-wise: $T_{ij} = P(X_t = i \mid X_{t-1} = j)$. Each column sums to 1, and if $p_t$ is the column vector of state probabilities at time $t$, then $p_t = T p_{t-1}$.
  • A stationary distribution $\pi$ is one that does not change after a step: $T\pi = \pi$, with $\pi_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$. In linear-algebra terms, $\pi$ is the eigenvector of $T$ for eigenvalue 1, scaled to sum to 1, the same object behind PageRank.

Problem 6: A Three-State Chain

MQ6 · Q3 · 4 points

Consider a Markov chain, where at each timestep $t$ each $X_t$ takes values in $\{1, 2, 3\}$, with the following transition probability matrix

$$T = \begin{pmatrix} 1/3 & 1/3 & 0 \\ 2/3 & 1/3 & 2/3 \\ 0 & 1/3 & 1/3 \end{pmatrix}$$

where $T_{ij} = P(X_t = i|X_{t-1} = j)$. What is the stationary distribution of this Markov chain?

Set it up

Write $T\pi = \pi$ one row at a time. Row 1 and row 3 each involve only two unknowns, so they give ratios directly. Then normalise.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} \text{Row 1: } \tfrac13\pi_1 + \tfrac13\pi_2 &= \pi_1 \;\Rightarrow\; \pi_2 = 2\pi_1 \\[8pt] \text{Row 3: } \tfrac13\pi_2 + \tfrac13\pi_3 &= \pi_3 \;\Rightarrow\; \pi_2 = 2\pi_3 \\[8pt] \pi_1 + 2\pi_1 + \pi_1 &= 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \pi_1 = \tfrac14 \\[8pt] \pi &= \left(\tfrac14,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac14\right) \end{aligned}$$

Check row 2: $\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac12$. ✓ The middle state gets half the time, since both outer states feed into it with probability 2/3.

Mini-Quiz 7 (MQ7): Continuous Transformations and Dependence

Survival Function and the Exponential

The survival function is $P(X > x) = 1 - F(x)$, the probability of lasting beyond $x$. For $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ (density $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, $x \ge 0$; see Common Probability Distributions), it is simply $P(X > x) = e^{-\lambda x}$.

Problem 7: The First of Two Exponential Clocks

MQ7 · Q1 · 4 points

Let $X, Y$ be independent random variables with distribution as exponential with parameter $\lambda$. Let $Z = \min(X, Y)$. Give the probability density function of $Z$.

Set it up

"The minimum is bigger than $z$" means both are bigger than $z$. Use the survival function, then differentiate.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(Z > z) &= P(X > z)\,P(Y > z) \\[6pt] &= e^{-\lambda z} \cdot e^{-\lambda z} \\[6pt] &= e^{-2\lambda z} \\[8pt] F_Z(z) &= 1 - e^{-2\lambda z} \\[6pt] f_Z(z) &= 2\lambda e^{-2\lambda z}, \quad z \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

So $Z \sim \text{Exp}(2\lambda)$: two independent clocks running together ring at the combined rate. In general, the minimum of exponentials with rates $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots$ is exponential with rate $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \cdots$.

The CDF Method for $Y = g(X)$
$$\begin{aligned} F_Y(y) &= P(g(X) \le y) \\[6pt] &= P\big(X \le g^{-1}(y)\big) \quad (g \text{ increasing}) \\[6pt] f_Y(y) &= \frac{d}{dy}F_Y(y) \end{aligned}$$

Always work out the range of $Y$ first: it is where $g$ sends the range of $X$.

Problem 8: Exponentiating a Uniform

MQ7 · Q2 · 4 points

Let $X$ be continuous random variable distributed as $\text{Unif}(-1, 1)$. Let $Y = e^X$. Give the probability density function of $Y$ and plot it approximately.

Set it up

$X$ ranges over $(-1, 1)$, so $Y = e^X$ ranges over $(e^{-1}, e)$. The CDF of $X$ is $\frac{x + 1}{2}$ on that interval. $e^x$ is increasing, so $e^X \le y$ is the same as $X \le \ln y$.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} F_Y(y) &= P(X \le \ln y) \\[6pt] &= \frac{\ln y + 1}{2} \\[8pt] f_Y(y) &= \frac{1}{2y}, \quad \frac1e \le y \le e \end{aligned}$$

Check it integrates to 1: $\int_{1/e}^{e} \frac{1}{2y}\,dy = \frac12\big(1 - (-1)\big) = 1$. ✓

Covariance, Independence and the Multivariate Normal
  • Covariance: $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = E[X_1 X_2] - E[X_1]\,E[X_2]$. It measures linear association only.
  • Independent means the joint distribution factors, $P(X_1 \in A, X_2 \in B) = P(X_1 \in A)\,P(X_2 \in B)$ for all sets. Independence implies zero covariance, but zero covariance does not imply independence.
  • Multivariate normal (MVN): a vector is MVN when every linear combination $a_1 X_1 + a_2 X_2$ is normally distributed. Having normal marginals is not enough.

Problem 9: A Random Sign Flip

MQ7 · Q3 · 4 points

Let $X_1$ be a random variable distributed distributed as a Gaussian with mean 0 and variance 1. Let $Z$ be an independent discrete random variable taking values $+1$ and $-1$ with equal probability. Let $X_2 = Z * X_1$.

i.
How is $X_2$ distributed?
Check your work

Condition on $Z$ and use the symmetry of $N(0, 1)$, i.e. $-X_1$ has the same distribution as $X_1$.

$$\begin{aligned} P(X_2 \le x) &= \tfrac12 P(X_1 \le x) + \tfrac12 P(-X_1 \le x) \\[6pt] &= \tfrac12\Phi(x) + \tfrac12\Phi(x) \\[6pt] &= \Phi(x) \end{aligned}$$

So $X_2 \sim N(0, 1)$.

ii.
What is the covariance between $X_1$ and $X_2$ ?
Check your work

Write $X_1 X_2 = Z X_1^2$ and use the independence of $Z$ and $X_1$.

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) &= E[Z X_1^2] - E[X_1]\,E[X_2] \\[6pt] &= E[Z]\,E[X_1^2] - 0 \\[6pt] &= 0 \times 1 = 0 \end{aligned}$$
iii.
Are $X_1$ and $X_2$ independent?
Check your work

No. $|X_2| = |X_1|$ always, so knowing $X_1$ pins $X_2$ down to two values. For example, $P(|X_2| > 2 \mid |X_1| < 1) = 0$, but $P(|X_2| > 2) \approx 0.046$.

iv.
If you have several independent samples of $(X_1, X_2)$ how would the scatter plot of the points look like? Draw an approximate diagram.
Check your work

All points fall on the two diagonals $x_2 = x_1$ and $x_2 = -x_1$, roughly half on each, bunched near the origin.

v.
Is the vector $X = [X_1, X_2]$ distributed according to the multivariate normal distribution?
Check your work

No. A vector is multivariate normal only if every linear combination is normal. But $X_1 + X_2 = (1 + Z)X_1$ equals exactly 0 with probability ½ (whenever $Z = -1$), and is $2X_1$ otherwise. A distribution with a point mass of ½ at 0 is not normal.

This is the classic warning: normal marginals plus zero covariance do not give independence. That implication holds only when the pair is jointly normal.

Mini-Quiz 8 (MQ8): Inequalities, Gaussian Conditioning and KKT

Markov's Inequality

If $X \ge 0$ and $a > 0$, then

$$P(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}$$

Why: $X \ge a \cdot \mathbf{1}\{X \ge a\}$ always (the right side is $a$ when $X \ge a$ and 0 otherwise). Take expectations of both sides: $E[X] \ge a\,P(X \ge a)$. The bound is tight only when $X$ puts all its mass at 0 and at $a$.

Problem 10: How Likely Is 20 Times the Mean?

MQ8 · Q1 · 4 points

Let $X$ be a positive random variable with $E[X] = 10$. Give an upper bound on the probability of the event $X > 200$. Give an example distribution for $X$ where this bound is satisfied with equality.

Set it up

All you know is the mean and the sign, which is exactly Markov's setting. For equality, put as much mass as possible at 200 while keeping the mean at 10, and put the rest at the lowest allowed value.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} P(X > 200) \le P(X \ge 200) &\le \frac{10}{200} \\[6pt] &= 0.05 \end{aligned}$$

Equality example: $X = 200$ with probability $0.05$, and $X = 0$ with probability $0.95$. Then $E[X] = 0.05 \times 200 = 10$ and $P(X \ge 200) = 0.05$.

A fine point worth one line in your answer: with the strict inequality "$X > 200$" and a strictly positive $X$, the bound can be approached but not reached exactly. The intended answer is the two-point distribution above, which gives equality for $P(X \ge 200)$.

Conditioning a Bivariate Normal

If $(X_1, X_2)$ is bivariate normal with means $\mu_1, \mu_2$, variances $\sigma_{11}, \sigma_{22}$ and covariance $\sigma_{12}$, then $X_2 \mid X_1 = a$ is normal with

$$\begin{aligned} E[X_2 \mid X_1 = a] &= \mu_2 + \frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{11}}(a - \mu_1) \\[8pt] \operatorname{Var}(X_2 \mid X_1 = a) &= \sigma_{22} - \frac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_{11}} \end{aligned}$$

Read it as a regression: the mean shifts by the slope $\sigma_{12}/\sigma_{11}$ times how far $a$ is from its average, and the variance shrinks by the part of $X_2$ that $X_1$ explains. Note the variance does not depend on $a$.

Problem 11: Conditioning a 2D Gaussian

MQ8 · Q2 · 4 points

Let $X = [X_1, X_2]$ be a 2-dimensional multivariate normal vector with mean parameter $\mu = [2, 3]$ and covariance parameter $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 3 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$. Give the distribution of $X_2$ conditioned on $X_1 = a$ for some $a \in \mathbb{R}$.

Set it up

Read off $\mu_1 = 2$, $\mu_2 = 3$, $\sigma_{11} = 4$, $\sigma_{12} = 3$, $\sigma_{22} = 9$ and plug into the two formulas.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} E[X_2 \mid X_1 = a] &= 3 + \frac{3}{4}(a - 2) \\[6pt] &= \frac{3}{4}a + \frac{3}{2} \\[8pt] \operatorname{Var}(X_2 \mid X_1 = a) &= 9 - \frac{3^2}{4} \\[6pt] &= \frac{27}{4} \\[8pt] X_2 \mid X_1 = a \;&\sim\; N\!\left(\tfrac34 a + \tfrac32,\ \tfrac{27}{4}\right) \end{aligned}$$

The correlation is $\rho = 3/\sqrt{4 \cdot 9} = 0.5$, and the variance shrinks by the factor $1 - \rho^2 = 0.75$: $9 \times 0.75 = 6.75$. ✓

KKT Conditions for $\min f(x)$ subject to $g_i(x) \le 0$

A point $x^*$ is a KKT point if there are multipliers $\mu_i$ (one per constraint) such that all four hold:

  1. Primal feasibility: $g_i(x^*) \le 0$ for every $i$.
  2. Stationarity: $\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_i \mu_i \nabla g_i(x^*) = 0$.
  3. Dual feasibility: $\mu_i \ge 0$ for every $i$.
  4. Complementary slackness: $\mu_i\,g_i(x^*) = 0$. A constraint that is not active (not satisfied with equality) must have $\mu_i = 0$.

Geometrically: at a constrained minimum, the downhill direction $-\nabla f$ points straight out of the feasible set, so it must be a non-negative combination of the outward normals $\nabla g_i$ of the active walls. For a convex problem, KKT is both necessary and sufficient for a global minimum.

Problem 12: Checking KKT on a Diamond

MQ8 · Q3 · 4 points

Consider the following optimisation problem over a 2-dimensional vector $x$ with 4 inequality constraints.

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\;\; & (x_1 - 2)^2 + (x_2 - 2)^2 \\[6pt] \text{s.t. }\;\; & x_1 + x_2 \le 1; \quad -x_1 - x_2 \le 1 \\[6pt] & x_1 - x_2 \le 1; \quad -x_1 + x_2 \le 1 \end{aligned}$$

Check the KKT conditions at the following 2 points: $x = [\tfrac12, \tfrac12]$ and $x = [1, 0]$. Show steps demonstrating how you checked the KKT conditions.

Set it up

The four constraints together say $|x_1| + |x_2| \le 1$, a diamond. Write $g_1 = x_1 + x_2 - 1$, $g_2 = -x_1 - x_2 - 1$, $g_3 = x_1 - x_2 - 1$, $g_4 = -x_1 + x_2 - 1$, so $\nabla g_1 = (1, 1)$, $\nabla g_2 = (-1, -1)$, $\nabla g_3 = (1, -1)$, $\nabla g_4 = (-1, 1)$. Also $\nabla f = \big(2(x_1 - 2),\ 2(x_2 - 2)\big)$. At each point: find the active constraints, set the others' $\mu$ to 0, solve stationarity, then check the signs.

Check your work

At $x = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$.

$$\begin{aligned} g &= (0,\ -2,\ -1,\ -1) \quad \Rightarrow \text{ feasible; only } g_1 \text{ active} \\[8pt] \nabla f &= (-3,\ -3) \\[8pt] (-3, -3) + \mu_1 (1, 1) &= (0, 0) \;\Rightarrow\; \mu_1 = 3 \\[8pt] \mu &= (3, 0, 0, 0) \end{aligned}$$

Feasible ✓, stationarity ✓ with $\mu_1 = 3 \ge 0$ ✓, complementary slackness ✓ (inactive constraints have $\mu = 0$). KKT holds. The problem is convex (convex objective, linear constraints), so this is the global minimum, with $f = 4.5$. It is simply the point of the diamond closest to $(2, 2)$.

At $x = (1, 0)$.

$$\begin{aligned} g &= (0,\ -2,\ 0,\ -2) \quad \Rightarrow \text{ feasible; } g_1, g_3 \text{ active} \\[8pt] \nabla f &= (-2,\ -4) \\[8pt] (-2, -4) + \mu_1(1, 1) + \mu_3(1, -1) &= (0, 0) \\[8pt] \mu_1 + \mu_3 &= 2 \\[6pt] \mu_1 - \mu_3 &= 4 \\[8pt] \mu_1 = 3, \quad \mu_3 &= -1 < 0 \end{aligned}$$

Stationarity can only be met with a negative multiplier, so dual feasibility fails. KKT does not hold. The negative $\mu_3$ says the wall $x_1 - x_2 \le 1$ is not actually holding you back: moving along the edge toward $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ lowers $f$ from 5 toward 4.5.

Connecting the Dots

Twelve problems, but only a handful of moves. Counting handles any finite, equally likely world: poker hands, three coins, the 49 pairs of two dice. Conditioning is the same act every time, restricting to what is known and renormalising: in a table of eight coin outcomes, in Bayes' theorem for a medical test, in the first-step equation for two 6s, and in the Gaussian formula, where conditioning on $X_1$ shifts the mean along a regression line. Going through the CDF handles any new random variable built from old ones: the max and min of dice, the min of exponentials, $e^X$. And linear algebra keeps returning: the stationary distribution is an eigenvector, the covariance matrix drives the conditional normal, and KKT asks whether one vector lies in the cone spanned by others.
