Two machines fill bags of chips. Both are set to 100 g and both have a standard deviation of 2 g. On paper they are identical. But the first one wobbles a little on every bag, while the second is almost perfect most of the time and every so often throws out a bag that is wildly off. If you sell chips, these are not the same machine at all: the second one is the one that gets you a complaint about a half-empty bag.

The mean tells you where a distribution sits. The variance tells you how spread out it is. Neither can tell those two machines apart. The number that can is the kurtosis. In the course slides it gets one line, "fat tailed", followed by a question: if I make the tails fatter, am I not just increasing the variance? This article answers that question properly: what kurtosis measures, why it is built from a fourth power, what a high kurtosis actually looks like, and the common myth that it measures how "peaked" a curve is.

If two distributions have the same mean and the same variance, what is left to tell them apart?

1. Same Mean, Same Variance, Different Story

Here are two small data sets, eight values each.

Data setValuesMeanVariance
A ("always a bit off")−1, −1, −1, −1, 1, 1, 1, 101
B ("usually perfect, sometimes far off")0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, −2, 201

Check the variance of B (we divide by $n$ here, treating the data as the whole population):

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma^2_B &= \frac{6 \times 0^2 + (-2)^2 + 2^2}{8} \\[6pt] &= \frac{8}{8} = 1 \end{aligned}$$

Every value in A is exactly one standard deviation from the mean. B has most values sitting on the mean and two values sitting two standard deviations away. Same centre, same spread, completely different shape. We need a summary that reacts to rare, far-away values much more strongly than the variance does.

2. The Definition: An Average of Fourth Powers

First remove the location and scale, so we compare shapes only. Each value becomes a z-score: how many standard deviations it lies from the mean, $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$. By construction $E[Z] = 0$ and $E[Z^2] = 1$ for every distribution, so the first two moments of $Z$ carry no information about shape. The third and fourth do.

Kurtosis and Excess Kurtosis

For a random variable $X$ with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the kurtosis is the fourth standardised moment:

$$\kappa = E\!\left[\left(\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^{4}\right] = \frac{E\big[(X-\mu)^4\big]}{\sigma^4}$$

The excess kurtosis is $\kappa - 3$, measured relative to the normal distribution, which has $\kappa = 3$. A distribution with $\kappa > 3$ is called leptokurtic (heavier tails than normal). One with $\kappa < 3$ is platykurtic (lighter tails). The normal itself is mesokurtic.

Apply it to the two data sets. Their z-scores are just the values themselves, since the mean is 0 and the standard deviation is 1:

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa_A &= \frac{8 \times 1^4}{8} \\[6pt] &= 1 \\[10pt] \kappa_B &= \frac{6 \times 0^4 + 2^4 + 2^4}{8} \\[6pt] &= \frac{32}{8} = 4 \end{aligned}$$

The variance could not tell them apart; the kurtosis separates them by a factor of four. All of B's kurtosis comes from its two far-away values. The six zeros contribute nothing.

Kurtosis has no units and ignores shifting and scaling. Because it is built from z-scores, $Y = a + bX$ (with $b \ne 0$) has exactly the same kurtosis as $X$. Weighing in grams or kilograms changes nothing. Kurtosis describes shape and nothing else.

3. Why a Fourth Power? Kurtosis Is a Tail Detector

Why raise to the fourth power, and what does that power do to values near the centre compared with values far out?

Look at what $z^4$ does to a few z-scores:

$z$0.512345
$z^2$ (used by variance)0.251491625
$z^4$ (used by kurtosis)0.062511681256625

Inside one standard deviation, the fourth power shrinks values toward zero. Outside it, the fourth power blows them up: a point 3 standard deviations out counts 81 times as much as a point at 1. So kurtosis is almost blind to what happens near the centre and extremely sensitive to what happens far away. That is the whole idea.

We can make "almost blind to the centre" exact. Split the average of $Z^4$ into the part from $|Z| \le 1$ and the part from $|Z| > 1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa &= E\big[Z^4 \,;\, |Z| \le 1\big] + E\big[Z^4 \,;\, |Z| > 1\big] \\[8pt] E\big[Z^4 \,;\, |Z| \le 1\big] &\le P(|Z| \le 1) \le 1 \end{aligned}$$

($E[Z^4; |Z| \le 1]$ means the average of $Z^4$ counting only outcomes with $|Z| \le 1$.) The second line holds because $z^4 \le 1$ whenever $|z| \le 1$. So the centre can contribute at most 1 to the kurtosis, however the distribution is shaped there. Everything above 1 must come from values more than one standard deviation from the mean. For the normal, the central part contributes only 0.11 of its 3.

This also gives the smallest possible kurtosis. Since $E[Z^4] \ge \big(E[Z^2]\big)^2 = 1$, every distribution has $\kappa \ge 1$. The minimum is reached by data set A, a fair coin coded as $\pm 1$: every value is exactly one standard deviation away, there is no tail at all, and $\kappa = 1$.

4. The Kurtosis of Familiar Distributions

To compare shapes fairly, give every distribution mean 0 and variance 1. Then $\kappa$ is just $E[Z^4]$.

Uniform: no tails at all

A uniform with variance 1 lives on $[-\sqrt3, \sqrt3]$ with density $\frac{1}{2\sqrt3}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt3}\int_{-\sqrt3}^{\sqrt3} z^4\,dz \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt3} \cdot \frac{2(\sqrt3)^5}{5} \\[6pt] &= \frac{(\sqrt3)^4}{5} = \frac{9}{5} = 1.8 \end{aligned}$$

Nothing can ever land beyond $1.73$ standard deviations, so the tails contribute nothing and $\kappa$ is low.

Normal: the benchmark 3

With $\varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}$, use $\varphi'(z) = -z\,\varphi(z)$ and integrate by parts:

$$\begin{aligned} E[Z^4] &= \int z^3 \cdot z\varphi(z)\,dz \\[6pt] &= \Big[-z^3\varphi(z)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int 3z^2\varphi(z)\,dz \\[6pt] &= 0 + 3\,E[Z^2] \\[6pt] &= 3 \end{aligned}$$

This is why "excess kurtosis" subtracts 3: it measures tails relative to the normal.

Laplace: exponential tails

The Laplace (double exponential) distribution has density $\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x|/b}$: two exponential distributions placed back to back. Its variance is $2b^2$ and its fourth central moment is $24b^4$, so

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa &= \frac{24b^4}{(2b^2)^2} \\[6pt] &= 6 \end{aligned}$$

The difference is in how fast the tails die: the normal's density falls like $e^{-z^2/2}$, the Laplace's only like $e^{-\sqrt2|z|}$. For large $z$ the square in the exponent wins by an enormous margin.

7. Fatter Tails, Not Just More Variance, and Not Peakedness

Now the slide's question can be answered.

"If I make the tails fatter, am I not just increasing the variance?"

Yes, if you only move mass outward. Kurtosis is measured after standardising, so the comparison is always made at a fixed variance. Think of the variance as a budget: $E[Z^2] = 1$. Moving some probability out to $|z| = 4$ spends a lot of that budget ($4^2 = 16$ per unit of mass). To stay at variance 1, the distribution has to save it elsewhere, by pulling other mass in from the shoulders toward the centre, where $z^2$ is cheap. The fourth power treats the two moves very differently. Say you move a sliver of mass $\delta$ out to $|z| = 4$: that costs $16\delta$ of variance and adds $256\delta$ to $\kappa$. To pay the $16\delta$ back, pull about $7\delta$ of mass in from the shoulder at $|z| = 1.5$ to the centre ($7 \times 1.5^2 \approx 16$). That removes only $7 \times 1.5^4 \approx 35\delta$ from $\kappa$. Net change: about $+221\delta$. Same variance, much larger kurtosis.

That is why high-kurtosis curves usually look tall and narrow in the middle, like the Laplace in Figure 1. The tall centre is the bill for the heavy tails. It is not what the number measures.

The "peakedness" myth. Many textbooks describe kurtosis as "how peaked" a distribution is. The bound in section 3 shows why that is wrong: the region within one standard deviation can contribute at most 1 to $\kappa$. For a direct counterexample, look at the table in section 4. The triangular distribution has a sharp point at its centre and is taller there than the normal (0.408 vs 0.399), yet its kurtosis is lower (2.4 vs 3), because its tails stop dead at $\pm\sqrt6 \approx 2.45$. Conversely, a distribution can have a flat, rounded centre and still have enormous kurtosis if it has a few far-away values. Read kurtosis as tail weight, or as "propensity to produce outliers".

8. Where High Kurtosis Comes From: Calm Most Days, Wild Some Days

Go back to the second chip machine. Most of the time it runs with a small spread; occasionally (a jam, a sensor glitch) it runs with a much larger spread. That is a mixture: with probability $1 - p$ a value comes from a calm normal with standard deviation $a$, and with probability $p$ from a wild normal with standard deviation $c \cdot a$. Daily stock returns behave the same way: quiet days punctuated by turbulent ones.

Kurtosis of a Scale Mixture of Normals

If $X$ is normal with mean 0 given a random standard deviation $S$, then

$$\begin{aligned} E[X^2] &= E[S^2] \\[6pt] E[X^4] &= 3\,E[S^4] \\[8pt] \kappa &= \frac{3\,E[S^4]}{\big(E[S^2]\big)^2} \;\ge\; 3 \end{aligned}$$

The inequality is $E[S^4] \ge (E[S^2])^2$, i.e. $\operatorname{Var}(S^2) \ge 0$, with equality only when $S$ never changes. Any randomness in the spread pushes the kurtosis above the normal's 3.

For the two-component mixture above, $E[S^2] = a^2(1 - p + pc^2)$ and $E[S^4] = a^4(1 - p + pc^4)$, so

$$\kappa = \frac{3\,(1 - p + p\,c^4)}{(1 - p + p\,c^2)^2}$$

With a 10% chance of a day three times as volatile ($p = 0.1$, $c = 3$):

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa &= \frac{3\,(0.9 + 0.1 \times 81)}{(0.9 + 0.1 \times 9)^2} \\[6pt] &= \frac{3 \times 9}{3.24} \\[6pt] &\approx 8.33 \end{aligned}$$

9. Kurtosis from Data

With data $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and sample mean $\bar{x}$, replace the expectations with averages:

$$\begin{aligned} m_2 &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2 \\[6pt] m_4 &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^4 \\[8pt] \hat\kappa &= \frac{m_4}{m_2^2} \end{aligned}$$

Two practical warnings:

  • Software usually reports excess kurtosis. Excel's =KURT() and most statistics packages return $\hat\kappa - 3$, often with a small-sample correction. A value near 0 means "normal-like tails", not "no kurtosis".
  • It is a noisy estimate. Because $z^4$ is dominated by the few most extreme points, a single outlier can move $\hat\kappa$ a lot, and heavy-tailed data can need thousands of observations before the estimate settles. Treat a sample kurtosis from 20 points with suspicion.

Why it matters

  • Risk. A model that assumes normal returns uses $P(|Z| > 4) \approx 0.00006$. If the real returns look like a Laplace, the true figure is about 55 times larger. That gap is how "once in a thousand years" losses happen every few years.
  • Outliers. High kurtosis is a warning that the data will produce extreme values, so a mean or a standard deviation computed from it may be unstable.
  • Tests and intervals. Confidence intervals and tests for a variance (the $\chi^2$ interval, the F-test) assume normal data and break down badly when kurtosis is high, much more than tests for a mean do.

$X$ has kurtosis 6. What is the kurtosis of $Y = 5 - 2X$?

Check your work

Still 6. The z-score of $Y$ is $\frac{Y - E[Y]}{\operatorname{SD}(Y)} = \frac{-2(X - \mu)}{2\sigma} = -Z$, and $(-Z)^4 = Z^4$.

Problem 3: Rare Events

Practice

A Bernoulli variable with success probability $p$ has kurtosis $\kappa = \dfrac{1 - 3p(1-p)}{p(1-p)}$. Evaluate it for $p = 0.5$ and $p = 0.1$, and explain the difference.

Check your work $$\begin{aligned} p = 0.5:\quad \kappa &= \frac{1 - 0.75}{0.25} = 1 \\[8pt] p = 0.1:\quad \kappa &= \frac{1 - 0.27}{0.09} \approx 8.1 \end{aligned}$$

A fair coin has no tail: both outcomes sit one standard deviation from the mean, the minimum possible kurtosis. With $p = 0.1$, the rare success sits $\frac{0.9}{0.3} = 3$ standard deviations above the mean, so it is an outlier, and the kurtosis is high. Rare events and high kurtosis go together.

Problem 4: A Rarer, Wilder Machine

Practice

A machine is wild 5% of the time, with a spread 4 times its normal spread ($p = 0.05$, $c = 4$). Find its kurtosis and compare it with $p = 0.1$, $c = 3$ (κ ≈ 8.33). Check your answer with Figure 4.

Rarer but more extreme bad days give a higher kurtosis: the fourth power rewards "how far" much more than "how often".

Connecting the Dots

The four moments form a ladder. The mean ($E[X]$) fixes location. The variance ($E[Z^2]$ before standardising) fixes scale. Once those two are removed by turning values into z-scores, the shape is what remains: the third moment, skewness, asks whether the far-out values lean left or right, and the fourth, kurtosis, asks how much far-out mass there is at all. Because the fourth power shrinks everything within one standard deviation and magnifies everything beyond it, kurtosis is in effect a tail detector, and its familiar "tall, narrow" look is only the price a fixed variance charges for heavy tails. The same idea links back to the course: in the MFDS Midsem Guide kurtosis appears as one of the four moments; here we have seen why it gets its own number, and why a model built on normal tails can badly underrate the chance of an extreme day.
