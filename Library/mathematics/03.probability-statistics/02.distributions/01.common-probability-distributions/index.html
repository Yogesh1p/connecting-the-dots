If a distribution depends on parameters $\theta$, we write:

$$ X \sim \mathcal{D}(\theta) $$

This means $X$ follows the distribution $\mathcal{D}$ with parameter(s) $\theta$. The symbol " $\sim$ " is read as "is distributed as" or "follows".

Each distribution below also comes with a story: a concrete experiment that produces it. The stories follow the approach of Blitzstein & Hwang, Introduction to Probability. A story is often more useful than the formula. If a word problem matches the story, you know the distribution, and you get its PMF, mean and variance without deriving anything.

1. $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$

Story: Bernoulli

Perform one experiment that ends in either success, with probability $p$, or failure, with probability $1-p$. Let $X = 1$ on success and $X = 0$ on failure. Then $X \sim \text{Bern}(p)$.

Every event has a Bernoulli hidden inside it: the indicator $I_A$, which equals 1 if $A$ happens and 0 if not, is $\text{Bern}(P(A))$. So $E[I_A] = P(A)$, and that one fact turns many "expected number of…" questions into adding up probabilities.

(Bernoulli trial). An experiment that can result in either a "success" or a "failure" (but not both). A Bernoulli random variable can be thought of as the indicator of success: it equals 1 if success occurs and 0 if failure occurs. It is a discrete random variable that can take on only two possible values, 0 and 1.

PMF (Probability Mass Function) $$ P(X=x) = \begin{cases} p, & x=1 \\ 1-p, & x=0 \end{cases} $$ Compact: $P(X=x) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x}$
CDF (Cumulative Distribution) $$ F(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \lt 0 \\ 1-p, & 0 \le x \lt 1 \\ 1, & x \ge 1 \end{cases} $$
Mean & Variance $E[X] = p$
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1-p)$
Median & Mode $\text{Mode/Median} = \begin{cases} 0, & p \lt \tfrac{1}{2} \\ \{0,1\}, & p = \tfrac{1}{2} \\ 1, & p \gt \tfrac{1}{2} \end{cases}$

Equivalencies: $\text{Bernoulli}(p) \equiv \text{Binomial}(n=1,p)$

2. $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p)$

Story: Binomial

Perform $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, each with the same success probability $p$, and count the successes. That count is $\text{Bin}(n, p)$. The same thing in urn language: an urn has a fraction $p$ of white balls, you draw $n$ balls with replacement (so the urn never changes), and $X$ counts the white ones.

Why $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$: any one particular arrangement of $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ by independence, and there are $\binom{n}{k}$ ways to choose which trials are the successes. Writing $X = I_1 + \dots + I_n$ as a sum of indicators gives $E[X] = np$ at once.

Examples: heads in $n$ coin tosses, defective parts in a batch of $n$ from a stable process, purchases from $n$ demo invites.

Suppose that $n$ independent Bernoulli trials are performed, each with the same success probability $p$. Let $X$ be the number of successes. We write $X \sim \text{Bin}(n,p)$, where $n$ is a positive integer and $0 < p < 1$.

PMF $$ P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k} $$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n$
CDF (No closed-form) $$ F(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor x\rfloor}\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k} $$
Mean & Variance $E[X] = np$
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1-p)$
Median & Mode $m \in \{\lfloor np \rfloor, \lceil np \rceil\}$
$\text{Mode} = \begin{cases} \lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor, & \text{if } (n+1)p \notin \mathbb{Z} \\ \{(n+1)p - 1, \, (n+1)p\}, & \text{if } (n+1)p \in \mathbb{Z} \end{cases}$

3. $X \sim \text{HGeom}(w, b, n)$

Story: Hypergeometric

An urn holds $w$ white balls and $b$ black balls. Draw $n$ of them without replacement, so that every set of $n$ balls is equally likely. Let $X$ be the number of white balls in your sample. Then $X \sim \text{HGeom}(w, b, n)$.

It is the binomial story with one change. Drawing with replacement keeps the urn the same every time, so the draws are independent and $X \sim \text{Bin}\big(n, \tfrac{w}{w+b}\big)$. Drawing without replacement changes the urn after each draw: taking a white ball makes the next white ball less likely. The draws are dependent, so the binomial formula no longer applies, and we count instead.

Counting the PMF. Think of the $n$ draws as one handful. There are $\binom{w+b}{n}$ equally likely handfuls. A handful with exactly $k$ white balls is built by choosing which $k$ of the $w$ white balls you got, and which $n-k$ of the $b$ black balls fill the rest.

PMF $$ P(X=k)=\frac{\binom{w}{k}\binom{b}{n-k}}{\binom{w+b}{n}} $$ for integers $k$ with $0 \le k \le w$ and $0 \le n-k \le b$; otherwise $0$
CDF (No closed-form) $$ F(x)=\sum_{k \le x}\frac{\binom{w}{k}\binom{b}{n-k}}{\binom{w+b}{n}} $$
Mean & Variance With $N = w+b$ and $p = \frac{w}{N}$:
$E[X] = np$
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = np(1-p)\,\frac{N-n}{N-1}$
Compared with the Binomial Same mean. The variance is smaller by the finite population correction $\frac{N-n}{N-1}$, because sampling without replacement cannot produce the extreme runs that independent draws can. When $N \gg n$ the correction is close to 1 and $\text{HGeom} \approx \text{Bin}(n, p)$.

The same story in other clothes. "White" is whatever you are counting, "black" is everything else:

  • Cards: the number of aces in a 5-card hand is $\text{HGeom}(4, 48, 5)$.
  • Quality inspection: a lot of 50 items has 3 defectives; inspecting 10 without replacement, the defectives found are $\text{HGeom}(3, 47, 10)$.
  • Capture–recapture: a forest has $N$ elk. Catch $m$, tag them and release them. Later catch $n$ elk. The number of tagged elk in the second catch is $\text{HGeom}(m, N-m, n)$, which is how ecologists estimate $N$ from the tagged fraction.

4. Uniform Distributions

Story: Uniform

Discrete: write each element of a finite set $C$ on a slip of paper, put the slips in a hat, shake it, and draw one. The value drawn is $\text{DUnif}(C)$, and $P(X \in A) = \frac{|A|}{|C|}$: probability is counting.

Continuous: pick a point "completely at random" from the interval $(a, b)$, meaning the chance of landing in any sub-interval depends only on its length: $P(c \le X \le d) = \frac{d-c}{b-a}$. Probability is length. Its special role: from a single $\text{Unif}(0,1)$ draw you can build a draw from any continuous distribution by plugging it into the inverse CDF, which is how computers simulate random numbers.

Discrete Uniform ($X \sim \text{DUnif}(C)$): Choose one number uniformly at random from a finite, nonempty set $C$. All values are equally likely.

Continuous Uniform ($X \sim \text{Uniform}(a,b)$): A random variable $X$ has equal probability density over every point in the continuous interval $[a, b]$.

PDF (Continuous) $$ f(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{b-a}, & a \le x \le b \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
CDF (Continuous) $$ F(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x \lt a \\ \frac{x-a}{b-a}, & a \le x \le b \\ 1, & x \gt b \end{cases} $$
Mean & Variance $E[X] = \frac{a+b}{2}$
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}$
Median & Mode $\frac{a+b}{2}$
Every point is equally likely (No unique mode).

5. $X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)$

Story: Exponential

You are waiting for the first arrival (a customer, a phone call, a failure) when arrivals happen at a constant average rate $\lambda$ per unit time, and the process has no memory. The waiting time is $\text{Expo}(\lambda)$.

It is the continuous version of the geometric: chop time into tiny slots of length $\Delta$, and let each slot independently contain an arrival with probability $\lambda\Delta$. The number of slots until the first arrival is geometric, and as $\Delta \to 0$ the waiting time becomes exponential. That is why it inherits memorylessness: a slot never knows how many empty slots came before it.

A continuous distribution used for modeling waiting times between independent events that occur at a constant average rate $\lambda \gt 0$. It is uniquely characterized by its memoryless property.

PDF $$ f(x) = \begin{cases} \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, & x \ge 0 \\ 0, & x \lt 0 \end{cases} $$
CDF $$ F(x)= \begin{cases} 1 - e^{-\lambda x}, & x \ge 0 \\ 0, & x \lt 0 \end{cases} $$
Mean & Variance $E[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda}$
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$
Median & Mode $\frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$
$0$
Survival Function: $S(x)=P(X \gt x)= e^{-\lambda x}$

Memoryless Property: $P(X \gt s + t \mid X \gt t) = P(X \gt s)$

Relationship with Poisson Process: If events occur according to a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, the waiting time between events is $\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$.

6. $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$

Story: Poisson

There are a very large number of opportunities for something to happen, each one rare and roughly independent of the others, and $X$ counts how many happen: emails in an hour, typos on a page, earthquakes in a year. If there are $n$ opportunities each with probability $p$, the count is approximately $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ with $\lambda = np$, the expected number. This is the Poisson paradigm.

In a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, the number of arrivals in a window of length $t$ is $\text{Pois}(\lambda t)$, and the gaps between arrivals are $\text{Expo}(\lambda)$: the count and the waiting time are two views of the same process.

A discrete distribution used to model the number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space, when events happen independently and with a constant average rate $\lambda \gt 0$.

PMF $$ P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} $$ for $k = 0,1,2,\dots$
CDF $$ F(k) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^i}{i!} $$
Mean & Variance $E[X] = \lambda$
$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda$
Mode $\lfloor \lambda \rfloor$ (if $\lambda \notin \mathbb{Z}$)
  • Additivity: If $X_1 \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)$ and $X_2 \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)$ are independent, $X_1 + X_2 \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$.
  • Approximation to Binomial: If $n$ is large, $p$ is small, and $\lambda = np$, then $\text{Binomial}(n,p) \approx \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$. Useful for rare-event modeling.

7 & 8. The Normal Distributions

Story: Normal

Add up many small, independent contributions, none of which dominates. Whatever the individual pieces look like, the total is approximately normal: this is the Central Limit Theorem. Heights (many genes and environmental factors), measurement error (many small disturbances) and averages of large samples all follow this story.

Every normal is a stretched and shifted standard normal: if $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ then $X = \mu + \sigma Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. That is why one $z$-table is enough for all of them.

A continuous, symmetric, bell-shaped distribution widely used due to the Central Limit Theorem. $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ where $\mu$ is the mean and $\sigma^2$ is the variance.

Standard Normal: $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ If $Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$ then $Z$ is standard normal. $$ \phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2} $$
General Normal PDF $$ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) $$
68–95–99.7 Rule (Empirical Rule):
68% of observations lie in $\mu \pm \sigma$
95% lie in $\mu \pm 2\sigma$
99.7% lie in $\mu \pm 3\sigma$

9. T-distribution & 10. Chi-squared

Story: Student t

Divide a standard normal by an independent, estimated scale: $T = \dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{V/n}}$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ independent of $V \sim \chi^2_n$. Then $T \sim t_n$. It appears whenever you standardise a sample mean with the sample standard deviation instead of the true one: $\dfrac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{n-1}$ for normal data. The randomness in $S$ is what fattens the tails.

T-distribution: Used instead of normal when the sample size is small and variance is unknown. It has heavier tails to account for extra uncertainty.

Story: Chi-squared

Take $n$ independent standard normals, square each one, and add: $V = Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_n^2 \sim \chi^2_n$. It is a sum of squared errors, which is why it governs variances: for normal data, $\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$. It is never negative and is skewed to the right, with mean $n$.

Chi-squared distribution: Models the variability of squared normal deviations. Used to study variance, goodness of fit, and independence.

Geometric & First Success Distributions

Story: Geometric and First Success

Perform independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $p$, one after another, until the first success. The number of failures before the first success is $\text{Geom}(p)$; the number of trials including the success is $\text{FS}(p)$. Examples: sales calls until the first sale, rolls until the first 6.

The PMF $q^k p$ reads straight off the story: $k$ failures in a row (probability $q^k$), then one success ($p$). The means are $E[\text{Geom}] = \frac{q}{p}$ and $E[\text{FS}] = \frac{1}{p}$. Like the exponential, it is memoryless: a run of failures does not make success any more "due".

Geometric ($X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$): The number of failures before the first successful trial. PMF is $P(X=k) = q^k p$ for $k=0,1,2,\dots$, where $q = 1-p$.

First Success ($Y \sim \text{FS}(p)$): The number of trials until the first successful trial, including the success. If $X \sim \text{Geom}(p)$, then $X + 1 \sim \text{FS}(p)$.
